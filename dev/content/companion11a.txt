1 One valley, five walkers
To train a model we lower a loss. The loss is one number. It says how wrong the model is right
now. We want it small. Every method in this session does the same thing. It takes a small step
downhill, again and again. The methods differ in only one way: how they pick each step.
We use one tiny example all session. It has a model with just two numbers to learn. We call them
w1 and w2 . These learned numbers are called weights. We keep both in one list, w = [ w1 , w2 ].
A list of numbers like this is a vector. We write a vector in bold.
The loss for our toy model is
                                          J(w) = 21 w12 + 5 w22 .
This shape is a valley. It is gentle along w1 and steep along w2 . The best point is w = [0, 0],
where J = 0. We start at w(0) = [1, 1]. There the loss is J = 21 (1) + 5(1) = 5.5.

      ☞ The intuition
   Every method answers one small question. “I can feel the slope under my feet. How big
   a step do I take, and which way?” Gradient descent uses a fixed step. Momentum adds
   memory. AdaGrad gives each weight its own step, built from its past. RMSProp does that
   but forgets old history. Adam mixes memory and per-weight steps, then fixes a start-up
   glitch. That is the whole session in five lines.

      ⋆ Everyday picture
   Picture a wide bowl that is not round. It is squashed. It is steep from front to back, and
   gentle from side to side. Drop a ball in. The steep way and the gentle way need different
   care. One single step size cannot suit both at once. That mismatch is the whole problem
   this session solves.


The gradient: a list of slopes
The slope is not one number. We have two weights, so we have two slopes. We stack them in a
list. This list of slopes is the gradient. We write it ∇J. The symbol ∇ is read “nabla.” For our
loss the gradient is
                                       ∇J(w) = [ w1 , 10 w2 ].
Here is where each part comes from. The slope of 12 w12 in the w1 direction is w1 . The slope of
5 w22 in the w2 direction is 10 w2 . At the start w = [1, 1], so ∇J = [1, 10]. The steep weight has
a slope ten times bigger than the gentle one.

Four moves on vectors
Every method below works on the whole vector at once. We reuse four simple moves. Each one
works one entry at a time.

• Scale. α ∇J multiplies every entry by α. So 0.1 · [1, 10] = [0.1, 1].

• Subtract. w − s subtracts matching entries. So [1, 1] − [0.1, 1] = [0.9, 0].

• Multiply entry by entry (⊙). ∇J ⊙ ∇J = [ g12 , g22 ]. So [1, 10] ⊙ [1, 10] = [1, 100]. Read
  ⊙ as “times, entry by entry.”

• Root and divide entry by entry.              [1, 100] = [1, 10], and [1, 10] [1, 10] = [1, 1].

One rule ties these together. When two vectors meet with ⊙, a square root, or a fraction bar,
you work one entry at a time.
Fixed settings for the whole session. We start at w(0) = [1, 1]. We use the same step
size α = 0.1 for all five methods, so the race is fair. The step size says how much of the slope
becomes a real step. One iteration means one update of the whole vector. We write w(t) for
the weights after t iterations.

      ✓ Key takeaway
   One loss, J = 12 w12 + 5w22 . One start, [1, 1]. One step size, 0.1. Five methods now walk
   this valley. Every difference you see comes from the update rule alone.

2 Gradient descent: the plain walker
You are in thick fog on a hillside. You cannot see the bottom. You can only feel the slope under
your feet. So you take a small step straight downhill. Then you do it again. That is the whole
method.

      ☞ The intuition
   The gradient points uphill. So step the other way. Multiply the slope by the step size α,
   and subtract it. Big slope, big step. Small slope, small step. Nothing is remembered. Each
   step starts with a blank mind.


The rule, on the whole vector at once, is

                                 w(t) = w(t−1) − α ∇J w(t−1) .
                                                                   


Name the pieces. w is the weight vector. ∇J is the slope vector. The step size α = 0.1 sets how
much slope becomes step.

      ✎ Worked example: two steps of gradient descent, α = 0.1

   Start at w(0) = [1, 1], where J = 5.5.
   Step 1. Feel the slopes. ∇J([1, 1]) = [1, 10].
   Step 2. Take the step. α ∇J = 0.1 · [1, 10] = [0.1, 1]. So

                                      w(1) = [1, 1] − [0.1, 1] = [ 0.9, 0 ].

              The steep weight w2 went from 1 to 0 in one step. It landed right on the floor.
              That is because 0.1 × 10 × 1 = 1, exactly the distance it had to cover.
   Step 3. Loss check. J([0.9, 0]) = 21 (0.81) + 5(0) = 0.405. Down from 5.5.
   Step 4. Feel the slopes again. ∇J([0.9, 0]) = [0.9, 0].
   Step 5. Take the step. α ∇J = [0.09, 0], so

                    w(2) = [0.9, 0] − [0.09, 0] = [ 0.81, 0 ],     J = 12 (0.812 ) = 0.3281.

   Step 6. Sense-check. w2 stays at 0; its slope there is 0, so it does not move. w1 shrinks
           by a tenth each step: 1 → 0.9 → 0.81. Slow, but steady.


Here α = 0.1 is a lucky fit for the steep weight w2 . It lands on the floor in one step. But the
same α is too small for the gentle weight w1 . That weight only shrinks by a tenth each step. It
will need many steps to reach 0. If we raise α to hurry w1 along, then w2 starts to overshoot
and bounce. One step size cannot be right for both directions at once. That single fact is why
the next four methods exist.

      ✗ Watch out
   A common thought is “just lower α, then.” Lowering α calms the steep weight. But it
   makes the gentle weight even slower. On this valley no single α is good for both. Raise it
   and w2 bounces. Lower it and w1 crawls.

Where this shows up in ML. Backpropagation is just the fast way to compute ∇J across
the layers of a network. The update on top is exactly this rule. Swap the full dataset for a small

   Gradient descent: w ← w − α ∇J. One shared step size must serve every direction at once.

3 Momentum: give the walker a memory
Stop walking. Roll a ball instead. A rolling ball does not stop at every tiny dip. Its built-up
motion carries it through. And once it is rolling one way, a single odd bump barely changes its
path.

      ⋆ Everyday picture
   Picture a heavy marble rolling down a bumpy hill. Small dips cannot trap it. Its own
   motion carries it across them. But there is a catch. A fast marble can roll past the lowest
   point and has to swing back. Keep both halves of this picture. Both show up in the
   numbers.

The idea is simple. Each step is the fresh downhill pull, plus a faded copy of the last move. The
last move is the memory. Writing w for the weights and β for the memory dial, the rule is

                             w(t) = w(t−1) − α ∇J w(t−1) + v(t) ,                                                       (1)
                                                                         

                             v   (t)
                                       =β w   (t−1)
                                                      −w   (t−2) 
                                                                     ,       v   (1)
                                                                                       = [0, 0].                        (2)

Name the pieces. v(t) is the velocity vector. It is β times the last move, so it is the memory.
The dial β (the Greek letter beta) is the friction parameter. It sets how much of the last move
survives. At β = 0 this is plain gradient descent. We use β = 0.9. The piece (w(t−1) − w(t−2) ) is
simply the last move.

      ✎ Worked example: two steps of momentum, α = 0.1, β = 0.9

   Start at w(0) = [1, 1].
   Step 1. No memory yet. At step 1 the velocity is [0, 0]. So step 1 is just gradient
           descent: w(1) = [ 0.9, 0 ], and J = 0.405.
   Step 2. Build the memory. The last move was w(1) −w(0) = [0.9, 0]−[1, 1] = [−0.1, −1].
           So the velocity is

                                        v(2) = 0.9 · [−0.1, −1] = [ −0.09, −0.9 ].

   Step 3. Fresh downhill pull. ∇J([0.9, 0]) = [0.9, 0], so −α ∇J = [−0.09, 0].
   Step 4. Add them up.

                         w(2) = [0.9, 0] + [−0.09, 0] + [−0.09, −0.9] = [ 0.72, −0.9 ].

   Step 5. Read the w2 line. At w2 = 0 the fresh slope is 0. It says “stop.” But the
           memory says “you were going down fast, keep going,” which is −0.9. So w2
           shoots past the floor, down to −0.9. That is the overshoot.
   Step 6. Read the w1 line. The pull moved it 0.09. The memory added 0.09 more. In
           the steady direction the moves stack up and speed up.
   Step 7. Loss check. J([0.72, −0.9]) = 12 (0.722 ) + 5(0.92 ) = 0.2592 + 4.05 = 4.3092.
           Higher than gradient descent’s 0.3281 at the same point in time. This step went
           on killing the bounce and building speed, not on cutting the loss. Keep going
           and it pays that back.

      ✗ Watch out
   Momentum is not a strict “always downhill” method. The loss can rise for a step while
   speed builds. It did here: 4.31, up from the earlier 0.405. Do not read one rising step as a
   bug. Also never set β ≥ 1. Friction is what drains the extra bouncing energy. With none,
   the marble swings forever, or blows up.


Where this shows up in ML. SGD with momentum, at β about 0.9, is still the standard
choice for training vision networks. The same fading-memory trick, used on squared slopes
instead of slopes, gives us RMSProp next.

      ✓ Key takeaway
   Momentum: new move = fresh downhill step + β× last move. Directions that agree across
   steps stack up and speed up. Directions that flip cancel out. The gentle weight speeds
   along; the steep weight stops bouncing, after one overshoot.

4 AdaGrad: a step size for each weight
Momentum kept one shared step size and added memory. AdaGrad goes at the real problem
head-on. It stops sharing. It gives each weight its own step size, built from that weight’s own
past.

      ☞ The intuition
   Each weight keeps a running total of the squares of every slope it has√ ever felt. Call that
   total A for that weight. Before stepping, divide the weight’s step by A. A weight with a
   loud past, with big past slopes, gets its steps shrunk. A weight with a quiet past keeps big
   steps. Steep and gentle even out on their own.


      ⋆ Everyday picture
   Give every weight its own odometer. It does not record where the weight went. It records
   how much slope the weight has felt, added up as squares. Loud-history weights get gentle
   taps from now on. Quiet-history weights still get full pushes. The catch: these odometers
   never reset. A weight that was loud early is treated as loud forever.


The rule keeps A, a running total of squared slopes, one per weight:

never shows below.

5 RMSProp: AdaGrad that forgets
AdaGrad has an elephant’s memory. A slope from a thousand steps ago still brakes today’s
move. RMSProp keeps the per-weight idea and adds one word: forget. It blends the history
with a fading average, instead of a total that grows forever.

      ⋆ Everyday picture
   A selector does not judge a player on their whole-career average. They look at recent form:
   the last ten games, with the latest counting most. RMSProp scores each weight’s slope
   history the same way. Recent slopes count fully. Old ones fade away. The catch: after
   only one game, “recent form” is a wild guess. That small-sample wobble is exactly what
   Adam will fix.

The change touches just one line of AdaGrad. Instead of adding squared slopes forever, keep a
fading average. Keep a share ρ of the old value, and add (1 − ρ) of the new one:
                                 A ← ρ A + (1 − ρ) ∇J ⊙ ∇J,                                 (5)
                                            α ∇J
                                 w←w− √ .                                                   (6)
                                               A
The dial ρ (the Greek letter rho) is the forgetting dial. It sits between 0 and 1. We use
ρ = 0.9. A slope from t steps ago fades as ρ t . With ρ = 0.9, a slope ten steps old has faded
to about 0.910 ≈ 0.35 of its weight. As a rule of thumb, the average remembers about the last
1/(1 − ρ) = 10 steps.

   right after the zero start.

6 Adam: memory, per-weight steps, and a fair start
You have now met the two working ideas. Remember your direction, which is momentum.
Rescale by recent slope size, which is RMSProp. Adam keeps both, in one method. It also fixes
the zero-start over-step you just watched puff up RMSProp’s first move.

      ☞ The intuition
   Each weight carries two memories. F is the smoothed slope: which way, on average, has
   the weight been pushed? A is the smoothed squared slope: how loud has the pushing
   been? The step is the direction memory divided by the root of the loudness memory, times
   a rate αt . Early on both memories are still tiny. So αt shrinks the step on purpose, while
   the memories fill up.


      ⋆ Everyday picture
   A new assistant keeps two notebooks about a project. One tracks which way the decisions
   trend. The other tracks how big they run. After two days both notebooks are nearly empty.
   A careful boss discounts what they seem to say so soon. After a year the notebooks are
   full and can be read at face value. Adam’s rate αt is exactly that discount. It is strong
   early and gone later.


The recipe keeps two memories per weight and a rate that counts the step number:
                              F ← ρf F + (1 − ρf ) ∇J,                                                      (7)
                              A ← ρ A + (1 − ρ) ∇J ⊙ ∇J,                                                    (8)

Both memories start at zero. The dial ρf = 0.9 fades the slope memory F. The dial ρ = 0.999
fades the squared memory A. The rate αt is the fair-start rate. It counts the step number t
and undoes the too-small zero start.
Where αt comes from. After t steps a zero-started average has only filled to a share 1 − ρft
(or 1 − ρ t ) of full. So the honest, corrected memories are F/(1 − ρft ) and A/(1 − ρ t ). Put those
into an RMSProp-style step and tidy up. The leftover factor is exactly αt . Only A sits under a
root, so only its correction picks up a square root.

           and no early fade, as AdaGrad had. That is just what the design promised.

      ✗ Watch out
  Start counting at t = 1, not 0. At t = 0 the bottom of αt is 1 − ρ0f = 0, and the formula
  blows up. That is a classic bug. Names also differ across libraries. Most call the pair
  (ρf , ρ) by the names (β1 , β2 ), with the same defaults. And Adam still carries momentum,
  so it can roll past the bottom and swing back. The race figure below shows exactly that.

for transformers and large language models. At that scale, slope sizes differ across layers by
huge amounts, and the slopes are noisy. Nobody hand-tunes a rate for each layer. A self-scaling,

      ✗ Watch out
   Do not crown a winner from this one race. Our valley is tiny, clean, and free of noise. It
   is only 10-to-1 steep-to-gentle, and we picked α to suit it. Real training has millions of
   weights, steepness ratios in the thousands, and noisy slope readings. There, plain gradient
   descent’s safe step-size window slams shut. The adaptive methods keep working with far
   less tuning. The lesson of the demo is the mechanisms, not the finishing order.
