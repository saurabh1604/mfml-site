The whole lecture in one story
You are standing somewhere on a hillside in thick fog. You cannot see the valley. All you can feel
is the slope under your feet. So you take a small step downhill, feel again, step again. That is
gradient descent, and that is essentially how every model in this course learns.
    Four things can go wrong on that hillside, and this entire lecture is about those four things:

1. You get dropped in a bad place. Where you start matters.                                (Initialisation)
2. You reach the bottom of the wrong valley, or you cross a huge flat plateau where the ground
   feels level and you crawl.                                       (Local optima and flat regions)
3. The valley is not a bowl, it is a canyon. Steep across, gentle along. You bounce off the walls
   and barely move forward.                                                 (Differential curvature)
4. You reach the bottom, and the bottom is a lie. You have fitted the training data perfectly and
   learnt nothing true.                                                              (Overfitting)

    Everything else in the slides — mean-centering, normalisation, contour plots, second deriva-
tives — is machinery for handling those four problems.
    The one line to remember
    Minimising the loss is not the goal. Landing in a place that also behaves well on data you
    have never seen is the goal. Gradient descent is just the vehicle, and it has a few well-known
    handling problems.

1. The loss function: a scorecard for wrongness
A model makes a guess. The truth is known for the training data. The loss function is simply the
bill for being wrong.
     The slides use the least-squares loss
                                          n
                              J ( w ) = ∑ w T Xi − yi
                                                                2
                                                                    ,   ŷi = w T Xi .
                                         i =1

    Read it right to left, in words:

• Xi is one training example — one row of your table, one customer, one patient, one house.
• w T Xi is the model’s prediction: multiply each feature by its importance and add up.
• w T Xi − yi is the mistake on that one row.
• Square it, so that being −5 off is punished exactly as much as being +5 off, and so that big
  mistakes hurt disproportionately more than small ones.
• Sum over all n rows: total wrongness.

    In plain words

    J (w) is a machine that eats a set of weights and spits out a single number: “how bad”.
    Training means turning the weight knobs until that number is small. Nothing more mystical
    than that.

2. Where do you start? (Initialisation)
Gradient descent needs a first guess w0 . The slides say: usually pick each component randomly
from [−1, +1], or from [0, 1] if the parameters must stay non-negative.
   Why such small, boring numbers?

• Because arithmetic breaks. Suppose a feature has value x = 200 and you initialise w = 5000.
  Then ŷ = 106 while the truth is maybe 7. The squared error is about 1012 , the gradient is about
  109 , and the very first update throws w to some absurd value. Two more steps and you have
  inf or NaN. This is the overflow the slide warns about. Underflow is the mirror image: start
  with w = 10−12 inside a product of many such terms and everything rounds to zero, so no
  learning signal survives.
• Because the parameters talk to each other. If two weights are correlated — moving one is only
  sensible together with the other — then a careless start can drop you on the far side of a ridge,
  and you will descend into the wrong valley (Section 5).
• Because symmetry is fatal in networks. Initialise every weight in a layer to the same value
  and every neuron receives the same gradient forever; they stay identical clones and the layer
  behaves like a single neuron. Randomness is what breaks the tie.

  In plain words

  Starting values are a parachute drop. Drop yourself somewhere small, random and central;
  do not drop yourself off the edge of the map, and do not drop the whole team on the exact
  same square metre.

3. Overfitting: getting full marks by memorising
The situation
Four inputs x1 , x2 , x3 , x4 , one output y, and a model with five knobs:

                                ŷ = w1 x1 + w2 x2 + w3 x3 + w4 x4 + w5 .

The slide’s key observation: with fewer than five training rows you are solving fewer equations than
you have unknowns. Such a system has infinitely many exact solutions, so you can always drive
the training error to exactly zero. That sounds like a triumph. It is usually a disaster.
   In plain words

   Five unknowns, three equations. It is like being told “two numbers add to 10” and being
   asked what they are: (5, 5), (0, 10), (−3, 13) all work. The data has not pinned the answer
   down; your optimiser will just hand you one of the infinitely many, and it has no idea which
   one is meaningful.


The slide’s data

                                           x1   x2     x3     x4      y
                                           61   2       3     0.1     59
                                           40   0       4     0.5     40
                                           68   0      10     1.0     70

   We are told the real-world truth is y ≈ x1 . Look at the column: 61 → 59, 40 → 40, 68 → 70. Yes
— y is basically x1 with a little noise on top.

Candidate A: the honest model
Take w1 = 1 and w2 = w3 = w4 = w5 = 0, i.e. ŷ = x1 .


                      row 1:    ŷ = 61,    y = 59,         error = +2,        squared = 4

                      row 3:    ŷ = 68,    y = 70,         error = −2,        squared = 4

                                            J = 4 + 0 + 4 = 8.
Not zero. Close to zero, but not zero.

   The one line to remember
   More knobs than facts ⇒ zero training error is available ⇒ zero training error means nothing.
   Judge a model on data it has not seen.

   In plain words

   This is also the intuition behind regularisation, which you will meet later: add a penalty like
   λ ∑ j w2j to the loss. Candidate B has weights 12 and 5 and 20; Candidate A has a single weight
   of 1. The penalty makes B expensive and A cheap, so the optimiser stops preferring the
   memoriser.


4. Feature processing: why units wreck learning
The problem, stated with the slides’ example
Wealth y is modelled from age x1 ∈ [0, 100] and years of college x2 ∈ [0, 10]:

                                           y = w1 x12 + w2 x22 .

The sensitivity of the output to each knob is

                                        ∂y                ∂y
                                           = x12 ,           = x22 .
                                       ∂w1               ∂w2
Put in typical values, say x1 = 50 and x2 = 4:

                                       ∂y                  ∂y
                                          = 2500,             = 16.
                                      ∂w1                 ∂w2
The same nudge to w1 changes the answer roughly 156 times more than the same nudge to w2 .
   In plain words

  Two knobs on the same machine. One is a hair-trigger; a millimetre of turn swings the output
  wildly. The other is stiff; you can spin it and almost nothing happens. Gradient descent
  uses one learning rate η for both knobs. Any η that is gentle enough not to over-turn the
  hair-trigger is far too gentle to move the stiff one. You bounce in one direction and crawl in
  the other.

   Careful
   Slide 9 ends with “. . . we take small steps in respect of w2 and large steps in respect of w1 ” and
   slide 10 continues from there. Note the mechanism carefully: the step size η is the same for
   both; it is the gradient that is huge for w1 and tiny for w2 , so the actual movement η × gradient is
   large in w1 and small in w2 . Large movements in w1 overshoot and oscillate; small movements
   in w2 creep. That combination is what makes convergence painfully slow.

Why high dimensions make this catastrophic
The slides give a lovely counting argument. Take a loss that splits into independent one-variable
pieces:
                        F ( x1 , x2 , . . . , x d ) = A1 ( x1 ) + A2 ( x2 ) + · · · + A d ( x d ).
The partial derivative with respect to xi only sees Ai , because every other term is a constant as far
as xi is concerned:
                                              ∂F
                                                  = Ai′ ( xi ).
                                              ∂xi
So ∇ F = 0 exactly when every Ai′ ( xi∗ ) = 0 simultaneously. If Ai has k i local minima, you may pick
any one of them independently in each coordinate, and the total count of local minima of F is the
product
                                                    d
                                                  ∏ ki .
                                                  i =1

   In plain words

   It is a combination lock. Each dial can settle in one of k notches; the lock has kd settings in total,
   and only one of them opens the door. With a modest d = 10 and k = 3 that is 310 = 59,049
   local minima. With d = 50 it is about 7 × 1023 . Searching them is hopeless.


Flat regions
Look again at the shaded band in the figure, and at slide 15’s picture. Over a long stretch the curve
is almost horizontal, so F ′ ( x ) ≈ 0, so the step ηF ′ ( x ) ≈ 0. You are not at a minimum; you are
simply on a plateau, and you inch across it.
    If the gradient on a plateau is 0.001 and η = 0.01, each step moves you 10−5 . To cross a plateau
of width 2 you need about 200,000 steps. On a real dataset that is hours of GPU time spent going
nowhere.

   In plain words

  A salt flat in fog. The ground is level in every direction, so your “walk downhill” rule gives
  you almost nothing to act on. You shuffle. The cure is momentum: remember the direction you
  were already moving and keep rolling, like a ball that carries speed across the flat instead of
  re-deciding at every footstep.



6. Differential curvature: the canyon problem
Two bowls
                              Lcircle = x2 + y2 ,           Lellipse = x2 + 4y2 .
Both are convex, both have exactly one minimum at the origin. There is no local-minimum problem
here at all — and yet one is easy and the other is not.
   Second derivatives. For the circular bowl,

                                          ∂2 L             ∂2 L
                                               = 2,             = 2.
                                          ∂x2              ∂y2

For the elliptical bowl,
                                          ∂2 L             ∂2 L
                                               = 2,             = 8.
                                          ∂x2              ∂y2
Curvature is 4× larger in y than in x. That single ratio is the whole story.

The circular bowl: one step, if you like
Gradient: ∇ L = (2x, 2y). Update from a point ( x, y):

                                       x ← x − η (2x ) = (1 − 2η ) x
                                       y ← y − η (2y) = (1 − 2η )y

Both coordinates shrink by the same factor (1 − 2η ). Choose η = 0.5:

              (1 − 2η ) = 0      =⇒      ( x, y) → (0, 0) in a single step, from anywhere.

This is what slide 19 means by “a sufficiently large step-size can take us directly to the optimum in
one step”. The gradient points straight at the target because the level sets are circles.

The elliptical bowl: no such luck
Gradient: ∇ L = (2x, 8y). Update:

                                               x ← (1 − 2η ) x
                                               y ← (1 − 8η ) y

Now the two coordinates shrink by different factors, and one η must serve both.
   Stability requirement. For a coordinate not to blow up we need the factor’s magnitude below
1:
                     |1 − 2η | < 1 ⇒ η < 1,      |1 − 8η | < 1 ⇒ η < 0.25.
So η is capped at 0.25 by the steep direction, even though the shallow direction would happily
accept a step four times larger. The most sensitive parameter puts a ceiling on everyone else’s
learning rate.

  In plain words

  Skiing down a narrow gully. Straight down the gully is the direction you actually want to
  go, but the steepest slope at your feet points at the wall. So you shoot across, hit the far wall,
  shoot back. You are always going “downhill” and you are barely making progress. This is
  why the slides say the steepest-descent direction is only the best instantaneous direction, not
  the best direction in the long run.


Why the gradient is perpendicular to the contour line
Slide 19 states it; here is the argument in full.
    A contour line is the set of points where the function has one fixed value, say F = c. Take a
tiny displacement δx that stays on that contour. By the first-order Taylor expansion,

                                     F (x + δx) ≈ F (x) + ∇ F · δx.

                                                     11
ZC416 MFML • Lecture 10 Companion                                                       Optimization


But moving along a contour does not change F at all, so F (x + δx) = F (x), hence

                                              ∇ F · δx = 0.

A zero dot product means the two vectors are at right angles. Since δx points along the contour,
∇ F points across it.
  The one line to remember
  The gradient always crosses the contour lines at 90◦ . On circular contours “across the contour”
  happens to point at the centre. On stretched contours it does not — and that gap is exactly
  the zig-zag.

  The one line to remember
  The condition number dropped from about 1487 to about 10 — a 145× improvement — and
  we did not touch the model, the algorithm or the data’s meaning. We only changed the units.
  That is the cheapest win available in all of machine learning.

  What normalisation does not fix
  Notice κ is 10, not 1. The leftover stretch comes from the off-diagonal term −4.93: guns and
  butter are correlated in this dataset, so the two knobs are not independent, and the contours
  stay tilted (see the right panel above). Scaling equalises the axes; it does not decorrelate
  them. Removing that residual tilt is what PCA-whitening, and in spirit what Adam and
  second-order methods, are for.

   In plain words

   Almost every trick in modern optimisation is one of two ideas. (1) Reshape the landscape so
   plain gradient descent works — that is normalisation, batch-norm, better parameterisation.
   (2) Give the walker better instincts — momentum (keep rolling), adaptive learning rates
   (a private η per parameter, so the stiff knob is not held hostage by the hair-trigger one), and
   second-order methods (feel the curvature, not just the slope).



9. Check yourself
Q1. A model has 8 parameters and you train it on 6 rows. Should you be pleased when the training
error hits exactly zero?
    Q2. For L = x2 + 9y2 , what is the largest learning rate that will not blow up? Which direction
imposes the limit?
    Q3. A column of your data has µ = 40 and σ = 8. What is the standardised value of the entry
56?
    Q4. You run gradient descent twice from two different random starts and get two different
final losses. What does this tell you about the loss surface?
    Q5. Why does the gradient meet a contour line at a right angle?

     A1. No. With 8 unknowns and 6 equations the system is underdetermined, so infinitely many weight
vectors give zero error; the optimiser hands you one arbitrarily and it is probably a memoriser. Judge on
held-out data.
     A2. ∇ L = (2x, 18y), so the update factors are (1 − 2η ) and (1 − 18η ). Stability needs |1 − 18η | < 1, i.e.
η < 2/18 = 1/9 ≈ 0.111. The y-direction — the one with the larger curvature ∂2 L/∂y2 = 18 — sets the
ceiling.
     A3. z = (56 − 40)/8 = 2. The entry sits two standard deviations above the mean.
     A4. The surface is non-convex: it has more than one local minimum, and each start fell into a different
basin. (A convex bowl would return the same answer every time.)
