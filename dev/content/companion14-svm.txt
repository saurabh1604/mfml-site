     Infinitely many straight lines separate two clouds of points. SVM picks the one
     with the widest empty street around it. Once you fix an arbitrary scale, that

     depends only on a handful of borderline points, and it touches the data only
     through dot products. Replace the dot product with a kernel and the same
     machinery draws curved boundaries in spaces far too large to write down.

The problem, and the idea
You have 𝑛 labelled examples (𝑥𝑖 , 𝑦𝑖 ), where each 𝑥𝑖 ∈ ℝ𝑑 is a vector of
measurements and each label is 𝑦𝑖 ∈ {−1, +1}. You want a rule that takes a new 𝑥
and predicts its label.

    Linear classifier. Pick a direction 𝑤 and an offset 𝑏, compute the score
    𝑓(𝑥) = 𝑤 ⋅ 𝑥 + 𝑏              , and predict sign𝑓(𝑥). The set where 𝑓(𝑥) = 0 is the decision
    boundary.

If the two classes can be separated by such a boundary at all, they can be
separated by infinitely many of them — tilt the line a little, slide it a little, and it
still gets every training point right. Every one of those lines has zero training
error. Training error cannot choose between them.


1.1 So what should choose?
The training points are not the points you care about. You care about the next point,
which will land somewhere nearby but not exactly on top of an old one. A boundary
that skims within a hair of a training example is one small measurement wobble
away from getting that region wrong. A boundary that keeps a wide, empty buffer
on both sides can absorb that wobble.


     ◈ THE STREET

     Think of it as paving the widest possible street between the two classes,
     with no point of either class allowed inside the street. The decision boundary is
     the white line down the middle. Among all streets you could pave, take the
     widest. The idea is that simple — the rest of this course is turning "widest
     street" into equations, and then solving them.



    Margin. The width of that empty street: the perpendicular distance from the
    decision boundary out to the nearest training point, doubled. A maximum-
    margin classifier is the boundary that makes this as large as possible.

1.2 Why the margin is a sensible thing to maximise
     Robustness. Every training point can move by up to half the margin in any
     direction without changing its side of the boundary. Wide margin = tolerance to
     noise, quantified.

     Uniqueness. Out of infinitely many zero-error boundaries, "widest" picks exactly
     one. The problem stops being ill-posed.

     Generalisation. There are real theoretical bounds saying test error is controlled
     by the margin relative to how spread out the data is — not by the number of
     features. This is why SVMs behave sanely even when you have more features
     than examples, which sinks many other methods.

     Sparsity, for free. As Chapter 9 shows, the answer ends up depending only on
     the points that touch the street. Everything in the interior of each cloud is
     irrelevant.

The geometry of a hyperplane
Before we can maximise a width we need to be able to measure one. Two facts do
all the work, and both are two lines of algebra.


2.1 Proof that 𝑤 is perpendicular to the boundary
Take any two points 𝑝 and 𝑞 lying on the boundary. Both satisfy the equation:

                                                  𝑤⋅𝑝 + 𝑏 = 0      𝑤⋅𝑞 + 𝑏 = 0



Subtract the second from the first. The 𝑏 cancels:


                                                        𝑤 ⋅ (𝑝 − 𝑞) = 0

         the vector 𝑝 − 𝑞 points along the boundary, and 𝑤 is orthogonal to it — for every such pair, hence for
                                            every direction in the boundary



So 𝑤 is perpendicular to the entire boundary. It is the normal vector. This is exactly
why ‖𝑤‖ is about to control the width: widths are measured perpendicular to the
boundary, which means along 𝑤.

independent knobs: 𝑤 sets the orientation, 𝑏 sets the position.

Fixing the scale: where the ±1 comes from
Chapter 3 left one loose end: the constant 𝑐. The margin came out as 2𝑐 / ‖𝑤‖, which
still has an unknown in it. This chapter shows that 𝑐 is not a real quantity at all — it
is an artefact of how you happened to write the equations — and that we are free to
set it to 1.


4.1 Three planes, one geometry, infinitely many equations

4.4 Why not just maximise the raw score?
A natural first idea is to make min𝑖 𝑦𝑖 (𝑤 ⋅ 𝑥𝑖 + 𝑏) as big as possible. The table above
shows why it fails: multiply (𝑤, 𝑏) by 𝑘 = 106 and the score is a million times bigger
while the boundary has not moved. The optimiser would run 𝑘 → ∞ and report
infinite confidence in a line it never improved. The problem is ill-posed until you pin
the scale. There are exactly two clean ways out, and they are the same thing seen
from two sides:

     Fix the score, minimise the norm. Set 𝑐 = 1, then minimise ‖𝑤‖. ← this is what
     SVM does

      zero, solve.

   5. Check αi ≥ 0 and note which points are support vectors (all of them, in the usual symmetric
      exam data).

    THE RULE OF THUMB BEHIND ALL FIVE ANSWERS

  Try linear first, and mean it. Go to RBF when the boundary is genuinely curved and you
  cannot name the shape. Use a degree-2 polynomial when you can name the interaction that
  matters. Never pick a kernel because it is the fancier one — every extra dimension you buy is
  a dimension the model can overfit in, and C and γ then have to be cross-validated together.

    ANSWERS AT A GLANCE

  1. linear · 2. RBF (degree-2 polynomial also works) · 3. linear with a modest C — an over-
  lap problem, not a kernel problem · 4. polynomial p = 2, which supplies x1 x2 · 5. non-
  homogeneous polynomial p = 2, so the main effects survive.
