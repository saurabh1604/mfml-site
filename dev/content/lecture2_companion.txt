1. Why Vector Spaces? An ML-First Motivation
In Lecture 1 we played with systems of equations Ax = b. We found solutions, talked about pivots,
manipulated rows. That was tactical. Lecture 2 is strategic. We zoom out and ask: what kind of
world do these vectors live in?
   The answer is: a structured world we call a vector space. Once you know the rules of this world,
every algorithm in machine learning starts making sense as a small geometric story inside it.
   Reading the notation behind every ML object
  Before we go any further, let’s decode the symbols you’ll see throughout MFML. They look
  intimidating, but each one is just a way of saying “a list of real numbers with a particular
  shape.”

  Why an ML student should care
  Almost every object in ML is a vector in some vector space:

  • A photograph of a cat is a vector in RH×W ×3 — i.e., a 3-D tensor with height H, width
    W , and 3 color channels (R, G, B).
  • A sentence “the food was great” is a sequence of word-embedding vectors in R768 (i.e.,
    each word becomes a column of 768 real numbers, the BERT-base size) or R4096 (the typical
    hidden size for many modern LLMs).
  • A user on Netflix is a vector of preferences in some Rd — one column of d numbers, where
    the choice of d depends on the recommender system.
  • A neural network’s weights are matrices in Rm×n : an m-row, n-column grid of real
    numbers, which is itself a “vector” once we view the whole grid as a single object.

  Training a model is essentially about moving inside these spaces in clever ways. If you don’t
  know the rules of the space, you can’t reason about the move.

  The big-picture analogy
  Think of a vector space as the board game ML plays on. Groups give us the addition rules.
  Vector spaces add scaling rules. Subspaces are valid regions of the board. Linear independence
  says which moves are redundant. A basis is the minimal toolkit that can reach every square.
  Dimension is how big the board really is. Master those six pieces, and the rest of MFML is
  mostly geometry.



2. Groups: The Bare Skeleton of “Combining Things”
Before we can define a vector space, we need a more primitive structure: a group. A group is the
minimum amount of structure we need so that “combining two things gives us a sensible third thing.”
   Group — in plain English

  A group is just a set of things (call it G) together with a way to combine any two of them (call
  this combine-operation ⊗). For (G, ⊗) to deserve the name “group,” the combine-operation
  has to behave nicely in four ways:

 (G1) Closure — combining two elements of G gives back something in G. (“You don’t fall
      out of the set.”)
 (G2) Associativity — the order in which you group your operations doesn’t matter: (a⊗b)⊗c
      equals a ⊗ (b ⊗ c). (Think (1+2)+3 = 1+(2+3).)
 (G3) Identity — there’s a special “do-nothing” element e in the set: combining with e leaves
      any element unchanged. (For addition, e = 0. For multiplication, e = 1.)
 (G4) Inverse — every element has a partner that “undoes” it, returning you to e. (For
      addition, the partner of 5 is −5.)

   Bonus property: If the order of combination doesn’t matter either (a ⊗ b = b ⊗ a), we call
   the group Abelian. (Vanilla addition is Abelian; matrix multiplication is not.)

Example A — All whole numbers (positive and negative) with addition
Set + op: (Z, +). So our elements are . . . , −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, . . . and we combine using +.

• Adding two whole numbers gives a whole number. Closure ✓
• Grouping doesn’t matter: (2+3)+5 = 2+(3+5). Associativity ✓
• Adding 0 leaves any number unchanged. Identity ✓ (the do-nothing element is 0)
• Every number n has a partner −n that cancels it: 5 + (−5) = 0. Inverse ✓

Verdict: Abelian group. This is the simplest, friendliest example to keep in mind.

Example B — Counting numbers (with zero) under addition
Set + op: (N0 , +). Our elements are 0, 1, 2, 3, . . . (no negatives allowed).

• Adding two counting numbers gives a counting number. Closure ✓
• Grouping doesn’t matter. Associativity ✓
• 0 still acts as the identity. Identity ✓
• Here’s the problem: how do you “undo” adding 5? You’d need to add −5 — but −5 isn’t in the
  set. There’s no way back to 0 once you’ve moved away from it. Inverse ✗

Verdict: Not a group — no inverses. This is the lesson: even one missing axiom disqualifies you.

Example C — All whole numbers under multiplication
Set + op: (Z, ·). Same elements as Example A, but now we multiply instead of add.

• Multiplying two whole numbers gives a whole number. Closure ✓
• Multiplication is associative. Associativity ✓
• Multiplying by 1 leaves any number unchanged. Identity ✓ (the do-nothing element is 1)
• Now the problem: how do you “undo” multiplying by 2? You’d divide by 2, i.e., multiply by 12 .
  But 12 isn’t a whole number! Most whole numbers have no inverse here. Inverse ✗

Verdict: Not a group. Even though identity exists, almost no one has a partner.

Example D — All non-zero real numbers under multiplication
Set + op: (R \ {0}, ·). Take all the numbers on the number line, except 0, and multiply them.

• Two non-zero reals multiply to a non-zero real. Closure ✓
• Associativity is fine. Associativity ✓
• 1 is the do-nothing. Identity ✓
• Every non-zero x has a partner x1 (also a non-zero real), and x · x1 = 1. Inverse ✓

Verdict: Abelian group. Why we excluded zero: 0 has no multiplicative inverse (you can’t divide
by zero). Removing it fixes the inverse axiom.

Example E — Vectors of any fixed dimension under addition
Set + op: (Rn , +). Our elements are vectors (columns of n real numbers); we add them entry-by-
entry.

• Vector + vector = vector (same shape). Closure ✓
• Adding entry-by-entry inherits associativity. Associativity ✓
• The all-zeros vector 0 does nothing when added. Identity ✓
• Every x has a partner −x that cancels it: x + (−x) = 0. Inverse ✓

Verdict: Abelian group. This one matters most for ML — almost every vector you’ll meet (gradients,
embeddings, weight matrices) lives in this kind of group.

  The pattern — what to look for
   Closure and associativity rarely break; they’re the easy ones. The two axioms that knock
   candidates out are:

  • Identity — “Is there a do-nothing element?” (For addition that’s 0; for multiplication
    that’s 1.)
  • Inverse — “Can I always undo what I did?” This one fails most often — whenever your
    set is missing the negatives, fractions, or whatever “undo” partner each element needs.

  When checking any new structure, just walk these four questions in order. The moment one
  fails, you’ve found the issue.

   Bonus real-world group — clock arithmetic

  Imagine the hours on a 12-hour clock: {0, 1, 2, . . . , 11} where “addition” means “advance the
  hour hand and wrap around past 12.” For example, 9 + 5 = 14, but on the clock that’s
  14 − 12 = 2. So 9 + 5 = 2 in clock-world.
  This is an Abelian group: closure (you stay on the clock), associativity (grouping doesn’t
  matter), identity (0 does nothing), inverse (the partner of 5 is 7 because 5 + 7 = 12 ≡ 0). It’s
  the same four axioms, just on a finite, circular set — and it’s the foundation of cryptography,
  hashing, and the math behind modern AI’s positional embeddings.

   ML Perspective
  You won’t typically write “let G be a group” in your training loop. But you rely on group
  structure constantly:

  • Gradient descent does w ← w − η∇L. This is just repeated addition in the group (Rd , +).
    Closure guarantees the new weight vector still lives in Rd .
  • Rotations of an image (used in data augmentation) form a group SO(2). Composing two
    rotations gives a rotation; every rotation is invertible.
  • “Permutations of tokens” in a sentence form the symmetric group Sn — the rules attention
    layers must respect (or break, in the case of position encodings).



3. Vector Spaces: Group + Scaling
A vector space is what happens when you take an Abelian group and bolt on the ability to stretch
elements by real numbers.




                                                 7
MFML / MFDS Companion                                                                     Lecture 2 • Vector Spaces


   Vector Space — in plain English

   A vector space is a set of objects (we call them vectors) where you’re allowed to do exactly
   two things:

   • Add two vectors and get another vector. (“Inner” operation: u + v stays in the space.)
   • Scale a vector by a real number and get another vector. (“Outer” operation: 3u or −0.5u
     stays in the space.)

   And these two operations must play well together:

  (V1) Vector addition is an Abelian group — i.e., addition is associative and commutative,
       there’s a zero vector 0 that does nothing, and every v has a negative −v that cancels it.
  (V2) Distributivity — scaling distributes over addition, just like in school algebra: λ(x+y) =
       λx + λy and (λ + ψ)x = λx + ψx.
  (V3) Scaling stacks — scaling by λ and then by ψ is the same as scaling by λψ once:
       λ(ψx) = (λψ)x.
  (V4) Scaling by 1 does nothing — 1 · x = x.

   Translation. A vector space is just a place where the two basic moves of linear algebra (add,
   scale) always make sense and never throw you out of the playground.

   The drawing-board picture
   Imagine an infinite drawing board with an origin marked 0. A vector is a movement instruction
   from the origin.

   • Addition chains movements: “go along u, then along v.”
   • Scaling stretches a movement: “go three times as far along u.”

   The vector-space axioms are the guarantees that no matter how you mix these moves, you
   never fall off the board. That closure is what makes the space “a space”.

4. Subspaces: Spaces Inside Spaces
A vector space sometimes contains smaller, self-contained vector spaces inside it. We call those
subspaces.
  Vector Subspace — in plain English
   A subspace is just a smaller vector space living inside a bigger one. Concretely: a subset U of
   a vector space V that’s still a self-contained vector space all by itself, using the same addition
   and scaling rules.
   Think of it like a flat plane sitting inside the 3-D room you’re in: the plane is its own little
   2-D world that follows all the same rules as the surrounding 3-D space.

   The good news: most of the axioms come for free, because U inherits them from V . We only
need to verify three things:
   The Subspace Test — 3 things to check

   A subset U of a vector space V is a subspace if (and only if) all three are true:

  (S1) It contains the origin. The zero vector 0 must be in U . (If 0 isn’t in your set, stop —
       it can’t be a subspace.)
  (S2) It’s closed under adding. Pick any two vectors in U , add them: the result must still
       be in U .
  (S3) It’s closed under scaling. Pick any vector in U and any real number λ: the scaled
       vector λu must still be in U .

  Why only three? Everything else (associativity, distributivity, etc.) is inherited automatically
  from the bigger space V , since U uses the same operations. We only need to verify that we
  don’t accidentally leave U when we add or scale.

• (d) Any line through the origin. Same logic as the y-axis: scaling and adding stay on the

  The one-line rule of thumb
   In Rn , the only subspaces are flat shapes that pass through the origin: lines through
   0, planes through 0, the whole space, and just {0} itself. Anything that doesn’t pass through
   0, or anything curved or bounded (like the square), is not a subspace — because somewhere
   along the way, addition or scaling will take you outside.


4.2 The most important subspace: the null space
   Null Space of a Matrix — in plain English

  Take a matrix A (m rows, n columns). Feed it different input vectors x and look at what
  comes out: Ax.
  The null space of A is the set of all the inputs that A flattens to zero:

                                 null(A) = { x ∈ Rn : Ax = 0 }.

  You can think of it as “the directions A can’t see” — vectors that the matrix sends to nothing.

   Why is this a subspace? Walk through the test:

1. It contains 0 since A0 = 0.
2. If Ax = 0 and Ay = 0, then A(x + y) = Ax + Ay = 0. Closed under addition.
3. If Ax = 0, then A(λx) = λ(Ax) = 0. Closed under scaling.

So null(A) passes the subspace test for any matrix A.
  Why null spaces matter for ML
  The null space describes the directions a linear map cannot see. In ML this is everywhere:

  • In a linear regression y = Xw, any w in the null space of X leaves predictions unchanged.
    That’s why we need regularization when X has a non-trivial null space (collinear features!).
  • In PCA, the directions we throw away form a subspace; the kept directions form a
    complementary subspace. Both are subspaces.
  • In neural network compression, “pruning a weight” is essentially saying “this direction
    is in the (effective) null space of the layer” so removing it costs little.

5. Linear Combinations and Span: What You Can Reach
Now we ask: starting from a few vectors, where can scaling-and-adding take us?
   Linear Combination — in plain English

  Take a few vectors x1 , x2 , . . . , xk and a few real numbers λ1 , λ2 , . . . , λk (called weights or
  coefficients). Stretch each vector by its weight, then add them all up:

                                     v = λ1 x1 + λ2 x2 + · · · + λk xk .

   The result v is called a linear combination of those vectors. Different choices of weights give
   different combinations.

   Span — in plain English
   The span of a set of vectors is everything you can possibly build by linear combinations of
   them. Try every possible choice of weights λ1 , . . . , λk — the collection of all the resulting
   vectors is the span:
                                                    n                                 o
                         span(x1 , . . . , xk ) =       λ1 x1 + · · · + λk xk : λi ∈ R .

   Important fact: the span is always a subspace — i.e., it always passes the three subspace
   tests.

  By picking the right weights, you can hit any point (a, b): just take ae1 + be2 . So the span
  of these two vectors is the entire 2-D plane. That’s why {e1 , e2 } is the standard “coordinate
  basis” for R2 .

  Span as “reachable destinations”
  Picture each vector as a transport mode: a train going north-east, a bus going south, a bike
  going east. The span is every place on the map you can possibly arrive at by combining
  these modes (walking backwards counts — that’s a negative weight). If two of your modes go
  the same direction, the second one didn’t actually expand your reach: the map of reachable
  destinations stays the same.

  Span in machine learning
  • Word embeddings. Take the vectors for king, queen, man, woman. Their span is a
    small “concept subspace” inside the giant embedding space. Famous demonstrations like
    king − man + woman ≈ queen live entirely in this span.
  • Linear regression. The hypothesis class ŷ = Xw literally outputs vectors in
    span(columns of X) — also called the column space of X.
  • Low-rank approximation & PCA. Compressing data means projecting it onto the span
    of the top few eigenvectors. The smaller the span, the more you’ve compressed.

6. Linear Independence: The Art of Non-Redundancy
  Linear (In)dependence — in plain English

  The question: can you combine the vectors x1 , . . . , xk with weights and have them cancel
  each other out to give the zero vector — without setting all the weights to zero?
  Linearly dependent: Yes, you can. There exist weights λ1 , . . . , λk , not all zero, such that

                                      λ1 x1 + · · · + λk xk = 0.

  This means at least one of the vectors is “redundant” — it could have been expressed using
  the others. The set is wasting space.
  Linearly independent: No, it’s impossible. The only way to make them sum to 0 is the
  boring way: set every weight to zero. Each vector contributes something the others can’t
  replicate.

  The plain-English version
  Independent = “each vector brings something new to the table that none of the others can
  replicate.”
  Dependent = “one of these vectors can already be expressed using the others — it’s redundant.”

• Middle. The second arrow is just the first one stretched (here, by a factor of 1.7). One is a
  scaling of the other — pure duplication. Dependent.
• Right. Three arrows in 2-D space. The third one isn’t a scaling of either of the first two, but
  it can be obtained as a mix of them (e.g. “a bit of the first, plus a bit of the second”). It’s
  redundant. Dependent.

  Two handy shortcuts
  • The zero vector ruins independence instantly. If 0 is in your set, the set is automati-
    cally dependent. (Just put weight 1 on 0 and 0 on everyone else — you’ve got a non-trivial
    combination summing to 0.)
  • For non-zero vectors: dependent means at least one of them is a mix of the others.
    Independent means no vector can be reconstructed from the rest.

6.2 The algorithmic test: Gaussian elimination
You don’t have to guess. There is a foolproof procedure.
   The Gaussian elimination recipe

   1. Stack the vectors as the columns of a matrix A.
   2. Reduce A to row-echelon form.
   3. Look at the columns:

     • All columns have a pivot ⇒ vectors are independent.
     • Some column has no pivot ⇒ vectors are dependent; in fact each non-pivot column
       is a linear combination of the pivot columns to its left.

  Why ML practitioners chase “independence”
  “Linearly dependent features” is just the math name for what data scientists call multicollinear-
  ity.

  • Two columns of your design matrix are nearly proportional ⇒ the model has a (near-)null
    direction it can’t pin down. Coefficients become unstable and high-variance.
  • Solutions in practice: drop the redundant feature, regularize (ridge/L2), or use PCA to
    project onto an independent set of directions.
  • In deep learning, “filter redundancy” inside a CNN layer is the same idea — different filters
    learning essentially the same feature is wasted capacity.

7. The Independence-of-Combinations Theorem
This is the central technical idea of the lecture. It looks abstract at first, but it’s the engine behind
“finding a basis” and several proofs you’ll see later in MFML.

7.1 The setup, told as a story
Imagine you already have k trusted, non-redundant building blocks: vectors b1 , b2 , . . . , bk that are
linearly independent. From them, you build m new vectors x1 , . . . , xm — each one a recipe of how
much of each bi to mix:
                               xj = λ1j b1 + λ2j b2 + · · · + λkj bk .
Each new vector xj is fully described by its recipe: the list of weights (λ1j , . . . , λkj ).
   The natural question: are these new xj ’s themselves independent, or did we accidentally build
redundant ones?

7.2 Putting all the recipes into one picture
Pack the building blocks side-by-side as columns of a matrix:
                                               
                    B =        b1 b2 · · · bk       (a matrix with the bi as its columns).

Pack the recipe for xj into a column λj . With these two pieces, building xj becomes one matrix-vector
product:
                                             xj = B λj .
Read it like this: “stack the building blocks in B, multiply by the recipe λj , and out pops the new
vector xj .”


                                         B (k cols)     ·    λj    =     xj


                                       independent basiscoordinates new vector


7.3 The theorem, in one sentence
   The big result
   The new vectors x1 , . . . , xm are independent if and only if their recipes λ1 , . . . , λm
   are independent.
   In symbols:

                    {x1 , . . . , xm } independent      ⇐⇒        {λ1 , . . . , λm } independent.

   What this is really saying: once you’ve fixed a trustworthy basis, checking independence
   in the original space is the same problem as checking independence among the recipes. It’s a
   translation of the question into easier coordinates.


7.4 Why is this true? (a 2-step sketch)
We want to know whether the only way to make ψ1 x1 + · · · + ψm xm = 0 is to set every ψj = 0. Two
steps:
    Step 1 — substitute the recipes. Replace each xj with B λj :
                                                                                                    
            ψ1 x1 + · · · + ψm xm = ψ1 B λ1 + · · · + ψm B λm = B ψ1 λ1 + · · · + ψm λm .

Call the bracketed expression v.


                                                            17
MFML / MFDS Companion                                                      Lecture 2 • Vector Spaces


   Step 2 — use the trustworthy basis. Since the columns of B (our independent bi ) are
independent, the only way B v can equal 0 is if v = 0. In other words, the original question

                                          ψ1 x1 + · · · + ψm xm = 0

is exactly the same as the recipe-level question

                                          ψ1 λ1 + · · · + ψm λm = 0.

And that’s exactly the linear-independence question for the recipes.                                       ■
   The “coordinates inherit independence” picture
   Once you fix an independent set b1 , . . . , bk , every new vector built from them is fully described
   by its coordinate vector λj . The independence/dependence of the new vectors is the
   same as the independence/dependence of their coordinate vectors. Geometry in
   the original space mirrors geometry in coordinate space — that mirror is the whole reason
   coordinate systems are useful in the first place.


7.5 The big consequence: too many vectors ⇒ guaranteed redundancy
Each recipe λj is a column with k entries (one weight per building block). So the recipes live inside
Rk — a space “k entries wide.”
   If you build more new vectors than you have building blocks (m > k), you’re forcing more recipes
than Rk can hold independently. The recipes must have a hidden relation among them, and by the
theorem so do the xj .
   Pigeonhole consequence — in plain English

   If you build more new vectors than you have independent building blocks (m > k),
   the new vectors are guaranteed to be redundant.
  The matrix view: stacking the recipes as columns gives a k × m matrix with more columns
  than rows. That matrix can have at most k pivots, so there must be free (non-pivot) columns
  — which is exactly what dependence looks like.


                            pivot pivot        free   free




                  rows: k      k × m matrix, m > k            free columns ⇒ dependence


                                     columns: m

   Why this theorem matters in ML

   • Dimensionality reduction. Once you choose a small basis b1 , . . . , bk (e.g., principal
     components), every data point is just its coordinate vector in Rk . ML on the coordinates is
     mathematically equivalent to ML on the originals — the theorem above is what guarantees
     this.
   • Embedding analyses. When you ask “are these 50 word vectors independent in a 32-dim
     space?” the answer is always no (50 > 32) — you’ve discovered a relation purely from
     dimension counting.
   • Capacity arguments. A neural network layer with k neurons can only produce activations
     in a k-dimensional subspace. Any m > k output activations across a batch must satisfy a
     linear relation.

8. Basis and Dimension: The Skeleton of a Space
We’ve seen that span tells us what we can build, and independence tells us when our building blocks
are non-redundant. A basis is the perfect marriage of both.
   A grounded story to anchor everything
  Imagine you’re at the origin of a flat 2-D map and you want to be able to reach any point on
  the map by walking. You can only walk in straight lines, and you’re allowed to combine walks
  (“walk 3 steps in direction A, then 2 steps in direction B”).

   • If you only know north, you can reach the entire vertical axis but no east-west points. Not
     enough directions — you don’t have a generating set of the plane.
   • If you know north and east, you can reach any point on the map. Generating set!
   • If you know north, east, and north-east, you can still reach any point — but “north-east” is
     just a mix of the other two; it’s redundant. Generating set, but not minimal.
   • If you know north and east (and only those), you have the just-right toolkit: enough to
     reach everything, with nothing wasted. That’s a basis.

   The whole rest of this section is just formal vocabulary for the three situations above.

   Generating Set and Basis — in plain English

   Take a small collection of vectors {x1 , . . . , xk } in some vector space V :

   • It’s a generating set of V if you can build every vector in V by mixing these with weights.
     (Like “north + east is enough to reach the whole 2-D plane.”)

   • It’s a basis of V if it’s a generating set and no vector in it is redundant. (Like “north +
     east, with nothing extra.”)

   One-sentence slogan. A basis is the smallest possible toolkit that still reaches everything in
   the space.

   Recipe analogy — when does each name apply?
   Think of building vectors as cooking dishes:

   • Not a generating set: your pantry is missing ingredients — you literally cannot make
     some dishes.
   • Generating set, but not minimal: you have everything you need, plus some duplicates
     (two bottles of soy sauce). You can cook everything, but you’ve wasted shelf space.
   • Basis: every ingredient is essential, and together they let you make every dish. Remove
     any one and some dish becomes impossible to make.


8.1 Four ways to spot a basis
A basis can be described in four different-looking ways, but all four are saying the exact same thing.
Each angle gives a different gut feeling. Use whichever feels easier in the moment.

Let’s walk through what each one really means with a concrete picture.

• (1) Textbook definition. Like “north and east” on a 2-D map: together they reach every
  point (spans), and neither is a copy of the other (independent). This is the formal version of the
  Goldilocks story.

• (2) Smallest possible toolkit. If you tried to remove “east,” you’d lose every east-west point.
  So you can’t shrink the toolkit. This view is helpful when you want to prove a basis exists — start
  with a generating set and toss out redundant vectors one at a time until you can’t anymore.

• (3) Biggest possible toolkit. If you tried to add “north-east” to the basis, it would already
  be expressible as “north + east” — redundant. So you can’t grow the toolkit while staying
  independent. This view is helpful when you want to extend a partial set into a basis — keep
  adding non-redundant vectors until you can’t anymore.

• (4) Unique address book. This is the most useful one in practice. Once you’ve
  fixed a basis, every vector has exactly one recipe in it. Like GPS coordinates: there’s exactly
  one (latitude, longitude) for the Eiffel Tower — not two, not three. Without this uniqueness,
  “coordinates” wouldn’t even be a meaningful idea.

  Why all four are the same fact

  Property (1) is just (2) and (3) said together: “spans” = “can’t shrink any further while still
  spanning” (that’s (2)), and “independent” = “can’t grow without losing independence” (that’s
  (3)). And (4) follows from independence: if the basis weren’t independent, the same vector
  could have two different recipes (we proved this on the previous page).

  Why does a basis give unique coordinates?
   Suppose a single vector v has two different recipes in the basis:

                                v = λ1 b1 + · · · + λk bk = ψ1 b1 + · · · + ψk bk .

   Subtracting, the difference recipe satisfies (λ1 − ψ1 )b1 + · · · + (λk − ψk )bk = 0. But the bi are
   independent — so the only way that combination can be 0 is if every weight in it is zero. That
   forces λi = ψi for each i.
   Moral: the recipe is forced to be unique, because the basis is independent. “Basis = unique
   addresses” is just another way of saying “no redundancy in the basis.”

  • PCA = “find a new orthonormal basis for the data, ordered by variance.”

8.3 Dimension
  Dimension — in plain English

  Pick any basis of a (finite-dimensional) vector space V and count how many vectors are in it.
  Pick a different basis and count again. You’ll always get the same number.
  That number is intrinsic to the space itself — we call it the dimension of V , written dim(V ).
  It’s the answer to “how many independent directions does this space really have?”


• dim(Rn ) = n — a column of n real numbers needs n basis vectors (the standard ones e1 , . . . , en ).
• dim(Rm×n ) = m · n — an m × n matrix has mn entries; a basis is the family of “one-hot” matrices
  Eij that have a single 1 at position (i, j) and 0 elsewhere.
• If U ⊆ V , then dim(U ) ≤ dim(V ), with equality iff U = V (a subspace can never be “bigger”
  than the space it lives in).

  A very common confusion
  The number of components in a vector is not always the dimension of its space.
  Consider                                " #!

                                           1
  Each vector has 2 components, but the space is just the y-axis: it’s a line, not a plane. So
  dim(V ) = 1. Components count the ambient space; dimension counts the intrinsic space.

  Ambient vs. intrinsic dimension
  A road on a 2-D map has only 1 dimension of freedom: “how far along the road.” The map (the
  ambient space) is 2-D, but the road (the subspace) is 1-D. ML calls this distinction intrinsic
  dimensionality, and a huge amount of representation learning is about discovering it: real-world
  data tends to live on a low-dimensional surface inside its high-dimensional ambient space —
  the famous manifold hypothesis.

9. Finding a Basis: The Algorithm
You’re handed a bunch of vectors that span some subspace U ⊆ Rn . How do you extract a basis
from them?
   The 3-step procedure

   A basis of U = span(x1 , . . . , xm ) ⊆ Rn can be found by:

   1. Stack the spanning vectors as columns of a matrix A.
   2. Reduce A to row-echelon form.
   3. The original spanning vectors corresponding to the pivot columns form a basis of U .

   Important nuance
   The basis is the original pivot columns of A, not the columns of the row-reduced matrix. Row
   reduction tells you which columns to keep, not what they are.

9.2 Why this procedure works (1-paragraph proof sketch)
Row operations don’t change which subset of column vectors is independent — they only change
the rows (the way each column vector “looks”). So the columns that remain independent in the
reduced form are precisely the columns that were independent in the original. By the theorem of
Section 7, the pivot columns of A are independent; the non-pivot columns are linear combinations of
pivot columns to their left. Hence the original pivot columns span U and are independent — a basis.
   Connection to model capacity
  The number of pivot columns is the rank of the matrix A — the dimension of its column space.
  In ML:

  • For a feature matrix X, rank(X) tells you how many of your features are truly independent.
    Anything beyond that is collinearity.
  • For a neural network weight matrix W , rank(W ) caps how many “effective directions” the
    layer can express. Low-rank approximations (LoRA, factorized layers) explicitly exploit this
    to compress models.
  • For a Jacobian J of a network, rank(J) describes how many directions of input change
    actually affect the output.

10.2 The mental loop you should run from now on
  Four questions to ask whenever you see a vector or matrix in ML

  1. What space does this live in? (column space? null space? embedding space?)

  3. What is the span / column space / null space / rank? (capacity, expressiveness,

  This four-question loop is most of the geometric reasoning behind classical ML and a great
