1 | Why are we even talking about lines?


     PEN I NSTAGRAM . Scroll for ten seconds. The app just decided what to show you: which
O   photo, which order, which ad. Behind that decision is a tower of math. Strip the tower
down, and at the very bottom you find one humble object — a system of linear equations.
Same story for the GPS finding your route, the camera autofocusing on a face, ChatGPT
predicting the next word, your bank’s fraud alert. Every one of them, sooner or later, lands
on something of the form
                                         Ax = b.
So before we earn the right to talk about neural networks or transformers, we need to get
very comfortable with this little equation. That’s all of Lecture 1.

But wait — is the world actually linear?
Look out the window. Roads curve. Light bends around things. The stock market zigzags.
Almost nothing in real life is a straight line.
So why does an entire branch of math, and the engine room of every ML model, obsess
about linear things?




                                                 2
    Intuition

   Three honest reasons.
   1. Linear things can always be solved. Not “solved if we’re lucky” — always. We
   can write a recipe, hand it to a computer, and either get the answer or learn for sure
   no answer exists. Most non-linear problems do not give you that guarantee. (For
   non-linear systems, even checking “does a solution exist?” can be a research-level
   question.)
   2. Up close, almost everything looks linear. The Earth is round, but the floor of your
   room is flat. A roller-coaster track curves, but at any single instant it’s straight. A
   photograph of your face curves around your cheekbone, but each tiny pixel is a flat
   patch.
   This is why calculus works. ML — gradient descent, neural-network training, optimi-
   sation — runs on this same trick: zoom in enough, and the world is linear again. Solve the
   easy linear problem. Take a small step. Zoom in again. Repeat a million times. That is
   literally how a neural network learns.
   3. You can see it. Lines, planes, hyperplanes. Geometry you can draw on a napkin.
   That’s a luxury most other branches of math don’t give you.


The price you pay for simplicity
A single straight line cannot draw a circle. It cannot trace a roller-coaster’s curve. It cannot
follow a stock-market chart. Linear things are simple, but simple things are limited.
So machine learning’s job is mostly to stack many simple linear pieces, with a sprinkle of
non-linear glue (a function like ReLU or sigmoid) between them. Picture LEGO bricks: each
brick is dumb and rectangular — but a million of them can build a castle. A neural network
is exactly this: linear → nonlinear → linear → nonlinear → . . . . Mostly linear, with just
enough bend to escape the limits of straightness.

What is a vector, really?
A vector is just an ordered list of numbers — and the rules for adding and scaling them. That’s
it. Take a moment with that. Vectors are not mysterious arrows in some abstract space; they
are lists.
Some lists you already use:

• A house in a real-estate dataset is a vector: (area, bedrooms, age, price).
• A photo is a vector: one number per pixel, hundreds of thousands of them in a long list.
• A word, in modern NLP, is a vector — the famous “embedding” — maybe 768 numbers
  long, learned by the model.
• Even you, in your bank’s risk model, are a vector: (age, income, credit score, . . . ).

For now, picture an arrow on a piece of paper, or a list like (3, −2, 7). Whatever helps. Both
pictures are correct, and a big part of getting good at linear algebra is fluently switching
between the two.

2 | A small problem you can solve in your head


    Imagine this

  You run a small workshop that makes three kinds of furniture kits: chairs, tables, and
  cabinets. Each one needs different amounts of wood, labor, machine time, and shipping
  effort. Today you have exactly 9 units of wood, 8 hours of labor, 7 hours of machine
  time, and 11 units of shipping effort.
   Question: how many of each kit should you make so that nothing is wasted — every
   resource used exactly?


    HIS IS A REAL planning problem. A factory manager solves a version of it every Monday
T    morning. Translate the scenario into math, one resource at a time. If you make x1 chairs,
x2 tables, x3 cabinets, then:

                       x1 + 2x2 + x3 = 9    (use up all the wood)
                       2x1 + x2 + x3 = 8    (use up all the labor)
                       x1 + x2 + 2x3 = 7    (use up all the machine time)
                      3x1 + x2 + 2x3 = 11 (use up all the shipping)

How would you solve this on paper? You’d play a small game. Take two equations, scale
one of them, and subtract. A variable disappears. Repeat. That’s elimination. Plug the answer
back in to recover the others. That’s substitution.

    Intuition

  Watch one move so you’ve seen it once. Take equation 2 minus 2× equation 1:

                        (2x1 + x2 + x3 ) −2 ( x1 + 2x2 + x3 ) = 8 − 2 · 9
                        |     {z      }     |      {z      }
                              eq. 2                eq. 1

                                      =⇒ −3x2 − x3 = −10.
  The x1 vanished — that is what “eliminate” literally means. Now I have a smaller
  problem. Repeat that same move on the other equations and eventually one variable is
  alone. The answer pops out: x1 = 2, x2 = 3, x3 = 1.


    Try it yourself

   Plug x1 = 2, x2 = 3, x3 = 1 into all four equations and check they all hold. That tiny
   act of plugging-back-in is something you should always do — not for marks, but to
   feel the answer land. Try it now: 1(2) + 2(3) + 1(1) = 9. ✓



                                               4
Quick question — four equations, three unknowns?
Yes. That’s called overdetermined: more rules than variables. Usually that means no solution
exists at all. The fact that one does exist here is a small lucky accident — the four constraints
happen to agree with each other. Later in the lecture we’ll learn how to check for this directly,
just by reading the matrix — no guesswork needed.

    The one thing to remember

   Solving a linear system is just two moves — eliminate and substitute. Everything
   fancy in this lecture (matrices, Gaussian elimination, row-echelon form) is just those
   two moves, made automatic and made to scale.

3 | The three fates: zero, one, or infinity


    Imagine this

   A treasure is hidden somewhere on a flat island. Two friends each give you a clue.
   Each clue happens to describe a straight line: “the treasure is somewhere on this line.”
   What can possibly happen when you combine their clues?


    AUSE AND TRY to enumerate the possibilities yourself before reading on. Honestly try.
P   . . . Done? You probably found three:




      Lines cross                         Parallel lines                       Same line
  one X marks the spot                    clues disagree                     whole line works


Lines cross (one point of intersection): exactly one X marks the spot.
Parallel lines: the clues never meet, so they actively disagree — nowhere works.
Same line: the second clue is just the first one rephrased; the whole line is fair game.




                                                5
    Aha

   A linear system can never have exactly two answers. Or seven. Or forty-two. It is zero,
   one, or infinity. Those are the only options.
   Why? Suppose, just suppose, you found two distinct answers x1 and x2 . Sketch the
   line connecting them: every point on that line is also a valid answer. (Half-and-half is
   a solution. One-third / two-thirds is a solution. And so on — you can prove this in
   two lines using linearity.)
   So the moment you have two, you have a whole line. If you have a line in 2D, you
   might have a whole plane in 3D. Finite collections of more than one solution are simply not
   on the menu. The math doesn’t allow it.


Higher dimensions, same story
In 3D, each equation describes a plane (a flat sheet floating in space). Three planes can:

• meet at a single point (one solution),
• all share a common line (infinitely many, lying along that line),
• all coincide as one plane (infinitely many, lying all over that plane),
• or fail to share any common point (no solution — maybe two are parallel, or the three
  pairwise intersections form a triangle).

Same three fates: zero, one, or infinity. In n dimensions we just call these surfaces hyperplanes
and stop trying to draw them. The story doesn’t change.

4 | Three is fine. Three thousand is not.


    OU HANDLED the chair-table-cabinet problem in your head. Now imagine 30 equations.
Y   Now 3000. Now a million-row regression problem with a million variables. Hand-
elimination falls apart.




                                               6
    Intuition

  Worse: when you eliminate by hand, you make personal decisions at every step —
  “hmm, let me kill x2 first, the numbers are nicer that way.” That’s fine for one problem.
  Terrible for a million.
  A computer can’t be moody. It needs a recipe that

   • does the same kind of step at every stage (no “creative choices”),
   • is guaranteed to finish (no infinite loops),
   • tells you at the end which of the three fates you’re in.

   That’s what an algorithm is. The rest of the lecture is building one.


So we need two things:

1. A compact way to write the system, so the computer can read it. → matrix.
2. A mechanical procedure to eliminate. → Gaussian elimination.

Both arrive together. The rest of the lecture is just unpacking them.

And just like that, an entire system of equations becomes three letters: Ax = b.

5.1 Matrix arithmetic, in the time it takes to drink coffee
Adding two matrices: line them up and add cell by cell — only works if same shape.
Scalar multiply: multiply every cell by the number.
Multiplying two matrices is the weird one: row-times-column. Hold tight; in a moment we’ll
see why this peculiar rule is actually beautiful.

                                               7
A few facts you’ll use forever:

• ( AB)C = A( BC ) — you can regroup.
• A( B + C ) = AB + AC — you can distribute.
• ( AB)T = B T A T — transpose flips the order.
• AB ̸= BA in general — matrix multiplication is not commutative. Order matters.

    Intuition

   Why does order matter? Think about real-life sequences. Put on socks, then shoes —
   fine. Reverse it (shoes first, then socks) — absurd. Same operations, different order,
   totally different outcome.
   Or: rotate this page by 90◦ clockwise, then translate it 5 cm to the right. Now do the
   same two operations in the opposite order. The page ends up in different places. That
   is AB ̸= BA.


5.2 Why is matrix multiplication defined that strange way?
The row-times-column rule looks invented and arbitrary. It is not. It is forced upon us, the
moment we decide that matrices should represent transformations.

    Intuition

   Picture two photo filters in an editing app.

   • Filter B blurs your photo.
   • Filter A then darkens it.

  Apply them in that order — B first, then A — and the combined effect is one new filter,
  “blur-and-then-darken.” As matrices, that combined filter is the product AB. (Read
  right-to-left: B acts first, A acts second. Yes, the order looks backwards. Yes, everyone
  gets confused at first. Yes, it eventually clicks.)
  The row-times-column rule is exactly what makes ( AB)x = A( Bx) come out true —
  “apply AB to x” equals “first apply B, then apply A.”
   Matrix multiplication isn’t a random recipe. It’s function composition wearing a numerical
   disguise.


5.3 Special matrices to recognise
A few matrices show up so often they get their own names:

• Identity matrix I: ones on the diagonal, zeros elsewhere. The “do nothing” filter. I A =
  AI = A, always.
• Zero matrix: all zeros. The “destroy everything” filter.
• Diagonal matrix: only the diagonal is non-zero. Stretches each axis independently —
  think of stretching a photo’s width and height by different amounts.



                                               8
• Symmetric matrix: A = A T . Mirror image across the diagonal. These are the nicest
  matrices in linear algebra. Lecture 4 will be very fond of them.

6 | Pause. Stare at Ax = b from three angles.


    HIS IS THE MOST important section of the lecture. Read it twice if you have to. We’re
T   going to look at the same equation, Ax = b, from three different angles. Each angle is
correct. Each one answers a different question.
          Switching between these three windows is the single most useful skill in
                                      linear algebra.
Anytime you’re stuck on a problem, ask yourself: which of the three windows would let me see
this clearly?

A toy example we’ll use throughout
To make all three views concrete, we’ll keep coming back to one tiny system:

                     2x1 + x2 = 5
                                                                 
                                                       2 1         5
                                        ⇐⇒          A=       , b=     .
                     x1 + 3x2 = 5                      1 3         5

The answer (you can verify) is x1 = 2, x2 = 1. Now let’s see the same answer through three
windows.

6.1 Window 1: each row is a rule
The first row of A paired with b1 is one equation: 2x1 + x2 = 5. One constraint. One rule the
answer must obey. The second row is another rule: x1 + 3x2 = 5.
Geometrically, each rule is a line in 2D (a plane in 3D, a hyperplane in higher dimensions).
Solving Ax = b means: find the point that obeys every rule simultaneously — the point that lies on
every line / plane at once. For our toy system, that’s the single point where the two lines cross:
(2, 1).

    Intuition

   This is the schoolbook view. It’s the picture you grew up with: “two lines, find where
   they meet.” It generalises perfectly — ten equations means ten hyperplanes, find the
   one point in their intersection.


6.2 Window 2: b is a recipe of column vectors
Now flip your gaze. Don’t look at rows; look at columns. Call the columns of A as
a1 , a2 , . . . , an . A line of arithmetic shows that

                             Ax = x1 a1 + x2 a2 + · · · + xn an .



                                                9
For our toy system: a1 = 21 and a2 = 13 , and we ask: which combination of these two
                                   
                       5
columns equals b = 5 ? The answer ( x1 , x2 ) = (2, 1) says: “two scoops of a1 plus one
scoop of a2 .”
Verify: 2 · 21 + 1 · 13 = 55 . ✓
                     

    Intuition

   x is a shopping list. Each column ai is an ingredient. The component xi tells you how
   much of ingredient i to throw in the pot. Solving Ax = b is asking: can I mix these
   ingredients in some proportion to make exactly b?


The set of everything you can possibly cook up by mixing the columns (any amounts, positive
or negative) is called the column space of A. If b lives inside the column space, the system
has a solution. If not, no recipe works.

6.3 Window 3: A is a machine that moves space
The deepest view. A is a function. You feed it a vector x, it spits out a vector Ax. Then
Ax = b is asking: which input does the machine need, in order to spit out b?
For our toy example, the machine A takes 21 as input and produces 55 as output. We
                                                                            

knew the output. We were asked to figure out the input.

    Aha

  Picture an old-school vending machine. You see the chocolate bar b sitting in the tray.
  Your job: figure out which button code x someone punched in.
  Three questions matter. Can you figure out the code at all? Could multiple codes
  produce the same item? Could it be that no code produces that item?
  All three are properties of the machine itself, not of the equation you’re staring at. The
  machine’s geometry decides everything.
  This is the view we’ll spend the rest of the lecture deepening. It’s also the view
  that 3Blue1Brown’s “Essence of Linear Algebra” series builds everything on. Highly
  recommended watch.

When the parallelogram is flat
Sometimes the two output arrows happen to point along the same line. The parallelogram
collapses to a line segment. The whole 2D plane just got squashed onto a 1D line.
Information was destroyed. Many different inputs now share the same output — imagine
Instagram applying a filter so aggressive that 100 different photos all come out looking

8 | Determinant — how much did space stretch?


    ICK UP where we left off. The unit square has area 1. After we apply A, it becomes a
P   parallelogram with some new area. That new area is exactly the determinant of A.
For our 2 × 2 matrix,
                                 det A = a11 a22 − a12 a21 .
Don’t memorise the formula yet — understand the picture first. The determinant tells you a
single number: by what factor did A scale areas?




            det A ≈ 1.6           det A = 1                          det A = 0
          space stretched        pure rotation                    squashed to a line




                                              12
     Intuition

    Determinant in plain English. The determinant carries two pieces of information at
    once: a magnitude and a sign.
    The magnitude | det A| is the area-scaling factor.

    • | det A| > 1: areas grew. Space was stretched.
    • | det A| < 1: areas shrank, but didn’t collapse.
    • | det A| = 1: areas unchanged.

    The sign of det A tells you orientation.

    • det A > 0: orientation preserved. ı̂ and ȷ̂ are still on the same sides as before.
    • det A < 0: orientation flipped — the world got mirrored. (You can tell because ı̂
      and ȷ̂ swapped sides.)

    Two famous special cases: a pure rotation has det A = +1 (no scaling, no flip). A pure
    reflection has det A = −1 (no scaling, but mirrored).
    And the dramatic one: det A = 0. The parallelogram squashed flat to a line — or even
    to a single point. A whole dimension just died. Like juicing a 3D fruit into a 2D puddle:
    you cannot un-juice it.


The “det = 0” case is the dramatic one. It says all of these in one breath:

• A is not invertible. There is no undo.
• The columns of A are linearly dependent — one is a combination of the others, so they
  don’t really give us anything new.
• Many directions of input space all collapse onto the same output point.

All three statements are the same fact, told in three languages. Get used to translating
between them.

9.1 If A−1 is so beautiful, why don’t we use it?
    Watch out

   Because computing A−1 explicitly is, in practice, almost always the wrong thing to do.
   It’s expensive. Roughly the same cost as solving the system directly — but with a
   much larger constant out front. In real ML, where A might be 10,000 × 10,000, that
   constant matters a lot.
   It’s fragile. If A is “almost singular” (its columns are nearly dependent), the inverse
   becomes a numerical disaster — huge entries that almost cancel, and tiny floating-point
   errors blow up into giant ones. Your answer is garbage and you don’t know it.
   It’s wasteful. You only need x for this particular b. You don’t need the full matrix that
   solves every b.
   Buying a master key for an apartment building when you only want to enter one
   apartment, once. Beautiful in theory. Dumb in practice.
   Industry rule of thumb: if you ever see numpy.linalg.inv(A) @ b in production
   code, replace it with numpy.linalg.solve(A, b). Same answer, faster, more stable.


So we need a different route. Something more like the hand-elimination we already know,
but turned into an algorithm a computer can follow blindly.

                                                        14
10 | Echelon form — elimination, but for a computer


    HE TRICK is the augmented matrix [ A | b ] — glue A and b together into a single grid,
T   separated by a vertical bar. Now anything you’d do to the equations in elimination has
a corresponding operation on the rows of this grid.

10.1 Three legal moves
There are exactly three things you’re allowed to do to rows. Each one corresponds to
something obvious you’d do to the equations themselves.

1. Swap two rows: Ri ↔ R j . (You’re just changing the order in which the equations are
   written. The system is the same.)
2. Scale a row by a non-zero number λ: Ri → λRi . (Multiplying both sides of an equation
   by the same non-zero thing.)
3. Add a multiple of one row to another: Ri → Ri + λR j . (Adding equal things to both sides
   of an equation.)

Each of these is reversible. Each leaves the solution set unchanged. They only change the
appearance of the system, never its meaning. We’re not solving a different problem — we’re
rewriting the same problem until the answer becomes obvious.

    Intuition

   Why must λ ̸= 0 when scaling? Multiplying a row by 0 turns it into “0 = 0” — a
   sentence that’s always true, and so carries no constraint. You’d effectively be throwing
   away one of your equations. The remaining system can pick up extra solutions the
   original didn’t allow. Erasing constraints is not allowed. Reversibility is the rule.


10.2 The strategy: chase a staircase
Use the three moves systematically. Start with column 1: get a non-zero entry at the very top
(a pivot), then use that pivot to kill every entry below it. Move to column 2: find the next
pivot below where the last one was, kill below it. Then column 3. Keep going.
The end result has the pivots marching down-and-right in a staircase pattern. We call this
shape row-echelon form (REF).

                   before                 after — row-echelon form

                                   row-reduce        ■
                                                             ■

A matrix is in REF when (1) all-zero rows sit at the bottom, (2) each pivot is strictly to the

right of the pivot in the row above, and (3) below each pivot, the column is all zeros.

• Last row says: −2x2 = −4, so x2 = 2.
• First row says: x1 + 2(2) = 5, so x1 = 1.

Reading the verdict from REF
Look at the last row. Every coefficient on the left-hand side is zero, and the right-hand side
is a + 1. So the row says, simply, 0 = a + 1. Two cases:

• If a ̸= −1, the row reads “0 = (something non-zero)”. Contradiction. No solution exists.
• If a = −1, the row reads “0 = 0”. Trivially true; gives us no new info, but doesn’t break
  anything either. Solutions exist.

The pivot columns (columns 1, 3, 4) hold the “bound” variables. The non-pivot columns
(2 and 5) hold free variables: free means they can take any value you like, and the bound
variables adjust to compensate.
When a = −1, get the answer. Set the free variables (x2 and x5 ) to 0. Back-substitute,
working from the bottom row up:

• Row 3 says x4 − 2x5 = 1. With x5 = 0: x4 = 1.
• Row 2 says x3 − x4 + 3x5 = −2. Plug in x4 = 1, x5 = 0: x3 = −1.
• Row 1 says x1 − 2x2 + x3 − x4 + x5 = 0. Plug in everything: x1 + 0 − 1 − 1 + 0 = 0, so
  x1 = 2.

So one particular solution is x p = (2, 0, −1, 1, 0).
To capture the wiggle room, we now solve the homogeneous system Ax = 0 (same REF, but
with the right side replaced by zeros). For each free variable in turn, set it to 1, set the other
free variable to 0, and back-substitute. Each pass gives one direction vector along which we
can drift while staying a solution.
First direction. Set x2 = 1, x5 = 0, RHS all zero:

• Row 3: x4 − 2(0) = 0 ⇒ x4 = 0.
• Row 2: x3 − 0 + 0 = 0 ⇒ x3 = 0.


                                                17
• Row 1: x1 − 2(1) + 0 − 0 + 0 = 0 ⇒ x1 = 2.

So n1 = (2, 1, 0, 0, 0).
Second direction. Set x2 = 0, x5 = 1, RHS all zero:

• Row 3: x4 − 2(1) = 0 ⇒ x4 = 2.
• Row 2: x3 − 2 + 3(1) = 0 ⇒ x3 = −1.
• Row 1: x1 − 2(0) + (−1) − 2 + 1 = 0 ⇒ x1 = 2.

So n2 = (2, 0, −1, 2, 1).
Total solution set: x = x p + λ1 n1 + λ2 n2 , for any real λ1 , λ2 .

    The one thing to remember

   Echelon form turns “solve a system” into “look at a staircase”. Where the staircase
   ends tells you the fate (zero / one / infinitely many), and a tiny bit of back-substitution
   gives you the answer.

11 | Reduced row-echelon form — the cleanest version


     EF IS GOOD . Reduced row-echelon form (RREF) is even better. We just keep going:
R
1. Scale every pivot row so the pivot itself is exactly 1.
2. Sweep upwards as well as downwards: kill every entry above each pivot too.

     Now every pivot column is just a standard basis vector — a single 1 surrounded by
     zeros. The answer is sitting on the right side of the bar, ready to read.

    Intuition

   REF is making your bed: sheets tucked, blanket pulled up, you can sleep in it.
   RREF is hospital corners: tucked, smooth, every pillow plumped, ready for inspection.
   Both achieve the same thing — the same solution set — but RREF is canonical. Every
   matrix has exactly one RREF, no matter what order you applied your moves. It is the
   matrix’s fingerprint. (REF is not unique — different roads lead to different REFs of the
   same matrix.)

    Imagine this

   You walk into a Subway. The deal is: every sandwich must include bread and a protein
   (those are the constraints — the “Ax = b” part). Beyond that, you’re free to add any
   combination of toppings (the wiggle room).
   The “particular solution” x p is one specific sandwich the shop pre-makes for you: one
   bread, one protein, no toppings. The “free variables” are the toppings (lettuce, onion,
   jalapeño, sauce, . . . ) you choose to add. Every sandwich on the menu = the pre-made
   base + your topping combination.


Geometrically: the solution set is a flat (a line, or a plane, or higher), anchored at x p , free to
slide around in the directions n1 , n2 , . . . . Imagine pinning a balloon to a wall with one tack:
the balloon can drift in any direction parallel to the wall, but it can’t leave the wall. The tack
is x p . The wall directions are the ni .

What are these direction vectors really?
They are the solutions to the simpler equation Ax = 0 — the inputs that A sends to zero.
The set of all such inputs has a name: the null space of A. So:

             full solution set of Ax = b = any one solution + null space of A.

This is one of those formulas you’ll meet again in stats (linear regression), in optimisation,
and in the very design of neural networks.

    The one thing to remember

  “How many solutions” depends only on how much wiggle room the matrix allows — in
  other words, how big its null space is. Zero wiggle room (only 0 in the null space) →
  one solution (or none). Some wiggle room → infinitely many.

     NCE YOU KNOW how to produce REF/RREF, you’ve also unlocked most of the rest of
O    linear algebra. Three big payoffs.

                                                   19
13.1 Rank: the “real” dimension after compression
    Intuition

   Imagine you have three friends in a group chat. Whenever one says something, the
   other two repeat it. There are three of them, but only one opinion in the room. You’d
   rightly say: “effectively, this is a one-person chat.”
  Rank works the same way. The rank of A is the number of pivots in its echelon form
  — the number of genuinely different directions inside A.
   If A is 3 × 3 but its rank is only 1, then no matter how you mix its columns, you only
   get a 1D line of outputs. The other two columns were “agreeing friends” — repeats (or
   scaled repeats) of the first.


A concrete example
Look at these two matrices:
                                                            
                                1 2 3                   1 2 3
                           B = 4 5 6 ,           C = 4 5 6  .
                                7 8 9                   7 8 10

B has rank 2 (because row3 − 2 row2 + row1 = 0 — one row is hidden in the other two).
C has rank 3 (the rows are genuinely independent). Single digit changed; rank changed;
everything about how these matrices behave is now different.

The headline fact
A square n × n matrix is invertible if and only if its rank is the maximum value, n. Lower
rank means A collapses some directions, and there is no way to undo that. This is the same
statement as “det A ̸= 0” and “columns linearly independent”. Three languages, one fact.

13.2 Computing A−1 using the bed-making trick
Here’s a beautiful side-effect of Gaussian elimination. Stack the identity matrix on the right
of A, forming [ A | I ]. Now row-reduce the left half until it becomes I. Magic: the right half
is now A−1 . (We won’t actually use this much in practice — remember the warning earlier
about not computing inverses — but it’s a great proof that elimination “knows” how to
invert.)

    Intuition

  Why does this work? Each row operation is the same as multiplying by an “elementary”
  matrix Ek on the left. So if a sequence Em · · · E2 E1 turns A into I, then that same
  sequence applied to I produces Em · · · E2 E1 · I = A−1 . The right half is recording the
  inverse for free. Two for the price of one.


13.3 Existence and uniqueness, in three lines
Every question about whether a system has solutions reduces to comparing ranks:
There is at least one solution ⇐⇒ rank( A) = rank([ A | b]).
(Translation: b lies in the column space of A — the columns can be mixed to produce it.)

                                              20
There is exactly one solution ⇐⇒ above holds, and rank( A) equals the number of variables
(n).
(Translation: no free variables, so no wiggle room.)
There are infinitely many solutions ⇐⇒ first condition holds and rank( A) < n (some
variables are free).

    Aha

   One number — rank — answers all three questions.
  That’s the unreasonable power of linear algebra: a single integer summarises the entire
  fate of a system of equations.

    The one thing to remember

   One image to keep: a matrix is a machine that moves space. Solving Ax = b is asking:

   One number to keep: rank — the matrix’s “real” dimension.
