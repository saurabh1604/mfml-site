1     Start here: pick the better line

Given a cloud of points and two candidate lines, the whole of PCA is choosing the line the
points spread out on the most.

Before any definitions, do the thing itself.
Figure 1 shows the same ninety data points twice. Each panel picks one line through the
middle of the cloud and slides every point onto it along the shortest path. That sliding is called
projection, and the coloured dots on the line are the projected points.
Look only at the coloured dots. On the left they are spread out along a long stretch of the line.
On the right they are bunched into a short segment.

      Direction A    spread on the line = 5.45            Direction B   spread on the line = 0.41

             Figure 1: Two candidate lines, same data. Grey threads show each point
             dropping onto the line. How to read it: compare how far apart the coloured
             dots end up. Direction A keeps the points distinct; direction B squashes
             different points on top of each other.

If you had to describe each point by a single number — its position along the line — which line
would you choose?
Direction A, obviously. On direction B, points that were far apart in the original cloud land
almost on top of each other, so that single number cannot tell them apart any more. On
direction A the points stay distinguishable. The number that measures “how far apart the dots
ended up” is the variance, printed in each panel’s title.
You have just done PCA by eye.

     The whole method in one sentence

    PCA searches over every possible direction, computes the variance of the data after
    projecting onto it, and keeps the directions with the highest variance. Everything else in
    this document is the machinery that does that search efficiently, plus the reason it works.

We went from two dimensions to one here. In real work you go from a hundred columns to
five, or from twenty thousand to fifty. The principle does not change; only the bookkeeping
gets bigger.


2     Why variance is the thing we chase

Every model says y = f ( x ) + ε; a column of x that never changes can never explain a change in
y; so “how much does this column change” is the right question, and variance is its answer.


                                                 4
The first section showed what PCA does. This section says why variance — and not some other
quantity — is the thing worth maximising. It is worth a few minutes, because once you see it,
everything from the covariance matrix to the eigenvalue ordering feels inevitable.

2.1     Every model you have ever built has the same skeleton

Whatever the model — linear regression, a decision tree, a neural network — you are writing
the same statement:
                                     y = f ( x ) + ε.                                    (1)
The target y is driven by the features x through some relationship f , plus an error ε for every-
thing you did not, or could not, measure. A linear model makes f a straight line. A tree makes
f a set of splits. A network makes f a stack of transformations. The skeleton is identical.
Now read the equation as a statement about change rather than about values:

            a change in y comes either from a change in x, or from a change in the error.

2.2     The statistic that measures change is variance

Ask what tool measures “how much does this thing change”. Not the mean — the mean says
where things sit, not how much they move. The answer is the variance:

                                           1 N                    1
                                             ∑                    N∑
                                                        2
                              Var( x ) =          xi − µ ,   µ=       xi .                    (2)
                                           N i =1                   i

This is not a new idea dressed up. You have already used exactly this split before, under
different names.
      You have met this split before

  In the analysis of variance you write TSS = SST + SSE: the total variation in y equals
  the part your factors explain plus the part they do not. The F test is the ratio of the two,
  “known variance over unknown variance”. And R2 = 1 − SSE/TSS = SST/TSS is simply
  the share of the variance in y that your features account for.
  All three are the same accounting exercise: split the movement in y into an explained part
  and an unexplained part. Variance is the currency in every one of them.


2.3     The one surprising move: PCA never looks at y

Here is where PCA departs from everything above. It throws y away.
Suppose you are predicting salary from four columns: age, experience, number of certifications,
number of trainings. PCA is handed only the four columns. It never sees a single salary.
Why is that reasonable? Because of the assumption we just wrote down: if x does not change,
y cannot change because of x. So a column with almost no variance carries almost no ability
to explain anything — whatever the target happens to be. You can rank the usefulness of
directions in x before you ever look at y.
In Figure 2 the last two columns are near-constant — almost every employee has zero certifica-
tions and zero trainings. Their variance is essentially zero, so they are dead weight. Dropping
them costs nothing.
That leaves age and experience. These two are a different problem: they are highly correlated.
Older employees generally have more experience, so the two columns largely repeat each other.
A standard feature-selection method would delete one of them. PCA does something better

3      The objects PCA works with — and the three notations



                                                                              6
Four objects: the data X, a direction w, the score z, and the rebuilt point x̃. The only real
difficulty is that three sources write them three different ways.


3.1     The four objects

• x ∈ RD — one data point, with D original features. All N points stacked as rows give the
  data matrix X, size N × D. We always assume X has been centred: the mean of every column
  has been subtracted, so each column of X averages to zero.
• W — a D × M matrix whose columns w1 , . . . , w M are the directions we keep. Each is a unit
  vector, w⊤m wm = 1, and they are mutually perpendicular.
• z ∈ R M — the scores, or code, of a point: z = W⊤ x. Component zm is how far along direction
  wm the point sits. For the whole dataset at once, Z = XW.
• x̃ ∈ RD — the point rebuilt from its scores: x̃ = Wz = WW⊤ x. This lives back in the original
  D-dimensional space, but only on the M-dimensional slice that W spans.

Entry Cjj is the variance of column j. Entry Cjk is the covariance between columns j and k —

      Divide by N , and centre first

      Two details that cost marks.
      One. Equation (3) divides by N, not by N − 1. The N − 1 version is the unbiased estimator
      for a sample, and it is what numpy and R use by default. The slide deck — and therefore
      this course — uses N. Use N.
      Two. The formula N1 X⊤ X is only the covariance if X is already centred. If you skip the
      centring you get the matrix of raw second moments, which is a different matrix with
      different eigenvectors. Centre first, every time.

4      PCA is a constrained optimisation problem

Maximise w⊤ Cw subject to w⊤ w = 1; the Lagrangian turns that into Cw = λw, and the
maximum value is λ itself.

This is the centre of the lecture. Everything before it is motivation and everything after it is
machinery. Six lines of algebra take us from “find the direction of most spread” to “find the top
eigenvector of the covariance matrix”, and they explain why eigenvectors appear in a problem
that never mentioned them.

4.1    Setting up the problem

We want a direction w ∈ RD . Project the centred data onto it:

                                     z = Xw,        zi = x⊤i w.                                (4)

Each zi is one number: where point i lands on the line through w.

Why the direction must have length one. Without a constraint the problem is broken. Double
the length of w and every zi doubles, so the variance quadruples — you could make it as large
as you like by stretching w, without pointing it anywhere better. That is cheating: we care about
the direction, not the length. So we demand

                                           w⊤ w = 1.                                           (5)

Recall from the vectors
                  √ lecture that the length of a vector is the square root of its dot product
with itself, ∥w∥ = w⊤ w. Squaring both sides of ∥w∥ = 1 gives w⊤ w = 1, which is the form
we want because it is polynomial and easy to differentiate.

4.4     The payoff: the maximum variance is the eigenvalue

We know the answer is one of the eigenvectors — but which one? Substitute (12) back into the
objective (8):
                Var(z) = w⊤ Cw = w⊤ (λw) = λ w⊤ w = λ · 1 = λ.                          (13)

      The result to remember

      The variance you get by projecting onto an eigenvector equals its eigenvalue. So to maximise
      the variance, pick the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue. To get the best M directions,
      take the top M eigenvalues.

                      maximum variance = λmax ,                 best direction = wmax .
      This also explains the ordering convention λ1 ≥ λ2 ≥ . . . : it is a ranking of the directions
      by how much of the data’s spread each one captures. And it is why a scree plot works at
      all.

That single line, (13), answers a whole family of questions at once. “What is the maximum
variance of the first principal component?” λ1 . “What is the variance along the second?” λ2 .
No computation required.

      The Lagrange multiplier is the variance

      A question that came up in class: the multiplier λ in (10) is a penalty term — what does it
      have to do with an eigenvalue?
      It turns out they are the same number. In (12) the multiplier appears as the eigenvalue,
      and in (13) the same number turns out to be the maximum variance. So all three readings
      are one quantity:


            Read as                    It is                                      Where you see it

            a Lagrange multiplier      the price of the constraint ∥w∥ = 1        (10)
            an eigenvalue              the stretch factor of C along w            (12)
            a variance                 the spread of the data along w             (13)

      Keep the roles straight, though: the principal component is the eigenvector w — a direction.
      The eigenvalue λ is the variance along it — a number.

5      From the covariance matrix to the directions

C is symmetric, so it always has a full set of perpendicular eigenvectors with real eigenvalues;
their sum is the total variance in the data.

Section 4 reduced PCA to one instruction: find the eigenvectors of C and sort them by eigenvalue.
This section says why that instruction is always answerable, and records two facts we use
constantly.

5.1     The spectral theorem does the heavy lifting

For a general square matrix, eigenvectors can be complex, can fail to be perpendicular, and can
fail to form a basis at all. None of that can happen here, because C is symmetric (Cjk = Ckj —
the covariance of columns j and k does not care about the order).

      What symmetry buys us

      For a real symmetric D × D matrix C:
      • all D eigenvalues are real;
      • eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are automatically perpendicular;
      • there is a full orthonormal basis w1 , . . . , wD of eigenvectors, so C = WΛW⊤ with
        W⊤ W = I and Λ = diag(λ1 , . . . , λ D ).
      And because C is a covariance matrix, every λd ≥ 0: by (13) an eigenvalue is a variance,
      and a variance cannot be negative.

That is why we can always sort λ1 ≥ λ2 ≥ · · · ≥ λ D ≥ 0 and always take the top M: the list
is real, ordered, and complete. It is also why the principal components come out mutually
perpendicular without our asking.

6      The same picture through the SVD

Any matrix factors as X = UΣV⊤ — turn, stretch, turn — and feeding that into C collapses it
straight into the eigendecomposition, with λd = σd2 /N.

The singular value decomposition writes any N × D matrix as

                                                       X = U Σ V⊤ ,                                          (18)

where U and V have orthonormal columns (U⊤ U = I, V⊤ V = I) and Σ is diagonal with the
non-negative singular values σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ . . . on it.

      Turn, stretch, turn

      Every matrix, however complicated it looks, does exactly three things in sequence: it turns
      space, it stretches the axes by fixed amounts, and it turns the result again. That is the whole
      content of the SVD. V⊤ is the first turn, Σ is the stretch, U is the second turn.
      Follow a unit circle through those three moves and you get an ellipse. The ellipse’s axis
      lengths are the singular values, and its axis directions are the columns of U.

6.2     Why anyone bothers with route B

If the two routes agree, why does almost every library use the SVD?

• It never forms C. Building X⊤ X squares the condition number of the problem, which throws

• It links the two views of PCA. The slides derive PCA twice: once by maximising projected
  variance, once by minimising reconstruction error. Equation (23) is the bridge — the same
  U, Σ, V answer both questions. That is why the slides call this “the connection between the
  maximum variance view and the singular value decomposition”.
• It hands you low-rank approximation for free, which is the next section.

7      Low-rank approximation: the Eckart–Young theorem

Truncating the SVD after M terms gives the best possible rank-M version of the data — which is
another way of saying that keeping the top M components loses as little as it is possible to lose.

7.1     The question

subspace — which one sits closest to our actual data?

7.2     The answer

      Eckart–Young

      Take the SVD and simply stop after the top M singular values:

      singular values. No rank-M matrix gets closer. This holds for both the spectral norm and
      the Frobenius norm.

      You are packing a suitcase and can take M items. Eckart–Young says the best strategy is

      when the case is full. What is not obvious — and is what the theorem actually proves —
      is that no clever repacking beats this greedy order. Taking the top M singular directions
      really is optimal, not just sensible.


      This is the second derivation of PCA, in disguise

      The slides say PCA can be derived two ways: by maximising the projected variance, or by
      minimising the average reconstruction error. We did the first in §4. Eckart–Young is the
      second.
      Reconstructing every point from M components gives X̃ = XWW⊤ , a rank-M matrix.
      Asking for the smallest possible N1 ∑i ∥ xi − x̃i ∥2 is exactly problem (24), and its solution is
      the top M singular directions — the same W we found by maximising variance.
      Both routes end at the top eigenvectors of C because of (17): total variance is fixed, so
      keeping the most and losing the least are the same instruction.

    “maximum variance” view.


8      Getting the eigenvectors in practice

The characteristic polynomial works by hand up to 4 × 4 and then stops working for anyone, so
real software iterates — and if you only want the top direction, one very short loop does it.


8.1     The textbook method, and where it breaks

By hand you find eigenvalues by solving the characteristic equation
                                              det(C − λI ) = 0.                                      (26)
For a 2 × 2 matrix this is a quadratic and you use the usual formula. For 3 × 3 it is a cubic, still
solvable in radicals. For 4 × 4 it is a quartic — there is a formula, though nobody enjoys it.
At 5 × 5 the method dies, and not for want of effort.

      Abel–Ruffini: this is not a gap in our cleverness

      The characteristic polynomial of a D × D matrix has degree D. The Abel–Ruffini theorem
      proves that a general polynomial of degree 5 or more has no solution formula in radicals.
      Not “none is known” — none can exist.
      So for D ≥ 5 there is no closed-form route from a matrix to its eigenvalues. Every practical
      eigenvalue algorithm is therefore iterative: start with a guess and improve it until it stops
      changing.

This is worth stating clearly because it changes what “solving” means. In an exam, with a 2 × 2
or 3 × 3 matrix, the characteristic polynomial is the method. In software, on a 500 × 500 matrix,
nothing of the sort is happening — it is running an iteration.

                                                          16
8.2     Do not compute what you will throw away

There is a second practical point. A full eigendecomposition of a D × D matrix costs roughly
O( D3 ) operations and returns all D eigenvectors. But PCA usually wants M = 2 or M = 10 of
them and discards the other several hundred. Paying the full cost and then binning most of the
result is wasteful.
Iterative methods that go directly after the top few eigenvectors are substantially cheaper, and
they are what libraries call when you ask for a fixed number of components.

8.3     Power iteration: the whole idea in one line

The simplest such method, for the single largest eigenvector, is power iteration. Pick a starting
vector x0 that has some component along w1 — that is, w⊤1 x0 ̸= 0, which a randomly chosen
vector satisfies with probability 1 — then repeat

                                                C xk
                                    x k +1 =           ,    k = 0, 1, 2, . . .                  (27)
                                               ∥C xk ∥
Multiply by C; rescale back to unit length; repeat. That is the entire algorithm.

Why it works. Write the starting vector in the eigenbasis — which we may, since the eigenvectors
form a complete orthonormal set:

                                    x0 = c1 w1 + c2 w2 + · · · + c D wD .                       (28)

Each multiplication by C scales the wd part by λd , so after k steps (before rescaling)
                                                         "            k           #
                                                                      λ 2
             C k x0 = c1 λ1k w1 + c2 λ2k w2 + . . . = λ1k c1 w1 + c2      w2 + . . . .          (29)

Provided the top eigenvalue is strictly the largest, λ1 > λ2 , every ratio λd /λ1 is less than one,
so every term except the first shrinks geometrically towards zero. The w1 component takes over.
The normalisation in (27) just stops the numbers overflowing; it does not affect the direction.

      When power iteration crawls

      The convergence speed is governed by the ratio λ2 /λ1 . If the top two eigenvalues are
      close — say λ1 = 5.0 and λ2 = 4.9 — the ratio is 0.98 and progress is glacial. In that
      situation the top two directions carry almost the same variance anyway, so which one
      comes “first” is not a meaningful question. Real libraries use more sophisticated iterations
      that handle this case.

9.1     Long data and wide data

and the standard recipe falls over.

9.2     Why the standard recipe fails

Take X of size 50 × 20,000. Then X⊤ X is 20,000 × 20,000: four hundred million numbers, about
3.2 GB in double precision — for a dataset whose raw form is only 8 MB. And storage is the
smaller problem. Eigendecomposition costs about D3 operations, which here is 8 × 1012 —
hours of computing, to extract directions from fifty data points.

      The tempting wrong fix

      The natural reaction is: “X⊤ X is huge, so let us use XX⊤ instead — it is only 50 × 50.”
      That is the right instinct and the wrong statement. XX⊤ is not the covariance matrix; its
      rows and columns are indexed by data points, not by features, and its eigenvectors live in
      the wrong space entirely — they have N components where we need D.
      The fix below rescues the instinct: use XX⊤ as a stepping stone, then convert its eigenvec-
      tors into the ones you actually want.

Step 1. Centre X (size N × D, with D ≫ N).

   You could not have had more than N components anyway

   Use the training mean and standard deviation

   The µd and σd above are the ones computed from the training data. Not from the test set,
   and not recomputed on the single new point — which would be meaningless anyway,
   since one point has no spread.

   training data, and then applied unchanged to anything new. Recomputing them on test

11.5 Step 5 (optional) — Undo the preparation

x̃∗ is still standardised. To read it in the original units, reverse steps 2 and 1, in that order:
                                       (d)               (d)
                                   xoriginal units = x̃∗ · σd + µd .                                 (43)

That is panel (f) of Figure 11. The points now lie on a line in the original coordinate system —
the same data, compressed to M dimensions and unpacked again.

   Why WW⊤ is not the identity, and why that is the point

   W⊤ W = IM always — the columns are orthonormal, so that product is a small M × M
   identity.
   But WW⊤ is D × D and equals ID only when M = D. When M < D it is a projector: it flat-
   tens any vector onto the M-dimensional slice that W spans, and everything perpendicular
   to that slice is destroyed. That lost perpendicular part is exactly the reconstruction error,
   and its mean squared length, N1 ∑i ∥ xi − x̃i ∥2 , equals λ M+1 + · · · + λ D — the eigenvalues
   you chose not to keep.
   Order matters, and it is a useful check: W⊤ W small and equal to I; WW⊤ big and not equal
   to I.


  Section recap. Centre; standardise; eigendecompose C and keep the top M unit eigenvec-
  tors as W; project with Z = XW. For new points, reuse the training µ, σ and W. To return
  to original units, undo the standardising and add the mean back.

13 Traps, limits, and honest caveats

PCA is linear, unit-sensitive, and blind to your target — knowing when those three things bite
is most of what separates using it well from using it by habit.


13.1 PCA is linear, and only linear

Every component is a straight-line combination z = a1 x1 + · · · + a D x D , so PCA can only find
flat structure. If your data lies on a curve, a spiral or a sphere, no straight direction describes it
well and PCA will report that several components are needed when really there is one curved
one.
There are non-linear alternatives — kernel PCA, and manifold methods such as t-SNE and
UMAP. What matters here is knowing the boundary: PCA’s honest claim is about linear struc-
ture.

                                                  29
13.2 PCA is blind to your target

This is the deepest limitation, and it is the flip side of §2’s most useful property. PCA ranks
directions by variance in x. It has no idea whether those directions have anything to do with y,
and Figure 14 shows a case where it ranks them exactly backwards.

Figure 14 is not a contrived picture; this happens whenever the signal you care about is subtle
and the dominant variation is something else — lighting in images, batch effects in gene
data, overall loudness in audio. If you need a reduction that keeps target information, use a
supervised method (partial least squares, linear discriminant analysis) instead of, or alongside,
PCA.

13.3 Units decide the answer

Covered in §11 and shown in Figure 12: because variance carries units, changing a column’s
unit changes the components. Always make a deliberate decision about standardising.

13.4 Outliers pull the components around

Variance is a sum of squared deviations, so a single far-away point contributes enormously. One
mis-keyed value can swing the first component towards itself. Look at the data before running
PCA, and treat a component that seems to be describing one point as a warning sign.

13.5 The sign of a component is arbitrary

If w satisfies Cw = λw then so does −w, with the same eigenvalue. Both are equally valid
answers, and different software will hand you different signs for the same dataset. Nothing is
wrong — the scores simply flip sign with the vector, and every variance is unchanged. Do not
read meaning into whether a loading is +0.7 or −0.7 on its own; read the pattern of signs within
a component.


                                                       30
13.6 Components are new features, not old ones

Dropping components is not the same as dropping columns. After PCA you are no longer
measuring age or experience — you are measuring mixtures. Every original column still
contributes to every component. If your client needs a model stated in terms of the things they
actually measured, PCA gives you accuracy at the cost of one extra explanatory step (§10’s
loadings).

  Section recap. PCA finds linear structure only; it ranks by variance in x and can discard
  exactly the direction that predicts y; it is sensitive to units and to outliers; component signs
  are arbitrary; and its outputs are mixtures, not original columns.

Solution 5

How to attack this type. Size questions first — the covariance matrix is always (number
of features) × (number of features), whatever N is. The derivation in (b) is one move:
left-multiply the eigen-equation by X and regroup the brackets. In (c) remember that a
proportionality is not an answer until you normalise, and remember the rank argument —
centring costs one degree of freedom.
(a) Σ is D × D = 12,000 × 12,000, which is 1.44 × 108 entries. At 8 bytes each:
                      1.44 × 108 × 8 = 1.152 × 109 bytes ≈ 1.15 GB.
Two problems. First, storage — over a gigabyte for a raw dataset of a few megabytes.
Second, and worse, eigendecomposition costs about O( D3 ) operations, which here is
≈ 1.7 × 1012 — hours of computation to extract structure from only 40 data points.
