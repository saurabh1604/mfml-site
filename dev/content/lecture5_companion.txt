Part 0 | Decomposition, Explained Easily
THE BIG PICTURE — WHY TAKE A PERFECTLY GOOD MATRIX APART

You already know how to decompose things. The number 12 is hard to reason about until you
 √ it as 2 × 2 × 3. Suddenly everything is obvious: it is even, divisible by 3, its square root is
write
2 3. You did not change the number — you revealed what it was made of.
Matrix decomposition is exactly this idea, applied to transformations. A matrix is not
just a grid of numbers — it is a verb. It takes vectors and rotates, stretches, squishes, or shears
them. A raw matrix mixes all those actions into one tangled bundle. Decomposition factors that
bundle into a short sequence of pure, simple moves you can actually picture.



                                                                            indivisible prime
                                12           =      2        2     3
                                                                            building blocks




                           A (tangled)       =      rotate   · stretch   · rotate


  Same trick, two worlds. Factoring a number exposes its primes; factoring a matrix exposes the handful of pure
                                        rotations and stretches hiding inside it.



   KEY IDEA

   The one sentence to remember. A matrix decomposition rewrites A as a product of
   matrices that each do one easy thing — usually rotate, scale along axes, and rotate
   back. The product still equals A; it is just written in a language you can read.

A matrix is a transformation — watch it move

Feed a matrix the same little square grid and a unit circle, and look at what comes out. That
output is the matrix’s personality.



                  input               pure stretch           pure rotation           shear (mix)




  Three pure moves and one mixed one. Decomposition’s whole job is to take that messy shear and rewrite it as

The ladder of generality — our roadmap

Here is the punchline of the entire lecture in one image. The three decompositions are not three
unrelated tricks; they are the same idea applied to progressively harder matrices. Each
rung relaxes a restriction the previous one needed.



                             SVD — any matrix at all (rectangular, any rank)


                           Eigen — any square matrix with enough eigenvectors

                                                   Spectral
                                             symmetric matrices only
                                   A = Q Λ QT (the cleanest, most perfect case)


                                                     A = P DP −1

                                                     A = U ΣV T

  We start in the small teal box — the perfect, symmetric world where everything is orthogonal — and earn our
  way outward. Spectral is a special case of eigen; eigen’s idea, generalized to handle every matrix, becomes SVD.



Why climb in this order? Because each step teaches one new lesson:

 1. Spectral shows the dream scenario: perpendicular axes, real scalings, no distortion — a matrix
    that is pure stretch in disguise.
 2. Eigen asks: what if the natural axes are not perpendicular? We keep the “scale along special
    directions” idea but pay a price (the inverse P −1 instead of a clean transpose).
 3. SVD asks the boldest question: what if the matrix is not even square, so “eigenvectors” do
    not exist? The fix is elegant — use two sets of perpendicular axes, one for input and one for
    output. That single idea works for every matrix in existence.




                                                         2
   WHY IT MATTERS

   This is not abstract bookkeeping. Spectral decomposition is the engine of PCA and quadratic
   optimization; SVD powers image & data compression, recommender systems, latent
   semantic analysis, noise removal, and the pseudo-inverse behind least-squares regres-
   sion. Master the geometry here and those applications stop being magic.




Part 1 | Spectral Decomposition
THE PERFECT CASE — SYMMETRIC MATRICES, FLAWLESS GEOMETRY, EVERYTHING
PERPENDICULAR

We begin at the top of the food chain: the matrices that behave perfectly. A symmetric matrix
(one where A = AT ) is secretly nothing but a pure stretch along a set of perpendicular axes. No
twist, no shear, no surprises. Understand this case and the rest of the lecture is just “what breaks
when we lose this perfection?”

   INTUITION

   The trampoline. Picture a circular trampoline. Grab it at two perpendicular points and
   stretch: one axis grows by a factor λ1 , the perpendicular axis by λ2 . The circle becomes an
   ellipse whose axes line up perfectly with the directions you pulled. That is exactly what a
   symmetric matrix does. The pull-directions are its eigenvectors (they stay perpendicular
   and never rotate); the stretch-factors are its eigenvalues λi .



The defining equation: a direction the matrix cannot rotate

An eigenvector q is a stubborn direction: when the matrix acts on it, it is not knocked off its line
— it only gets longer or shorter (or flipped):

            Aq = λq      ⇐⇒     “A acts on q as plain multiplication by the number λ.”

Every other vector gets rotated and stretched in a tangled way. Eigenvectors are the rare directions
where the tangle disappears. They are the natural axes of the transformation.


Stacking the eigenvectors: A = QΛQT

For a symmetric matrix the Spectral Theorem guarantees a full set of eigenvectors that are
orthonormal (perpendicular, unit length). Stack them as columns of Q and put the eigenvalues
on the diagonal of Λ:
                                    A = Q Λ QT        QT Q = I.

Read right-to-left: QT rotates into the eigen-axes, Λ scales each axis by its eigenvalue, Q rotates
back. Because Q is orthogonal, “undo the rotation” is just the transpose QT — no inverse needed.
This clean transpose is the luxury only symmetric matrices enjoy.

The most beautiful reading: a sum of projections

Multiply QΛQT out and it unpacks into a weighted sum of simple rank-1 pieces:

                             A = QΛQT = λ1 q1 q1T + λ2 q2 q2T + · · · + λn qn qnT .

Each block qi qiT is a projector — “flatten everything onto the line through qi .” So a symmetric
matrix is literally “project onto axis 1 and amplify by λ1 , plus project onto axis 2 and amplify by
λ2 , . . . ” The eigenvalues are an honest importance ranking. (Hold onto this — it is the seed of
SVD and compression in Part 4.)

Part 2 | Eigen Decomposition
ANY SQUARE MATRIX — PERPENDICULARITY GONE, THE IDEA SURVIVES

Now we leave the perfect symmetric world. A general square matrix still has eigenvectors — stub-
born directions it only scales — but they are no longer perpendicular. The “stretch along special
axes” story is intact; we just lose the orthogonal luxury and pay with an honest matrix inverse.

   INTUITION

   Speak the matrix’s native language. A general matrix looks chaotic in the standard x, y
   grid because it both rotates and stretches. But it has its own private coordinate system —
   its eigenvectors — in which it does nothing but scale. The trick A = P DP −1 is a translator:
   P −1 translates your vector into the matrix’s native eigen-language, D does the embarrassingly
   simple scaling there, and P translates back.



The decomposition: A = P DP −1

Collect the (non-orthogonal) eigenvectors as columns of P and the eigenvalues on D. From AP =
P D with P invertible:

                   A = P D P −1              P = [ p1 p2 · · · pn ],     D = diag(λ1 , . . . , λn ).

   KEY IDEA

   The crucial difference from Part 1. Spectral gave A = QΛQT — the transpose, because
   orthonormal columns satisfy Q−1 = QT . Here the columns of P are not orthonormal, so
   P −1 ̸= P T and we must compute a real inverse. Spectral decomposition is just the lucky case
   of eigen decomposition where P happens to be orthogonal. Same machine, one restriction
   removed.




                   your vector     P −1    eigen-coords     D     scale axes        P   result Ax
                   standard x, y          native language       ×λ1 , ×λ2 , . . .       back to x, y


  Eigen decomposition = a change of language. The hard transformation A becomes the trivial diagonal D once
                 you speak the matrix’s own eigen-coordinates. Read A = P DP −1 right-to-left.

Part 3 | Singular Value Decomposition
EVERY MATRIX — SQUARE OR RECTANGULAR, ANY RANK — FACTORS INTO ROTATE
· STRETCH · ROTATE

The boldest question in the lecture. Eigen decomposition needed a square matrix so input and
output lived in the same space. But a 3 × 2 matrix eats 2-D vectors and outputs 3-D ones —
“eigenvector” is meaningless, since Av cannot even be parallel to v. SVD’s answer is one of the most
elegant ideas in all of linear algebra.

   INTUITION

   The sphere always becomes an ellipsoid. Feed any matrix the unit sphere of inputs;
   the output is always a perfect ellipsoid. SVD finds the perpendicular input directions (vi )
   mapped to the perpendicular axes of that ellipsoid (ui ), and the axis lengths are the singular
   values σi . Eigen asked “which directions don’t rotate?” — too much for a rectangular
   matrix. SVD asks the always-answerable question: “which perpendicular input frame lands
   on a perpendicular output frame?”



The cleverness: use two orthonormal frames, not one

Eigen reused a single set of axes for input and output (hence “square”). SVD uses two separate
orthonormal frames — input V and output U — with a pure diagonal stretch Σ bridging them:

                            V (n × n, orthonormal) : input axes (right-singular vectors)
                       T
           A = U ΣV         Σ (m × n, diagonal ≥ 0) : stretch factors σi
                            U (m × m, orthonormal) : output axes (left-singular vectors)

Right-to-left: V T rotates the input so special directions align with the axes, Σ stretches each axis
by σi (and pads dimensions with zeros), U rotates into the final pose.

   The definitive SVD picture. A circle is rotated by V T until special directions sit on the axes, stretched by Σ
     into an ellipse, then rotated by U into its final orientation. The singular values σ1 ≥ σ2 are the ellipse’s
                                                   semi-axis lengths.




How to find U, Σ, V — the symmetric-matrix bridge

We reduce SVD to Part 1. Although A may be rectangular, both AT A and AAT are square and
symmetric:

              AT A = V ΣT Σ V T ⇒ V = eigenvectors of AT A, σi2 = its eigenvalues

                             AAT = U ΣΣT U T ⇒ U = eigenvectors of AAT
And once you have V and the σi , each output axis is the normalized image of an input axis (from
AV = U Σ):
                                                       ui = σ1i A vi .

   KEY IDEA
                                                                                   √
  Recipe. (1) Spectrally decompose the smaller of AT A or AAT . (2) σi = λi (drop zeros).
  (3) Its eigenvectors give V (or U ). (4) Get the other family free via ui = σ1i Avi . (5) Assemble
  A = U ΣV T , padding Σ with zeros to match A’s shape.

Part 4 | Low-Rank Approximation & the Spectral Norm
THE PAYOFF — WHY SVD IS THE MATHEMATICS BEHIND COMPRESSION

Remember how a symmetric matrix split into importance-ranked projection layers? SVD does the
same for every matrix — and this is where it earns its place as arguably the most useful factorization
in applied mathematics.


A matrix is a stack of rank-1 layers

Multiplying U ΣV T out, Σ pairs each ui with its vi and scales by σi . The matrix becomes a sum of
r (= rank) rank-1 layers, sorted loudest first:
                             r
                             X
                        A=         σi ui viT = σ1 u1 v1T + σ2 u2 v2T + · · · + σr ur vrT .
                                               | {z } | {z }                   | {z }
                             i=1
                                                loudest      quieter            faintest

Because σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ · · · , the first few layers carry almost all the energy. Truncate at k terms for
                                   = ki=1 σi ui viT .
                                      P
the best rank-k summary: A(k)b

   KEY IDEA

   Eckart–Young theorem. Of all rank-k matrices, this truncated SVD is provably the closest
   to A (in both spectral and Frobenius norms). You cannot do better with rank k, and the
   error is dictated exactly by the singular values you discarded.

   WHY IT MATTERS

   A 1000 × 1000 image is a million numbers. If its singular values decay fast (they usually

   KEY IDEA

   The thirty-second recap. A matrix is a transformation. Decomposition rewrites it as ro-
   tate → stretch → rotate. When the matrix is symmetric, the two rotations are transposes
   of each other and the axes are perpendicular (spectral). For a general square matrix the
   axes tilt, so we swap the transpose for an inverse (eigen). For any matrix at all, we use two
   independent perpendicular frames joined by a non-negative stretch (SVD) — and sorting
   that stretch by size hands us compression, denoising, and the spectral norm for free.
