## 2 · Exemplar — the level of writing we want (Unit 16, CBOW)
Read this once and match its depth and flow in every section. (Numbers are pre-verified.)

> **Imagine this.** The teacher writes on the board: *"Every morning my grandfather drinks a hot cup of ____."* Nobody asks what the missing word is. "Chai," says the whole class. You did not look anything up. The words around the blank — *drinks, hot, cup* — each pointed somewhere, and together they pointed at one word.
>
> That game — fill in the blank from the words around it — is the first way word2vec learns. It is called **CBOW**, the *continuous bag of words*.
>
> Take the tiny sentence *we drink chai daily* and hide the middle word. The neighbours inside the window are *drink* and *daily*. The machine plays in four moves.
>
> **1. Look up the neighbours.** Every word has an *input vector*, a row of the input table. Here drink = (1, 0) and daily = (0, 1).
> **2. Average them.** h = ((1, 0) + (0, 1)) / 2 = (0.5, 0.5). This single point is the neighbours' combined opinion. Averaging throws away their order — that is the "bag" in the name. The vectors are dense numbers, not counts — that is the "continuous".
> **3. Score every word in the vocabulary.** Every word also has an *output vector*. Its score is the dot product with h (Unit 3): we 0, drink 0.5, chai 1, daily 0.5, cricket −1.
> **4. Turn scores into a guess.** Softmax (Unit 14) gives 0.135, 0.223, **0.368**, 0.223, 0.050. The true word is chai, so the surprise is −ln 0.368 ≈ 1.00.
>
> **How it learns.** Unit 15 told us the error at the output of a softmax is just *prediction − truth*: (0.135, 0.223, **−0.632**, 0.223, 0.050). Chai's output vector gets pulled toward h; every other word's output vector gets pushed away from h, each in proportion to how much probability it wrongly took. The blame then flows back into h and, because h was an average of two neighbours, it is **shared equally** between drink and daily. After one step (η = 1) the model gives chai 0.594 instead of 0.368, and the surprise drops from 1.00 to 0.52.
>
> **Why does this learn meaning?** Every time chai is the blank, its output vector is pulled toward the average of the neighbours it had. Coffee is the blank in the same kinds of sentences, so it is pulled toward the same places. Two words pulled toward the same places end up in the same place. Nobody told the machine that chai and coffee are drinks — the blanks did.
>
> **Trap:** the vectors we keep are not the answers to the blanks. After training we throw the game away and keep the *input table* — the neighbours' vectors. The game was only the exam that forced the table to be good.
>
> *In one sentence:* CBOW averages the neighbours' vectors, scores every word against that average, and nudges the vectors until the true middle word wins — a committee of neighbours filling in the blank.

Notice: a real question, one anchor picture (the blank on the board / the committee), mechanism in numbered moves with tiny numbers, the link to Units 3/14/15, the "why", the trap, the memory hook.

### Act II — Guessing the next word by counting
**s2 · A sentence is a chain of guesses.** New framing. Chain rule of probability: P(w₁…wₙ) = P(w₁)·P(w₂|w₁)·P(w₃|w₁w₂)… — "a sentence is a string of guesses, each allowed to use everything before it." Too much history to count → **Markov assumption**: keep only the last N−1 words ("a guesser with a short memory"; bigram N = 2, trigram N = 3). Counting = maximum likelihood (Unit 14's coin: heads/tosses). Corpus with sentence marks:
`<s> I drink chai </s>` · `<s> I drink coffee </s>` · `<s> I drink chai </s>` · `<s> I play cricket </s>`.
P(I|<s>) = 4/4, P(drink|I) = 3/4, P(chai|drink) = 2/3, P(</s>|chai) = 2/2 ⇒ P(<s> I drink chai </s>) = 1·¾·⅔·1 = **1/2**. Generating text = rolling a loaded die from the current word's row (Shannon's game). Why the `</s>` matters: it lets the model decide when a sentence ends, and makes probabilities of all sentences add to 1. Trap: a bigram model has no idea what came two words back ("the train to Delhi from platform four is ___" — it sees only "is"). Widget: **w-bigram** (keep; update to `<s>`/`</s>`; show the chain of guesses as a highlighted path through the table with the product building up; add a "memory" selector bigram/trigram that highlights which previous words the guesser may use; keep "write a sentence").

**s3 · Never seen is not impossible: smoothing.** Anchor: a new chaiwala opens in town — you have never bought from him, but that does not make it impossible. Add-one ("one free ticket to every pair"): P(chai|drink) = (2+1)/(3+7) = **0.3**, P(cricket|drink) = 1/10 = **0.1** (V = 7 next-word types: I, drink, play, chai, coffee, cricket, </s>). **Trap:** with a real vocabulary add-one steals almost everything from what you saw: with V = 50 000, P(chai|drink) = 3/50 003 ≈ **0.00006** — the two real sightings are drowned. Fixes: add-k with small k; **interpolation** — ask three advisers (trigram, bigram, unigram) and blend with weights that add to 1; **backoff** — if the specialist has never seen it, ask the generalist. Worked interpolation (λ = 0.8 bigram + 0.2 unigram; 16 tokens after the `<s>` marks: I 4, drink 3, play 1, chai 2, coffee 1, cricket 1, </s> 4): P(chai|drink) = 0.8·⅔ + 0.2·2/16 = **0.5583**, P(cricket|drink) = 0.8·0 + 0.2·1/16 = **0.0125**. Widget (new, 2-D): **w-smooth** — the "drink" row as bars under MLE / add-one / add-k (k slider 0.01–1) / interpolation (λ slider); a V slider (7 → 50 000) showing add-one flattening the row; a "mass moved" meter (how much probability was taken from seen words and given to unseen ones); rows always sum to 1 (test it).

### Act IV — Stop counting, start predicting: word2vec
**s8 · From counting to predicting: the word2vec idea.** Anchor: *don't write down the gossip — train a guesser, then read its mind.* The count table is huge and has to be rebuilt when new text arrives; word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013) slides a window over the text and plays a guessing game at every position. Two games: **fill in the blank** (CBOW: neighbours → middle word) and **guess the neighbours** (skip-gram: middle word → each neighbour). Both use the same small network: one-hot word → **input table** W_in (V × d) → a hidden list of d numbers → **output table** W_out (d × V) → softmax over the whole vocabulary. After training, **throw the game away and keep the input table** — its rows are the word vectors. Counting examples: a 6-word sentence with window C = 2 gives CBOW **6** examples (one per position) but skip-gram **18** (centre, neighbour) pairs (2+3+4+4+3+2). Widget: `<!--@W2V window-->` (by 16-w2v): the window slides; each position emits one CBOW example or up to 2C skip-gram pairs; counters. Also draw (static SVG) the shared network picture with shapes V → d → V.

**s9 · CBOW: the committee fills in the blank.** Use the exemplar in `R22-REWORK.md §2` as the basis (expand it into the full rhythm). Toy model (V = 5: we, drink, chai, daily, cricket; d = 2). Input vectors: we (0,1), drink (1,0), chai (1,1), daily (0,1), cricket (−1,0). Output vectors: we (0,0), drink (1,0), chai (1,1), daily (0,1), cricket (−1,−1). Sentence *we drink chai daily*, window 1, centre chai, context {drink, daily}.
h = (0.5, 0.5); scores (0, 0.5, 1, 0.5, −1); softmax **(0.1354, 0.2233, 0.3682, 0.2233, 0.0498)**; loss −ln 0.3682 = **0.9993**; error e = p − y = (0.1354, 0.2233, −0.6318, 0.2233, 0.0498); output update u_w ← u_w − η e_w h; blame for h: EH = Σ e_w u_w = **(−0.4584, −0.4584)**, shared equally: each context vector ← v − η·EH/2. **s13 · Why it works: counting and predicting meet.** Anchor: two strangers who shop at the same stores end up carrying the same bags — words that keep the same company receive the same pulls, so their vectors converge. ### Act III — Order and depth: the transformer block
**s8 · Where is each word? Three ways to add position.** Keep order-blindness (**w-shuffle**: shuffle the words, each word's answer moves with it — attention treats a sentence as a bag). Compare three fixes side by side (table + widget tabs): **learned position vectors** (a lookup row per position; simple; cannot go past the longest training length), **sinusoidal** (clock hands at different speeds; d = 4 values: pos 1 = (0.8415, 0.5403, 0.0100, 1.0000), pos 2 = (0.9093, −0.4161, 0.0200, 0.9998), pos 3 = (0.1411, −0.9900, 0.0300, 0.9996); key property: PE(1)·PE(2) = PE(2)·PE(3) = **1.5403** — the dot product depends only on the distance), **rotary / RoPE** (rotate q and k by angles proportional to position; θ = 30°, q = k = (1, 0): positions (3,1), (5,3), (2,0) all give q·k = cos 60° = **0.5** — only the gap matters). ### Act IV — How transformers learn, and the three families
**s10 · No peeking: the causal mask, and learning every word at once.** Keep the mask worked example (masked rows **(1, 0, 0)**, **(0.196, 0.804, 0)**, **(0.248, 0.248, 0.503)**; outputs (2, 0), (0.391, 1.609), (1, 1); **w-mask**). New and important: because each position may look only to its left, **one forward pass trains every next-word guess in the sentence at once** — an n-word sentence gives n − 1 (or n) losses in parallel, where an RNN had to walk word by word. Anchor: the whole class answers at once, each student covering the answers to their right.

**s11 · Three families: BERT, GPT and the encoder–decoder.** New. **Encoder-only (BERT, 2018)**: no mask, every word sees both sides; trained by **masked-word guessing** — pick 15% of tokens (of 512 that is **76.8 ≈ 77**), of those 80% become [MASK], 10% a random word, 10% stay (≈ 61.4 / 7.7 / 7.7); good for understanding (classification, search, tagging). **Decoder-only (GPT)**: causal mask, next-word prediction on every position (511 guesses from a 512-token text vs BERT's ~77 — more learning signal per text); good for writing. **Encoder–decoder (the original Transformer, T5)**: encoder reads both ways, decoder writes with a causal mask and **cross-attention** — its queries come from the decoder, keys and values from the encoder: Act I's interpreter, rebuilt from attention alone. Comparison table (what each position may see, training game, typical use, examples). Widget (new): **w-families** — pick a family; the attention-permission grid (who may look at whom) and, under each token, whether it produces a loss; a toggle to run the masking game on a sample sentence.
