Machines with Memory

You read a sentence one word at a time, and you remember what came before. A network can do the same. It keeps a small running note — a list of numbers — and updates it after every word, using the same weights each time. Training it sends the blame back through every word, and something surprising happens: the blame gets multiplied by the same matrix again and again. So its eigenvalues (Unit 4) decide everything — below 1 the memory fades away, above 1 it blows up. Then we meet the fix: gates, and an express lane for memory. Last, a machine that translates a whole sentence — and the one bottleneck that sets up Unit 18.

Why order matters

Imagine this

Two newspaper headlines. "Dog bites man." Nobody cares. "Man bites dog." Front page!

Both use the same three words. Only the order is different — and the order is the whole story. A machine that reads must notice the order.

In Unit 16 every word became a list of numbers. So how do we turn a whole sentence into numbers? Here are three ideas. The first two fail, and how they fail tells us what we need.

Idea 1 · Throw the words in a bag. Add up the word vectors, or just count the words. People call this a bag of words. It is quick. But "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" have exactly the same bag: one "dog", one "bites", one "man". The sums are the same. The order is lost.

Idea 2 · Look at the last few words. Keep a small window, say the last 3 words, like the word-pair counts of Unit 16. This keeps local order. But it forgets everything older. Take the announcement "The train to Delhi from platform four is late". When "late" arrives, a 3-word window holds only "four is late". What is late? The word "train" has already dropped out.

Idea 3 · Read one word at a time and keep a running note. Think of a shopkeeper with a notebook. After each customer, he updates one line. But a plain running total does not care about order either: 5 + 3 is the same as 3 + 5. So we add one twist. Before writing in the new word, we first change the old note a little — turn it, shrink it — with a matrix. Then an old word has been turned many times and a new word only once. The order leaves its mark.

A tiny example. Give each word a toy vector (hand-made, not trained): \(\text{dog}=(1,0)\), \(\text{man}=(0,1)\), \(\text{bites}=(1,1)\). The note starts at \((0,0)\). At each word we do two things: turn the note a quarter turn with \(W=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\ 1&0\end{pmatrix}\), then add the word. A quarter turn sends \((a,b)\) to \((-b,a)\).

  • dog bites man: after "dog" the note is \((1,0)\). Turn it: \((0,1)\); add "bites": \((1,2)\). Turn it: \((-2,1)\); add "man": \((-2,2)\).
  • man bites dog: after "man", \((0,1)\). Turn: \((-1,0)\); add "bites": \((0,1)\). Turn: \((-1,0)\); add "dog": \((0,0)\).

Two different notes: \((-2,2)\) and \((0,0)\). Without the turn, both would end at the plain sum \((2,2)\). The turn is what makes the note remember order.

The recurrent cell: one small machine, used again and again

Imagine this

You sit in a long lecture with one small card for notes. After each new sentence from the teacher, you rewrite the card: keep some of what was there, add the new point, and squeeze it so it still fits on the card.

You use the same habit every time. That habit is the recurrent cell. The card is its memory.

Now we make the running note a real network part. The note is a list of numbers called the hidden state, \(\mathbf h_t\) — "the note after word \(t\)". At each word the cell does one small job:

\[\mathbf h_t=\tanh\big(W\,\mathbf h_{t-1}+U\,\mathbf x_t+\mathbf b\big).\]

  • \(W\mathbf h_{t-1}\): carry the old note over, changed by the matrix \(W\).
  • \(U\mathbf x_t\): write in the new word \(\mathbf x_t\), mixed by the matrix \(U\).
  • \(\mathbf b\): a small shift, as in every layer of Unit 15.
  • \(\tanh\): squeeze every number to between −1 and 1, so the note never overflows the card. It is the bend of Unit 15.

The most important fact: \(W\), \(U\) and \(\mathbf b\) are the same at every word. The cell does not grow a new set of weights for word 7. One small machine reads a sentence of any length.

A tiny example with one number. Let the note be a single number. Take \(w=0.5\), \(u=1\), \(b=0\), start with \(h_0=0\), and feed three inputs \(x=(1,0,0)\) — one real word, then two blanks.

\[\begin{aligned}h_1&=\tanh(0.5\cdot0+1\cdot1)=\tanh(1)\approx0.7616\\ h_2&=\tanh(0.5\cdot0.7616+0)=\tanh(0.3808)\approx0.3634\\ h_3&=\tanh(0.5\cdot0.3634+0)=\tanh(0.1817)\approx0.1797\end{aligned}\]

Watch the first word fade. Each step keeps half of the note (\(w=0.5\)), and tanh squeezes it a little more. After three steps only 0.18 is left of the 0.76. Keep this in mind — it is the seed of the whole unit.

1
The top \(e^{z}-e^{-z}\) is always smaller in size than the bottom \(e^{z}+e^{-z}\), because \(e^{\pm z}>0\). So \(|\tanh z|<1\). This is why the note never overflows the card: every entry of \(\mathbf h_t\) stays inside \((-1,1)\).
2

Unrolling: the loop is a chain of twins

Imagine this

A dabbawala carries a lunch box from station to station. Film him, and print one photo at every station. Lay the photos in a row. It looks like a long chain of dabbawalas passing the box along.

But it is one man, doing the same job again and again. The chain is just the loop, laid out in time.

We often draw the recurrent cell as one box with a loop arrow: its note goes out and comes straight back in. That is compact, but hard to reason about. So we unroll it: draw one copy of the box for every word, and turn the loop into an arrow from each copy to the next.

For a three-word sentence the unrolled picture is

\[\mathbf h_0\xrightarrow{\ \text{cell}\ }\mathbf h_1\xrightarrow{\ \text{cell}\ }\mathbf h_2\xrightarrow{\ \text{cell}\ }\mathbf h_3,\]

with the words \(\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_2,\mathbf x_3\) coming in from below. Look at it and you see an old friend: a deep network from Unit 15, three layers deep. There is one difference. In Unit 15 every layer had its own matrix. Here every "layer" uses the same \(W\), \(U\) and \(\mathbf b\). We say the weights are shared (or tied).

Count the numbers it learns. Say the note has \(h\) numbers and each word has \(d\). Then \(W\) is \(h\times h\), \(U\) is \(h\times d\) and \(\mathbf b\) has \(h\) entries, so

\[\text{numbers to learn}=h\cdot h+h\cdot d+h=h(h+d)+h.\]

With \(h=4\) and \(d=3\): \(16+12+4=32\). For a 3-word sentence: 32. For a 3000-word book: still 32. The unrolled chain gets longer; the list of weights does not.

Prove it · the weight count does not depend on the length

Claim. A recurrent cell with note size \(h\) and word size \(d\) learns \(h(h+d)+h\) numbers, whatever the number of words \(T\). Without sharing, the \(T\)-step chain would need \(T\) times as many.

1
Shapes: \(W\mathbf h_{t-1}\) must give \(h\) numbers from \(h\) numbers, so \(W\) is \(h\times h\). \(U\mathbf x_t\) gives \(h\) numbers from \(d\), so \(U\) is \(h\times d\). \(\mathbf b\) has \(h\). Total \(h^2+hd+h=h(h+d)+h\). The shape rule of Unit 15: rows for where the numbers go, columns for where they come from.
2
Unrolling makes \(T\) copies, but every copy points at the same \(W,U,\mathbf b\). The list of distinct numbers is the same list. If each step had its own, we would have \(T\big(h(h+d)+h\big)\). ∎ Example: \(h=4\), \(d=3\): 32 numbers shared, or \(320\) for 10 unshared steps. Sharing is also what lets the cell read a sentence longer than any it saw in training.

In one sentence: Unrolled, a recurrent cell is a deep network as long as the sentence whose layers are twins, so it learns only \(h(h+d)+h\) numbers however long the sentence is.

Many shapes of the same idea

Imagine this

One pen can write very different things: a caption under a photo, a single word on a review ("great!"), a label on each item of a list, or a full letter in another language.

The recurrent cell is that pen. What changes is only where the inputs go in and where we read the answers out.

So far the cell only reads. To make it say something, we add a read-out layer. At any word \(t\) we can turn the note into scores and then into probabilities with softmax (Unit 14):

\[\hat{\mathbf y}_t=\operatorname{softmax}\big(V\,\mathbf h_t+\mathbf c\big).\]

Where we put inputs and read-outs gives the four classic shapes:

  • Many → one. Read a whole review, then read out once at the end: happy or unhappy. (Sentiment.)
  • One → many. Give it one input — a photo's vector — and let it write a caption word by word.
  • Many → many, same length. Read out at every word: tag each word as a noun, a verb or other.
  • Many → many, different length. Read a whole English sentence, then write a Hindi one. This needs two cells, and it is §10.

And one favourite: the language model. At every word, guess the next word. This is the neural language model of Unit 16, but now the note carries all earlier words, not just the last two.

A tiny example. After reading "the train to", the read-out gives the scores Delhi 2, Mumbai 1, late 0.5, the −1. Softmax turns them into probabilities: \(e^2\approx7.389\), \(e^1\approx2.718\), \(e^{0.5}\approx1.649\), \(e^{-1}\approx0.368\), total \(\approx12.124\). So Delhi gets \(7.389/12.124\approx0.609\), Mumbai \(0.224\), late \(0.136\), the \(0.030\). If the true next word is "Delhi", this step's loss is \(-\ln0.609\approx0.495\) — the surprise of Unit 14. The loss for the whole sentence is the sum of these, one per word.

Backprop through time: blame walks back along the words

Imagine this

A relay team loses the race. The coach starts at the finish line and walks back along the track, asking each runner: "How much of the delay was yours?"

Every runner here is the same person — our one cell, used at every word. So when the coach adds up the blame for that one person, he adds it from every leg of the race.

An unrolled cell is a deep network (§3), so we train it exactly as in Unit 15: a forward pass that stores every note, then a backward pass with the same two rules. We just walk back along time instead of along layers. People call this backpropagation through time, or BPTT.

  • Rule A, at every tanh. Multiply the incoming blame by the slope of tanh, which is \(1-h_t^2\) (you stored \(h_t\) on the way forward).
  • Rule B, at every \(w\,h_{t-1}+u\,x_t+b\). Blame for the old note: multiply by \(w\). Blame for the weight: blame times the note that came in, \(h_{t-1}\).
  • The new twist: add up. The same \(w\) is used at every step. So its gradient is the sum of what each step reports.

A tiny example. Take §2's cell (\(w=0.5\), inputs \(1,0,0\), notes \(0.7616,\ 0.3634,\ 0.1797\)). How much does a small nudge to \(h_1\) move \(h_3\)? Walk back one step at a time. Each step is "slope of tanh times \(w\)":

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial h_3}{\partial h_2}&=w\,(1-h_3^2)=0.5\,(1-0.1797^2)\approx0.4838\\ \frac{\partial h_2}{\partial h_1}&=w\,(1-h_2^2)=0.5\,(1-0.3634^2)\approx0.4340\\ \frac{\partial h_3}{\partial h_1}&=0.4838\times0.4340\approx0.2100\end{aligned}\]

A nudge to the first note reaches the third note at only about a fifth of its size. The blame going back shrinks the same way. That is the first word's voice fading — now seen from the backward side.

Why memory fades or explodes

Imagine this

Hold a microphone near its own loudspeaker. If the speaker plays the sound a little softer than it came in, every loop makes it quieter, and it dies away. If it plays a little louder, every loop makes it bigger, and within a second there is a terrible screech.

Only when the loop gives back exactly what came in does the sound hold steady. Memory in a recurrent cell lives on the same knife edge.

Strip the cell down to its loop: no tanh, no new words, just \(h_t=w\,h_{t-1}\). After \(T\) steps,

\[h_T=w^T\,h_0,\qquad\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_0}=w^T.\]

The same power shows up forward (how much of the first word is left) and backward (how much blame reaches it). Put in numbers for \(T=10\):

  • \(w=0.5\): \(0.5^{10}\approx0.000977\). Less than a thousandth is left. The memory has faded.
  • \(w=1.5\): \(1.5^{10}\approx57.67\). The blame is 58 times too big. It has exploded.
  • \(w=1\): \(1^{10}=1\). Held steady — the knife edge.

Powers are ruthless. A number a little below 1 goes to 0; a little above 1 runs away. After 50 steps even \(w=0.9\) leaves \(0.005\), and \(w=1.1\) gives about 117.

Now with a real note: a matrix. When the note is a list of numbers, the loop is \(\mathbf h_t=W\mathbf h_{t-1}\), so \(\mathbf h_T=W^T\mathbf h_0\). Powers of a matrix. How do we see where those go? With the eigenvectors of Unit 4: the special directions that \(W\) only stretches, never turns. Along an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\lambda\), each step just multiplies by \(\lambda\) — back to the one-number story.

A tiny example: \(W=\begin{pmatrix}0.9&0.4\\ 0.1&0.6\end{pmatrix}\). Its eigenvalues are \(\lambda_1=1\) and \(\lambda_2=0.5\) (check: they add to the trace \(1.5\) and multiply to the determinant \(0.54-0.04=0.5\)). The directions are \((4,1)\) for \(\lambda_1=1\) and \((1,-1)\) for \(\lambda_2=0.5\).

Start the note at \(\mathbf v=(1,0)\). Split it along the two directions: \((1,0)=0.2\,(4,1)+0.2\,(1,-1)\). Now every step keeps the first part whole and halves the second:

\[W^t\mathbf v=0.2\,(4,1)+0.2\cdot0.5^t\,(1,-1)\ \longrightarrow\ (0.8,\,0.2).\]

After one step, \((0.9,0.1)\); after two, \((0.85,0.15)\); after ten, almost exactly \((0.8,0.2)\). The part along the \(\lambda=1\) direction survives. The part along the \(\lambda=0.5\) direction halves every step and is gone.

So the rule is: look at the biggest eigenvalue size, \(|\lambda|_{\max}\). Below 1, everything fades. Above 1, something explodes. Backward, the blame is multiplied by \(W^{\mathsf T}\) at each step — and \(W^{\mathsf T}\) has the same eigenvalues, so the same verdict holds for the blame. And the tanh slope \(1-h^2\) is never more than 1, so it can only add fading.

First aid for explosions: clip the gradient

Imagine this

School buses in India carry a speed governor. The driver can press the pedal as hard as he likes; the bus will not go faster than the limit. It still goes where he steers — only not at a crazy speed.

Gradient clipping is a speed governor for training.

When blame explodes, the gradient can become enormous for a single step. Picture the loss as a landscape: mostly gentle slopes, but here and there a cliff — a sudden wall where the loss jumps. That wall is what exploding powers of \(W\) look like. Touch the edge of the cliff and the slope is huge. A plain step \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\mathbf g\) then throws the weights far away, and all the progress so far is lost.

The fix is simple. Pick a limit \(c\). If the gradient is longer than \(c\), shrink it to length \(c\) and keep its direction:

\[\text{if }\|\mathbf g\|>c:\qquad\mathbf g\leftarrow c\,\frac{\mathbf g}{\|\mathbf g\|}.\]

A tiny example. \(\mathbf g=(30,40)\), so \(\|\mathbf g\|=\sqrt{900+1600}=50\). With \(c=5\): \(\mathbf g\leftarrow5\cdot(30,40)/50=(3,4)\). Same direction, length 5. A gradient like \((1,2,2)\), of length 3, is shorter than 5 and passes through untouched.

Clipping only helps with explosions. It cannot help with fading: if the blame reaching word 1 is \(10^{-9}\), there is no signal left to rescue. For fading we need a new design — that is §8.

The LSTM: a memory with an express lane

Imagine this

A shopkeeper's ledger — the bahi-khata — passes from day to day. Each evening he makes three small choices. With an eraser: what to rub out. With a pen: what to write in. And at the window: what to read out to the customer.

The ledger itself is never torn up and rewritten. It rides from day to day almost untouched. That is the LSTM's secret.

Why does the plain cell forget? At every step its whole note is multiplied by \(W\) and squeezed by tanh. There is no way to carry a number along unchanged. So in 1997 Hochreiter and Schmidhuber added a second note that runs along the top: the cell state \(\mathbf c_t\). People call the whole design LSTM, "long short-term memory". Think of the cell state as an express lane: at each step only two gentle things happen to it.

First, three gates are computed. A gate is a list of numbers between 0 and 1, made with a sigmoid (Unit 15). 0 means "shut", 1 means "wide open". Each gate looks at the new word and the last note:

  • the forget gate \(\mathbf f_t\) — the eraser: how much of the old cell state to keep;
  • the input gate \(\mathbf i_t\) — the pen: how much of the new candidate to write in;
  • the output gate \(\mathbf o_t\) — the window: how much of the cell state to show as the note.

The candidate \(\mathbf g_t=\tanh(\dots)\) is what the pen would write. Then:

\[\begin{aligned}\mathbf c_t&=\mathbf f_t\odot\mathbf c_{t-1}+\mathbf i_t\odot\mathbf g_t\\ \mathbf h_t&=\mathbf o_t\odot\tanh(\mathbf c_t)\end{aligned}\]

The symbol \(\odot\) just means "multiply entry by entry". Each gate has its own weights, like a small layer: \(\mathbf f_t=\sigma(W_f\mathbf h_{t-1}+U_f\mathbf x_t+\mathbf b_f)\), and the same shape for \(\mathbf i_t\), \(\mathbf o_t\) and \(\mathbf g_t\) (with tanh for \(\mathbf g_t\)).

A tiny example. Forget gate \(f=0.9\), old cell state \(c_{t-1}=1\), input gate \(i=0.2\), candidate \(g=0.5\):

\[c_t=0.9\cdot1+0.2\cdot0.5=0.9+0.1=1.0.\]

We erased a tenth, then wrote in a little. With the window fully open (\(o=1\)), the note is \(h_t=\tanh(1.0)\approx0.7616\).

The GRU: a lighter cousin with one blend dial

Imagine this

A chaiwala keeps one big pot on the stove all day. Every hour he pours in some fresh tea. A little fresh, and the pot tastes much as before. A lot fresh, and the old taste is gone.

How much fresh tea to pour — that is one dial. The GRU is built around this dial.

In 2014 Cho and colleagues asked: can we keep the express lane with fewer parts? Their GRU ("gated recurrent unit") drops the separate cell state. It keeps one note and two gates:

  • the update gate \(\mathbf z_t\) — the dial: how much new to pour in;
  • the reset gate \(\mathbf r_t\) — how much of the old note to look at when making the new candidate.

\[\begin{aligned}\tilde{\mathbf h}_t&=\tanh\big(W(\mathbf r_t\odot\mathbf h_{t-1})+U\mathbf x_t+\mathbf b\big)\\ \mathbf h_t&=(1-\mathbf z_t)\odot\mathbf h_{t-1}+\mathbf z_t\odot\tilde{\mathbf h}_t\end{aligned}\]

The second line is a blend: part old note, part fresh candidate, with the dial \(\mathbf z_t\) between 0 and 1. When \(z\) is near 0, the old note passes straight through — the express lane again, now multiplied by \(1-z\) at each step.

A tiny example. Old note \(h_{t-1}=0.8\), candidate \(\tilde h_t=-0.4\), dial \(z=0.25\):

\[h_t=0.75\cdot0.8+0.25\cdot(-0.4)=0.6-0.1=0.5.\]

Three quarters old, one quarter new. And since it is a blend, the new note always lies between the old note and the candidate.

Encoder–decoder: translate through one summary

Imagine this

An interpreter at a meeting listens to a whole sentence in English. She holds its meaning in her head. Then she speaks it in Hindi, word by word.

Now give her a rule: she may keep only one small card of notes between listening and speaking. A short sentence fits easily. A very long speech does not.

To translate, the input and the output have different lengths and different word orders. "The train to Delhi is late" is six English words; in Hindi it is "Delhi ki train late hai", five words, in another order. So we use two recurrent cells:

  • The encoder reads the English sentence word by word. Its final note, \(\mathbf s=\mathbf h_T\), is the summary of the whole sentence (people also call it the context vector).
  • The decoder starts from that summary and writes the Hindi sentence one word at a time. After each word it reads what it just wrote as its next input, and it stops when it writes a special word, \(\langle\text{end}\rangle\).

Training uses one small trick. While learning, we feed the decoder the true previous Hindi word, not its own guess. This is called teacher forcing — like a teacher who corrects a student after every word, so one early slip does not ruin the rest of the sentence. At test time there is no teacher, and the decoder must use its own words.

The bottleneck. The summary has a fixed size — say 8 numbers in our toy, 512 in a real system. A 5-word sentence and a 50-word sentence must both squeeze into those same 8 numbers. If each word brings 8 numbers of its own, that is 40 numbers into 8 for the short sentence, and 400 into 8 for the long one. And the first word must survive the whole encoder: if the encoder keeps 90% of an old word at each step (a toy rate), word 1 of a 50-word sentence speaks in the summary at \(0.9^{49}\approx0.0057\) of its first strength. This is exactly what the first translation systems of 2014 showed: good on short sentences, worse and worse on long ones.

Claim. A toy linear encoder \(\mathbf s_t=a\,\mathbf s_{t-1}+\mathbf e_t\) (keep a fraction \(a\) of the old summary, add the new word) gives \(\mathbf s_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a^{n-k}\mathbf e_k\). 1
Unroll as in §8's leaky sum: each new step multiplies everything earlier by \(a\) and adds one new word. The same shape as \(c_T=\sum(\prod f)\,ig\), with every gate fixed.
2
\(0.9^{n-1}<0.01\) means \(n-1>\ln0.01/\ln0.9\approx43.7\), so \(n-1\ge44\), \(n\ge45\). Check: \(0.9^{43}\approx0.0108\), \(0.9^{44}\approx0.0097\). ∎ A trained encoder is cleverer than a fixed leak — gates help — but it still has only a fixed number of slots for a sentence of any length.

In one sentence: An encoder squeezes the whole source sentence into one fixed-size summary and a decoder writes the translation from it, trained with teacher forcing — and that single summary is the bottleneck that long sentences overflow.

Picking the words: greedy or beam search

Imagine this

You are driving across a city. At every junction you take the road that looks fastest right now. Sometimes that road leads straight into a traffic jam, and a road that looked a little slower at first would have got you home sooner.

Keeping two or three routes in mind, instead of one, often finds the better trip.

At each step the decoder gives a probability for every possible next word. The probability of a whole sentence is the product of its word probabilities (the chain rule of Unit 14). We want the sentence with the biggest product. But there are far too many sentences to try them all.

Greedy decoding takes the single most likely word at each step and never looks back. Beam search keeps the \(k\) best partial sentences at every step (\(k\) is the beam width), grows each by one word, and again keeps only the best \(k\). With \(k=1\) it is greedy.

A tiny example. The decoder is writing the English for "hamari train late hai". First word: "the" 0.5, "our" 0.4, "a" 0.1. Second word, after "the": "train" 0.4, "bus" 0.3, "rain" 0.3. After "our": "train" 0.9, "bus" 0.1. After "a": "train" 0.6, "bus" 0.4.

  • Greedy: take "the" (0.5), then its best, "train" (0.4). Sentence "the train": \(0.5\times0.4=0.20\).
  • Beam, \(k=2\): keep "the" (0.5) and "our" (0.4). Grow both: the train 0.20, the bus 0.15, the rain 0.15, our train \(0.4\times0.9=0.36\), our bus 0.04. The best is "our train", 0.36.

Greedy lost because "our" looked slightly worse at the first step, even though it led to a far surer second word. In practice we add log-probabilities instead of multiplying (products of many small numbers become too tiny for a computer): \(\ln0.36\approx-1.022\) beats \(\ln0.20\approx-1.609\).

Greedy against beam search, on a tree of choicesEach branch is a possible next word with its probability. Gold paths are the ones the search is keeping; the number at each leaf is the whole sentence's probability.

Try: Press ▶ search with width 1 (greedy): it commits to "the" and ends at 0.20. Switch to width 2 and search again: "our" survives the first cut and wins with 0.36. Width 3 keeps everything and finds the same 0.36.

The realization

\[\begin{gathered}P(\text{sentence})=\prod_t P(\text{word}_t\mid\dots)\\ \text{score}=\sum_t\ln P(\text{word}_t\mid\dots)\end{gathered}\]

Greedy decoding picks the best word now; beam search keeps the \(k\) best sentences-so-far and can recover when a slightly weaker first word leads somewhere much better. It costs about \(k\) times the work of greedy, and it is still not a guarantee of the very best sentence — just a much better bet.

Try it, then answer

On this tree, does beam width 3 find a better sentence than beam width 2?

If you want the algebra · 1 proof, step by step
Prove it · adding logs picks the same winner, without the underflow

Claim. For sentences \(A\) and \(B\), \(\prod_tp_t^A>\prod_tp_t^B\) exactly when \(\sum_t\ln p_t^A>\sum_t\ln p_t^B\). A 200-word sentence whose words each have probability 0.01 has product \(10^{-400}\), which a computer's usual numbers round to 0, but log-score \(200\ln0.01\approx-921.0\).

1
\(\ln\) turns a product into a sum, \(\ln(xy)=\ln x+\ln y\), and it is increasing, so it never changes which of two positive numbers is bigger. Tree: \(\ln0.36\approx-1.022>\ln0.20\approx-1.609\), the same verdict as \(0.36>0.20\).
2
Ordinary 64-bit floats cannot hold positive numbers below about \(10^{-308}\) (\(10^{-324}\) with the tiniest special values), so \(10^{-400}\) becomes 0 and every long sentence would tie. The sum of logs stays an ordinary number. ∎ This is the same log-likelihood as Unit 14: training maximises it, and decoding searches for it.

In one sentence: A sentence's probability is the product of its word probabilities; greedy takes the best word each time and can land on 0.20, while a beam of width 2 keeps two candidates and finds 0.36.

What to carry forward

The whole unit, one card at a time.

Order needs memory

A bag of words loses order; a window loses the past. A running note that changes the old note before adding the new word keeps both.

The recurrent cell

\(\mathbf h_t=\tanh(W\mathbf h_{t-1}+U\mathbf x_t+\mathbf b)\), the same weights at every word: \(h(h+d)+h\) numbers for any length.

Unrolled

A deep network as long as the sentence, whose layers are twins. Read out with \(\operatorname{softmax}(V\mathbf h_t)\); the loss adds up over steps.

Backprop through time

Rule A and Rule B on the unrolled chain. The shared weight's gradient is the sum over steps. The blame to an early word is a product of one factor per step.

Fade or explode

The factors are powers of one matrix, so the biggest \(|\lambda|\) decides: \(0.5^{10}\approx0.001\), \(1.5^{10}\approx58\).

Clipping

If \(\|\mathbf g\|>c\), rescale to length \(c\). A cure for explosions, not for fading.

LSTM

\(\mathbf c_t=\mathbf f\odot\mathbf c_{t-1}+\mathbf i\odot\mathbf g\): an express lane where blame is multiplied only by the forget gate.

GRU

\(\mathbf h_t=(1-\mathbf z)\odot\mathbf h_{t-1}+\mathbf z\odot\tilde{\mathbf h}_t\): one blend dial. Blocks: plain 1, GRU 3, LSTM 4.

Encoder–decoder

Squeeze the source into one summary, write the target from it; teacher forcing to train, beam search to decode. The summary is a bottleneck.

Where this goes next.

  • Unit 18 · Attention and Transformers. Two problems are left over from this unit. The decoder sees the source only through one summary, and every word must wait for the word before it. Attention fixes both at once: every word can look directly at every other word, with weights from dot products (Unit 3) and softmax (Unit 14). The residual path of a transformer is another express lane — the same idea as the LSTM's.
  • Unit 19 · The Maths Inside an LLM. A language model at huge scale: next-word probabilities, and how the machine picks its words — greedy, beam, and the random sampling with temperature, top-k and top-p that chat models use.
The realization

\[\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial\mathbf h_T}{\partial\mathbf h_k}\ \text{carries}\ W^{T-k}\\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{eigenvalues decide}\end{gathered}\]

A machine with memory multiplies by the same matrix at every word, so its memory and its blame live or die by that matrix's eigenvalues. Gates build an express lane where the multiplier is a number the network controls, close to 1 when it wants to remember.

In one sentence: A recurrent cell reads one word at a time with shared weights, trains by backprop through time where powers of one matrix make memory fade or explode, is rescued by gates that open an express lane, and translates through a single summary — the bottleneck that attention removes next.

Practice arena — fourteen problems, solved in full

Fourteen problems, easy to hard: a forward pass by hand, one step with matrices, counting shared weights, blame through three steps, the step at which memory is gone, eigenvalues that decide, clipping, one LSTM step, the express lane, one GRU step, parameter counts, greedy against beam, the shapes of a translator, and a recorded run to diagnose. Every number was checked by machine.

Three habits do most of the work. Write the notes down: keep a table of every \(h_t\) (and \(c_t\)) on the way forward, because the way back needs them. Count steps, not words: from \(h_k\) to \(h_T\) there are \(T-k\) factors. And look for the power: whenever something is multiplied by the same number again and again, it is \(w^T\), and \(\ln\) turns "how many steps?" into a division.

Show the full solution

Step 2 — word 2. \(0.8\cdot0.7616=0.6093\); add \(-1\): \(-0.3907\). \(h_2=\tanh(-0.3907)\approx-0.3720\).
Step 3 — word 3. \(0.8\cdot(-0.3720)=-0.2976\); add \(0.5\): \(0.2024\). \(h_3=\tanh(0.2024)\approx0.1997\).

Problem 5mediumfade or explode

In the loop \(h_t=w\,h_{t-1}\), the blame after \(T\) steps is \(w^T\). (a) Write a formula for the first \(T\) at which \(w^T\) drops below \(\varepsilon\) (for \(0<w<1\)). (b) Apply it for \(w=0.8\), \(\varepsilon=10^{-3}\). (c) For \(w=1.2\), find the first \(T\) at which \(w^T\) exceeds 1000.

What this tests. Turning "how many steps?" into a division with logarithms. Plan. Take \(\ln\) of both sides; mind that \(\ln w<0\) flips the inequality.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(w^T<\varepsilon\iff T\ln w<\ln\varepsilon\iff T>\dfrac{\ln\varepsilon}{\ln w}\) (dividing by the negative \(\ln w\) flips the sign). So \(T=\big\lfloor\ln\varepsilon/\ln w\big\rfloor+1\).
