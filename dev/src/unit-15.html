For fourteen units we collected parts: matrices that move space, the chain rule, blame flowing backwards, a step downhill, softmax. A network is a stack of simple steps

Imagine this

Think of a line of stations in a factory. A part comes in. The first station does one small job and passes it on. The next station does another small job. No station is clever. But at the end of the line, a whole scooter rolls out.

A neural network is the same kind of line. Each station is simple. The power comes from putting many of them in a row.

Over fourteen units we collected the parts. A matrix is a machine that moves space (Unit 1). The chain rule multiplies slopes along a chain (Unit 6). Blame can flow backwards through a graph (Unit 7). A step downhill makes a guess better (Unit 9). Softmax turns scores into probabilities (Unit 14). Now we bolt them together.

Here is the one station a network is built from. We call it a layer, and it does three things in order:

  1. Multiply by a matrix. Mix the incoming numbers, each with its own weight.
  2. Add a shift. Add one extra number to each result. This is the bias.
  3. Bend. Pass each result through a simple bent function, like "keep it if positive, else make it zero". This is the activation.

In symbols, layer number \(l\) takes the list \(\mathbf a^{(l-1)}\) that arrives and makes two new lists:

\[\begin{gathered}\mathbf z^{(l)}=W^{(l)}\mathbf a^{(l-1)}+\mathbf b^{(l)},\\ \mathbf a^{(l)}=\operatorname{ReLU}\big(\mathbf z^{(l)}\big).\end{gathered}\]

The list \(\mathbf z\) is the mix before the bend. The list \(\mathbf a\) is what comes out after the bend. The superscript \((l)\) just says which layer. That is all a layer is.

Meet the salary network. We will use one small network for the whole unit. It guesses a person's salary (in lakhs) from two facts: their age and their years of experience. The two facts go in. A layer of 3 neurons works on them. A layer of 2 neurons works on that. One neuron at the end gives the guess, \(\hat y\). We write this shape as 2 → 3 → 2 → 1.

How big is each matrix? There is one simple rule. A weight joins one neuron in this layer to one neuron in the layer before. So the matrix has one row for every neuron here and one column for every neuron before:

\[W^{(l)}\ \text{is}\ (\text{neurons in layer } l)\times(\text{neurons in layer } l-1).\]

So \(W^{(1)}\) is \(3\times2\), \(W^{(2)}\) is \(2\times3\) and \(W^{(3)}\) is \(1\times2\). Each bias has one entry per neuron: \(3\times1\), \(2\times1\), \(1\times1\). Add them up: \(6+3+6+2+2+1=20\) numbers. Those 20 numbers are everything the network knows. Training means changing them.

One neuron is a line and a bend

Imagine this

Take a flat sheet of paper and fold it once along a straight crease. One side stays flat on the table. The other side lifts up like a ramp.

That fold is exactly what one neuron does to its inputs. The crease is a straight line. The bend happens along it.

Look at a single neuron with two inputs \(x_1, x_2\). First it mixes them: \(z=w_1x_1+w_2x_2+b\). Where is \(z\) exactly zero? On the straight line \(w_1x_1+w_2x_2+b=0\). On one side of that line \(z\) is positive, on the other side negative. This is the same line you met in Unit 13 — a support vector machine's line, just without the street around it.

A tiny example. Take \(w_1=1\), \(w_2=1\), \(b=-2\). At the point \((3,1)\): \(z=3+1-2=2\). At \((0,0)\): \(z=-2\). At \((1,1)\): \(z=0\) — right on the line.

Then the neuron bends. It passes \(z\) through an activation. There are four common choices:

  • ReLU, \(\max(0,z)\): keep a positive number, turn a negative one into zero. It is a hinge. With it, the flat plane folds along the line: flat zero on one side, a straight ramp on the other. Our example gives \(\operatorname{ReLU}(2)=2\) and \(\operatorname{ReLU}(-2)=0\).
  • Sigmoid, \(1/(1+e^{-z})\): a smooth step from 0 to 1. It reads like a probability (Unit 14). \(\sigma(0)=0.5\) on the line.
  • tanh: a smooth step from −1 to 1.
  • None: leave \(z\) as it is. No bend at all — just a tilted flat sheet.

The bend has a slope, and the slope will matter a lot on the way back. ReLU's slope is simple: 1 on the awake side, 0 on the sleeping side. Sigmoid's slope is never more than 0.25. Keep those two numbers in mind.

Prove it · sigmoid's slope is never more than ¼

Claim. For \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\), the slope is \(\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)\,(1-\sigma(z))\), and it is at most \(\tfrac14\), reached only at \(z=0\).

1
Write \(\sigma=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}\) and use the chain rule: \[\begin{aligned}\sigma'(z)&=\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2}\\ &=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}.\end{aligned}\] The derivative of \(u^{-1}\) is \(-u^{-2}u'\), and \(u'=-e^{-z}\).
2
The second factor is \(1-\sigma\), because \(\dfrac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}=1-\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\). So \(\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\). A handy form: the slope comes straight from the value you already stored on the way forward.
3
Put \(s=\sigma\in(0,1)\). Then \(s(1-s)=\tfrac14-(s-\tfrac12)^2\le\tfrac14\), with equality only at \(s=\tfrac12\), that is \(z=0\). ∎ Example: at \(z=2\), \(\sigma\approx0.881\) and the slope is \(0.881\times0.119\approx0.105\).

In one sentence: A neuron draws one straight line \(w\cdot x+b=0\) and bends the plane along it — a hinge with slope 0 or 1 for ReLU, a smooth step with slope at most 0.25 for sigmoid.

3

Why the bend matters: straight layers collapse

Imagine this

You photocopy a photo at 150%. Then you photocopy the copy at 80%. You could have done it in one go at 120%.

Two straight "stretch and shift" steps in a row are always one straight step. Stack a hundred of them and you still have just one. Depth without bends buys you nothing.

Let us check it with small numbers. Take two layers with no bend:

\[\begin{gathered}W_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\ 0&1\end{pmatrix},\ \mathbf b_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\ 0\end{pmatrix},\\ W_2=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\ 2&1\end{pmatrix},\ \mathbf b_2=\begin{pmatrix}0\\ 1\end{pmatrix}.\end{gathered}\]

Push \(\mathbf x=(1,1)\) through. Layer 1 gives \(W_1\mathbf x+\mathbf b_1=(1+2+1,\ 0+1+0)=(4,1)\). Layer 2 gives \(W_2(4,1)+\mathbf b_2=(4-1+0,\ 8+1+1)=(3,10)\).

Now do it in one step. Multiply the matrices once: \(W_2W_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 2&5\end{pmatrix}\). Push the first shift through the second matrix and add the second shift: \(W_2\mathbf b_1+\mathbf b_2=(1,3)\). So

\[\begin{aligned}&W_2(W_1\mathbf x+\mathbf b_1)+\mathbf b_2\\ &\quad=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 2&5\end{pmatrix}\mathbf x+\begin{pmatrix}1\\ 3\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}\]

Check at \((1,1)\): \((1+1+1,\ 2+5+3)=(3,10)\). The same answer, from one layer. In Unit 1 words: a matrix moves space so that straight grid lines stay straight and parallel. Two such moves in a row are still such a move.

So why do deep networks work? Because of the bend. Put a ReLU between the two layers, and the grid gets folded. A fold is something no single matrix can do. Try it below.

A straight step after a straight step is one straight step. Without bends, a network of any depth is just one matrix and one shift, and can only ever draw straight boundaries. The bend between layers is what lets depth fold space — and folding is what untangles data.

2
Multiplying by a matrix spreads over a sum (it is a linear machine, Unit 1): \[\begin{aligned}&W_2(W_1\mathbf x+\mathbf b_1)+\mathbf b_2\\ &=W_2W_1\mathbf x+W_2\mathbf b_1+\mathbf b_2\\ &=(W_2W_1)\mathbf x\\ &\quad+(W_2\mathbf b_1+\mathbf b_2).\end{aligned}\] ∎ With the numbers of §3: \(W_2W_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 2&5\end{pmatrix}\) and \(W_2\mathbf b_1+\mathbf b_2=(1,3)\).
Prove it · a hundred straight layers are still one

Claim. Any stack of \(n\) layers without activations, \(\mathbf x\mapsto W_n(\cdots(W_1\mathbf x+\mathbf b_1)\cdots)+\mathbf b_n\), equals one layer \(\mathbf x\mapsto M\mathbf x+\mathbf c\).

1
For \(n=1\) it is already one layer. Suppose the first \(n-1\) layers equal \(M'\mathbf x+\mathbf c'\). This is a proof by walking up one layer at a time (induction).
2
Add layer \(n\) on top and use the previous proof: \[\begin{aligned}&W_n(M'\mathbf x+\mathbf c')+\mathbf b_n\\ &=(W_nM')\,\mathbf x\\ &\quad+(W_n\mathbf c'+\mathbf b_n).\end{aligned}\] So \(M=W_n\cdots W_2W_1\) and the shifts collect into one vector \(\mathbf c\). ∎ The consequence: without bends, every boundary the network can draw is a straight line (a flat plane, in more dimensions) — exactly what one layer could draw.

In one sentence: Two straight layers are one straight layer — \(W_2W_1\) and \(W_2\mathbf b_1+\mathbf b_2\) — so without a bend depth is wasted, and the bend is what lets a network fold space.

Fold by fold: a network can draw any shape

Imagine this

You want to bend a straight piece of wire into the shape of a hill. One bend makes a corner. Three bends make a little tent. With enough bends, placed well, the wire can follow any curve you like — a hill, a valley, a wave.

Each ReLU neuron is one bend in the wire.

Work in one dimension, with one input \(x\). One ReLU neuron, \(\operatorname{ReLU}(x-c)\), is flat until \(x=c\) and then rises. It is one crease at \(c\).

Now add three of them with weights \(1,-2,1\):

\[\begin{aligned}h(x)=\ &\operatorname{ReLU}(x)-2\operatorname{ReLU}(x-1)\\ &+\operatorname{ReLU}(x-2).\end{aligned}\]

Read it off at a few points. \(h(0)=0\). \(h(1)=1-0+0=1\). \(h(2)=2-2+0=0\). And for \(x\ge2\): \(x-2(x-1)+(x-2)=0\). So \(h\) is a hat (a tent): flat, up to 1, down to 0, flat again. Three neurons, one bump.

Now the big idea. Any curve can be traced by little straight pieces. Every straight piece needs a crease at each end. So enough hats, added up, can trace any curve as closely as you like. This is the universal approximation result, said plainly: one hidden layer with enough neurons can get as close as you want to any smooth shape.

Be honest about what it does not say. It does not say how many neurons you need. It does not say how to find the weights. Finding them is the job of training (§7–§9).

1
For \(k=1\): \(t\) rises from 0 to 1 on \([0,\tfrac12]\) and falls back on \([\tfrac12,1]\). Two pieces, each covering all of \([0,1]\). Check the ReLU form: for \(x\le\tfrac12\) it is \(2x\); for \(x\ge\tfrac12\) it is \(2x-4(x-\tfrac12)=2-2x\).
2

3

In one sentence: Each ReLU adds one crease, three make a hat, enough hats trace any curve — and stacking layers folds the folds, doubling the pieces with every layer.

The forward pass, by hand

Imagine this

A dabbawala relay in Mumbai. The tiffin is picked up at home, handed to a runner, put on a train, passed to another runner, and delivered at the office. At every hand-over, someone writes a note in the register: "received at 10:05, passed on at 10:12".

The forward pass is that relay. Numbers are handed from layer to layer. And, just like the register, we write down every hand-over. We will need those notes on the way back.

Here are the starting numbers of the salary network. Everything is in lakhs.

\[\begin{aligned}W^{(1)}&=\begin{pmatrix}0.1&0.3\\ 0.2&-0.1\\ -0.1&0.2\end{pmatrix},\ \mathbf b^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\ -1\\ -1\end{pmatrix},\\ W^{(2)}&=\begin{pmatrix}1&-0.5&1\\ 1&0.5&-1\end{pmatrix},\ \mathbf b^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\ -2\end{pmatrix},\\ W^{(3)}&=\begin{pmatrix}4&2\end{pmatrix},\ b^{(3)}=2.\end{aligned}\]

The input is \(\mathbf a^{(0)}=(30,10)\): age 30, experience 10. The true salary is \(y=50\). Now walk the chain from left to right. Each row of a matrix times the input column is a dot product (Unit 3): multiply matching entries and add.

F1 · into hidden layer 1 (\(\mathbf z^{(1)}=W^{(1)}\mathbf a^{(0)}+\mathbf b^{(1)}\)). One row per neuron:

\[\begin{aligned}\text{row 1: }&(0.1)(30)+(0.3)(10)+1=3+3+1=7\\ \text{row 2: }&(0.2)(30)+(-0.1)(10)-1=6-1-1=4\\ \text{row 3: }&(-0.1)(30)+(0.2)(10)-1=-3+2-1=-2\end{aligned}\]

So \(\mathbf z^{(1)}=(7,4,-2)\). Its shape is \(3\times1\) ✓ — one number per neuron.

F2 · the bend. \(\mathbf a^{(1)}=\operatorname{ReLU}(7,4,-2)=(7,4,0)\). The third neuron got a negative number, so ReLU switched it off. It is asleep for this person. Keep an eye on it.

F3 · into hidden layer 2.

\[\begin{aligned}\text{row 1: }&(1)(7)+(-0.5)(4)+(1)(0)+1=7-2+0+1=6\\ \text{row 2: }&(1)(7)+(0.5)(4)+(-1)(0)-2=7+2-0-2=7\end{aligned}\]

F4 · the bend. Both are positive, so \(\mathbf a^{(2)}=(6,7)\). ReLU changes nothing here.

F5 · into the output. \(z^{(3)}=(4)(6)+(2)(7)+2=24+14+2=40\).

F6 · the last bend. \(\hat y=\operatorname{ReLU}(40)=40\). The network predicts 40 lakhs.

F7 · the score. \(L=\tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2=\tfrac12(40-50)^2=\tfrac12(100)=50\). The error is \(\hat y-y=-10\): the guess is 10 lakhs too low.

6

What comes out, and how we score it

Imagine this

Two kinds of guessing game. In the first, you guess a number — "this flat rents for 25 thousand" — and you are told how far off you were. In the second, you pick from a list — "this photo is a cat, a dog or a cow" — and you are told how sure you were of the right one.

A network ends with a head made for its game. The two heads look different. But the blame they send back has the same simple shape.

Regression head (a number). The output is the number \(\hat y\). We score it with \(L=\tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2\). Why a square? Unit 14 showed it: if the truth is the guess plus bell-shaped noise, then the squared error is exactly "how unlikely the data is". And why the \(\tfrac12\)? Differentiate:

\[\frac{\partial L}{\partial\hat y}=\tfrac12\cdot2(\hat y-y)=\hat y-y.\]

The \(\tfrac12\) is there only to cancel the 2. For our salary: \(40-50=-10\).

Classification head (a choice). The last layer gives one score per class, \(\mathbf z\). Softmax turns scores into probabilities (Unit 14): \(q_i=e^{z_i}/\sum_je^{z_j}\). The loss is the cross-entropy: minus the log of the probability you gave the right class (Unit 14). Take scores \(\mathbf z=(2,1,0)\) with class 1 correct:

\[\mathbf q=(0.665,\,0.245,\,0.090),\qquad L=-\ln0.665\approx0.408.\]

And the blame at the scores? Write the truth as a one-hot list \(\mathbf y=(1,0,0)\). Then

\[\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z}=\mathbf q-\mathbf y=(-0.335,\,0.245,\,0.090).\]

Look at the two results side by side: \(\hat y-y\) and \(\mathbf q-\mathbf y\). Both say prediction minus truth. The right class's score gets pushed up (negative blame means "increase me"). The wrong classes get pushed down, each in proportion to how much probability it wrongly took.

From here the blame enters the network the same way in both cases. If the layer below fed \(\mathbf a=(1,2)\) into these three scores, the weight blame is the outer product (Rule B, coming in §7):

\[\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}=(\mathbf q-\mathbf y)\,\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}=\begin{pmatrix}-0.335&-0.670\\ 0.245&0.489\\ 0.090&0.180\end{pmatrix}.\]

Two kinds of arrow, two rules

Imagine this

India has lost a close match by 10 runs. In the review, the coach does not blame everyone equally. The bowler who gave away the most runs gets the most blame. A player who was not on the field that day gets none at all.

The backward pass is that review. The mistake at the end, −10, is shared out along the wires — more to whoever contributed more, nothing to whoever was switched off.

Look again at the chain of eight nodes. The arrows between them come in only two kinds:

  • a linear arrow, \(\mathbf z=W\mathbf a+\mathbf b\) — three of them: \(\mathbf a^{(0)}\to\mathbf z^{(1)}\), \(\mathbf a^{(1)}\to\mathbf z^{(2)}\), \(\mathbf a^{(2)}\to z^{(3)}\);
  • a ReLU arrow, \(\mathbf a=\operatorname{ReLU}(\mathbf z)\) — three of them: \(\mathbf z^{(1)}\to\mathbf a^{(1)}\), \(\mathbf z^{(2)}\to\mathbf a^{(2)}\), \(z^{(3)}\to\hat y\).

The whole network alternates: linear, ReLU, linear, ReLU, linear, ReLU — then the loss. So to walk backwards we need only two rules, one for each kind of arrow. We walk back carrying one thing: the blame on the node we stand on, \(\partial L/\partial(\text{node})\). Each arrow we cross turns one blame into the next. This is exactly Unit 7's "local slope × incoming blame" (Unit 7, §3), now done with whole lists and matrices (Unit 7, §6).

Rule A · crossing a ReLU arrow backwards

Each \(a_j\) depends only on its own \(z_j\). So the blame passes straight through, times the local slope — and ReLU's slope is 1 or 0:

\[\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf a}\odot\operatorname{ReLU}'(\mathbf z).\]

The sign \(\odot\) means "multiply entry by entry". Tiny example: blame \((-60,10,-20)\) arriving at \(\mathbf z=(7,4,-2)\). The slopes are \((1,1,0)\). So the blame that gets through is \((-60,10,0)\). Rule A is a switch: open where the neuron was awake, shut where it was asleep.

Rule B · crossing a linear arrow backwards

Three things fed into \(\mathbf z=W\mathbf a+\mathbf b\), so crossing it back gives three answers:

\[\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z}\,\mathbf a^{\mathsf T},\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf b}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z},\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf a}=W^{\mathsf T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z}.\]

  • The weights: each weight's blame = the blame at its output × what flowed in through it. As a grid that is an outer product: a column times a row.
  • The shifts: a shift adds straight into its \(z\), so it gets exactly the blame of that \(z\).
  • The layer below: each input fed every neuron, so its blame adds up the blame of all of them, weighted by the wires. Forward you multiply by \(W\). Backward you multiply by \(W^{\mathsf T}\). Same wires, opposite direction.

Tiny example (layer 3 of the salary network): blame \(-10\) at \(z^{(3)}\), stored \(\mathbf a^{(2)}=(6,7)\), weights \(W^{(3)}=(4,2)\). Then \(\partial L/\partial W^{(3)}=(-10)(6,7)=(-60,-70)\), \(\partial L/\partial b^{(3)}=-10\), and \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf a^{(2)}=(4,2)^{\mathsf T}(-10)=(-40,-20)\).

Where does each transpose go? Use one rule that never fails: the blame of anything has the same shape as the thing. \(\partial L/\partial W\) must be shaped like \(W\), so it can only be (column)(row).

Claim. Claim. \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf a}=W^{\mathsf T}\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z}\).

1
Now \(a_k\) sits inside every \(z_j\) (each row uses every input). The backward pass, by hand

Imagine this

The dabbawala relay again — but now a tiffin reached the wrong office. The complaint travels back along the same route: office to last runner, last runner to the train, the train to the first runner. At each hand-over, the register says who touched it and how much.

That is the backward pass: the complaint walks the chain in reverse, and the register (the cache) tells each step what it needs.

Walk the salary network from right to left, one arrow at a time.

B1 · start at the loss. \(\partial L/\partial\hat y=\hat y-y=40-50=-10\).

B2 · cross \(z^{(3)}\to\hat y\) (Rule A). We stored \(z^{(3)}=40\), which is positive, so the switch is on: \(\partial L/\partial z^{(3)}=-10\times1=-10\).

B4 · cross \(\mathbf z^{(2)}\to\mathbf a^{(2)}\) (Rule A). Stored \(\mathbf z^{(2)}=(6,7)\): both awake, so \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf z^{(2)}=(-40,-20)\).

B5 · cross \(\mathbf a^{(1)}\to\mathbf z^{(2)}\) (Rule B). Stored \(\mathbf a^{(1)}=(7,4,0)\):

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(2)}}&=\begin{pmatrix}-40\\ -20\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}7&4&0\end{pmatrix}\\ &=\begin{pmatrix}-280&-160&0\\ -140&-80&0\end{pmatrix},\\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf b^{(2)}}&=\begin{pmatrix}-40\\ -20\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}\]

\[\begin{aligned}\text{row 1: }&(1)(-40)+(1)(-20)=-60\\ \text{row 2: }&(-0.5)(-40)+(0.5)(-20)=20-10=10\\ \text{row 3: }&(1)(-40)+(-1)(-20)=-40+20=-20\end{aligned}\]

B6 · cross \(\mathbf z^{(1)}\to\mathbf a^{(1)}\) (Rule A — the switch closes). Stored \(\mathbf z^{(1)}=(7,4,-2)\). The third is negative:

\[\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z^{(1)}}=\begin{pmatrix}-60\\ 10\\ -20\end{pmatrix}\odot\begin{pmatrix}1\\ 1\\ 0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-60\\ 10\\ 0\end{pmatrix}.\]

B7 · cross \(\mathbf a^{(0)}\to\mathbf z^{(1)}\) (Rule B, the last step). Stored \(\mathbf a^{(0)}=(30,10)\):

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(1)}}&=\begin{pmatrix}-60\\ 10\\ 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}30&10\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-1800&-600\\ 300&100\\ 0&0\end{pmatrix},\\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf b^{(1)}}&=\begin{pmatrix}-60\\ 10\\ 0\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}\]

That is all 20 gradients. Three moments are worth saying out loud:

  • The bigger weight gets more blame. In B3, the wire with weight 4 sends back −40 and the wire with weight 2 sends back −20. Twice the weight, twice the blame. That is all \(W^{\mathsf T}\) is doing.
  • A weight that carried nothing gets no blame. The third column of \(\partial L/\partial W^{(2)}\) is all zero, because it multiplied \(a^{(1)}_3=0\).
  • A neuron that slept on the way forward learns nothing on the way back. The −20 was blocked in B6, so the whole third row of \(\partial L/\partial W^{(1)}\) is zero. Its weights will not move this step.

Rule B: \(\partial L/\partial W^{(1)}_{jk}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial z^{(1)}_j}\,x_k\). The first factor does not change (the network's outputs are the same), so the gradient is proportional to \(x_k\). Age = 30 is why \(\partial L/\partial W^{(1)}_{11}=-60\times30=-1800\).
2
A step \(\Delta W_{jk}=-\eta\,\partial L/\partial W_{jk}\) changes \(z_j\) by \(\Delta W_{jk}\,x_k=-\eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j}x_k^2\). So the effect of one step grows like \(x_k^2\), and a step size that is safe must shrink like \(1/x_k^2\). ∎ That is the canyon of Unit 10: one very steep direction forces a tiny \(\eta\) on all of them. The cure is to scale the inputs (Unit 10, §5).

In one sentence: Step every number against its gradient — loss 50 → 18.89 — but a first-layer gradient carries the raw input inside it, so scale the inputs, and check the gradients against \((L(\theta+h)-L(\theta-h))/2h\).

When the blame fades, explodes, or dies

Imagine this

A message is whispered down a line of twenty children. If each child passes on only a quarter of what they heard, the last child hears almost nothing. If each child shouts it twice as loud, the last one is deafened. And if one child has fallen asleep, the message stops right there.

The backward pass is that line of children. Every layer multiplies the blame by its slope and its weights.

Walking back through one layer multiplies the blame by two things: the slope of the bend (Rule A) and the weights (Rule B). Through many layers these multiply up. Three things can go wrong.

Fading (vanishing) blame. Sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25. So through 5 sigmoid layers the blame shrinks by at least \(0.25^5\approx0.001\), and through 10 layers by \(0.25^{10}\approx10^{-6}\) — unless the weights are big enough to make up for it. The first layers then hear almost nothing and barely learn. tanh is better (its slope reaches 1 at \(z=0\)). ReLU's slope is exactly 1 on every awake neuron, so nothing fades there.

Exploding blame. If the weights are big, each layer multiplies the blame up. Twenty layers that each double it give about a million. Steps become wild. (Unit 17 will meet this again, as an eigenvalue story, when the same matrix is used over and over in time.)

Dead neurons. A ReLU neuron whose \(z\) is negative for every input never passes any blame back — Rule A blocks it every time — so its incoming weights never change again. One sleeping neuron is normal; our salary network has one. Too many at once is the "dying ReLU" problem, and a big negative bias or one huge step (as in §9) can cause it.

The usual fixes, named here and used in the playground: ReLU-family bends; careful starting weights, about \(\sqrt{2/\text{fan-in}}\) in size for ReLU (fan-in = the number of wires coming into a neuron); scaled inputs (Unit 10); and adaptive steps like Adam (Unit 11).

Claim. In a chain of \(k\) one-neuron sigmoid layers \(a_l=\sigma(w_la_{l-1}+b_l)\), \(\left|\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial a_0}\right|\le\left|\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial a_k}\right|\prod_{l=1}^k\frac{|w_l|}{4}\). With \(|w_l|\le1\) the blame shrinks at least by \(4^{-k}\).

1
Crossing layer \(l\) backwards multiplies the blame by the local slope: Rule A gives \(\sigma'(z_l)\le\tfrac14\), Rule B gives \(w_l\). So \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_{l-1}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_l}\,\sigma'(z_l)\,w_l.\] The §2 drawer proved \(\sigma'\le\tfrac14\).
2
Multiply the \(k\) crossings together and bound each slope by \(\tfrac14\). ∎ \(0.25^5\approx0.00098\) and \(0.25^{10}\approx9.5\times10^{-7}\). With ReLU the slope is exactly 1 on awake neurons, so nothing shrinks there; with weights bigger than 4 the product can grow instead — exploding blame.

In one sentence: The blame reaching an early layer is a product of one factor per layer, so it fades with sigmoid (≤ 0.25 each), explodes with big weights, and dies at neurons that are always asleep — good bends, starting weights and scaled inputs keep it alive.

What to carry forward

The whole unit fits on one card. Here it is, piece by piece.

A layer

Multiply by a matrix, add a shift, bend: \(\mathbf a=\operatorname{ReLU}(W\mathbf a_{\text{prev}}+\mathbf b)\). \(W\) is (neurons here) × (neurons before).

No bend ⇒ collapse

Two straight layers are one: \(W_2W_1\), \(W_2\mathbf b_1+\mathbf b_2\). Depth needs bends.

Folds ⇒ any shape

One ReLU is one crease; three make a hat; enough hats trace any curve. Depth folds the folds: \(2^k\) pieces.

Forward

Row times column, shift, bend, layer by layer — and store every z and a.

The seed

At the output, the blame is prediction minus truth: \(\hat y-y\), or \(\mathbf q-\mathbf y\) with softmax.

Rule A

Across a ReLU: \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf z=\partial L/\partial\mathbf a\odot\operatorname{ReLU}'(\mathbf z)\). A switch.

Rule B

Across \(W\mathbf a+\mathbf b\): \(\partial L/\partial W=\partial L/\partial\mathbf z\,\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}\), \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf b=\partial L/\partial\mathbf z\), \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf a=W^{\mathsf T}\partial L/\partial\mathbf z\).

Update and check

\(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\partial L/\partial\theta\). Scale the inputs. Check gradients with \((L(\theta+h)-L(\theta-h))/2h\).

Watch the blame

It is a product over layers: it fades (sigmoid), explodes (big weights) or dies (sleeping ReLUs).

Where this goes next.

  • Unit 16 · Words as Vectors. The network's first real job: reading. Each word becomes a list of numbers, and a softmax over the whole vocabulary — thousands of classes — predicts the next word. The blame at that softmax is \(\mathbf q-\mathbf y\) again.
  • Unit 17 · Machines with Memory. The same layer is used again and again, once per word, so the backward pass runs back through time — and the fading and exploding of §10 become an eigenvalue story.
  • Unit 18 · Attention and Transformers. Fixed wires are replaced by wires the network chooses for itself, using dot products (Unit 3) and softmax.
The realization

\[\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\hat y}=\hat y-y,\ \text{then, layer by layer:}\\ \text{local slope}\times\text{incoming blame}\end{gathered}\]

At the output, the whole gradient is prediction minus truth. Everything before it is Unit 7's "local slope times incoming blame", done with matrices — Rule A at every bend, Rule B at every matrix. That is backpropagation, whole.

In one sentence: A network is multiply–shift–bend repeated; training is a forward pass that stores everything, a backward pass that starts from prediction minus truth and uses two rules, and a small step downhill.

A network has shape 3 → 4 → 4 → 2. (a) Give the shape of every weight matrix and bias vector. (b) How many numbers does the network learn in total?

What this tests. The shape rule: \(W^{(l)}\) is (neurons in layer \(l\)) × (neurons in layer \(l-1\)). Plan. Walk the arrows one at a time; count weights and biases separately, then add.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — layer 1 (3 → 4). Rows = 4 neurons here, columns = 3 before: \(W^{(1)}\) is \(4\times3\) (12 numbers), \(\mathbf b^{(1)}\) is \(4\times1\) (4).
Step 2 — layer 2 (4 → 4). \(W^{(2)}\) is \(4\times4\) (16), \(\mathbf b^{(2)}\) is \(4\times1\) (4).
Step 3 — layer 3 (4 → 2). \(W^{(3)}\) is \(2\times4\) (8), \(\mathbf b^{(3)}\) is \(2\times1\) (2).
Step 4 — add up (b). \(12+4+16+4+8+2=46\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(W^{(1)}:4\times3\), \(\mathbf b^{(1)}:4\times1\), \(W^{(2)}:4\times4\), \(\mathbf b^{(2)}:4\times1\), \(W^{(3)}:2\times4\), \(\mathbf b^{(3)}:2\times1\). (b) 46 numbers.

Problem 2easyforward pass

A 2 → 2 → 1 network has a ReLU hidden layer and a plain (no bend) output: \[W^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\ 2&1\end{pmatrix},\ \mathbf b^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\ -3\end{pmatrix},\qquad W^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}3&-2\end{pmatrix},\ b^{(2)}=4.\] Find \(\mathbf z^{(1)}\), \(\mathbf a^{(1)}\) and \(\hat y\) for (a) \(\mathbf x=(2,1)\) and (b) \(\mathbf x=(1,3)\).

What this tests. Row-times-column, the shift, and the ReLU switch. Plan. One row per hidden neuron; check each sign before bending.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a), the mix. \[\begin{aligned}\text{row 1: }&(1)(2)+(-1)(1)+0=1\\ \text{row 2: }&(2)(2)+(1)(1)-3=2\end{aligned}\] So \(\mathbf z^{(1)}=(1,2)\).
Step 2 — (a), bend and output. Both positive, so \(\mathbf a^{(1)}=(1,2)\). Then \(\hat y=(3)(1)+(-2)(2)+4=3-4+4=3\).

Step 4 — (b), bend and output. The first neuron is asleep: \(\mathbf a^{(1)}=(0,2)\). Then \(\hat y=(3)(0)+(-2)(2)+4=0\).

Which neurons are awake depends on the input. The same network uses different "active wires" for different people.

Use the central difference \((L(\theta+h)-L(\theta-h))/2h\): its error shrinks like \(h^2\), the one-sided one only like \(h\).

Problem 11easyfading blame

A chain of 6 one-neuron sigmoid layers. (a) If every \(|w|\le2\), what is the most the blame can keep, as a fraction, from top to bottom? (b) And if every \(|w|\le1\)? (c) What is the factor through 6 awake ReLU layers with every \(w=1\)?

What this tests. The blame is a product of (slope × weight) per layer. Plan. Sigmoid's slope ≤ 0.25; ReLU's slope = 1 when awake.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). Each layer: at most \(0.25\times2=0.5\). Six layers: \(0.5^6=1/64\approx0.0156\).
Step 2 — (b). Each layer at most \(0.25\): \(0.25^6=1/4096\approx0.000244\).
Step 3 — (c). Each layer exactly \(1\times1=1\), so the factor is 1: nothing fades.

answers at a glance: (a) \(1/64\approx0.0156\). (b) \(1/4096\approx0.000244\). (c) 1.

Remember

Blame through \(k\) layers is a product of \(k\) factors. Keep each factor near 1.

Problem 12mediumdead neurons

A ReLU layer of 3 neurons sees a batch of 4 inputs \(\mathbf x_1=(1,0)\), \(\mathbf x_2=(2,1)\), \(\mathbf x_3=(0,1)\), \(\mathbf x_4=(1,1)\). Its pre-activations (rows = neurons, columns = inputs) and the incoming blames \(\partial L/\partial a\) are \[Z=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0.5&3\\ -3&-0.2&-1&-4\\ -1&-2&1&-0.5\end{pmatrix},\quad G=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&1\\ 0.5&-0.5&2&1\\ 0.4&-0.2&0.6&1\end{pmatrix}.\] (a) Which neuron is dead on this batch? (b) Find each neuron's bias gradient, summed over the batch. (c) Find each neuron's weight gradients, summed over the batch.

What this tests. Rule A per input, then Rule B summed over a batch. Plan. Mask \(G\) by the signs of \(Z\); sum rows for the biases; for the weights add (masked blame) × input.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the switches. Neuron 1 is awake on inputs 1, 3, 4. Neuron 2 is asleep on every input — dead. Neuron 3 is awake only on input 3.
Step 2 — masked blame. Neuron 1: \((1,0,1,1)\). Neuron 2: \((0,0,0,0)\). Neuron 3: \((0,0,0.6,0)\).
Step 3 — biases (b). Sum each row: \(3,\ 0,\ 0.6\).
Step 4 — weights (c). Neuron 1: \(1\cdot(1,0)+1\cdot(0,1)+1\cdot(1,1)=(2,2)\). Neuron 2: \((0,0)\). Neuron 3: \(0.6\cdot(0,1)=(0,0.6)\).

answers at a glance: (a) neuron 2. (b) \((3,\ 0,\ 0.6)\). (c) \((2,2)\), \((0,0)\), \((0,0.6)\).

Remember

A neuron asleep on the whole batch gets exactly zero gradient, so plain gradient descent can never wake it. That is a dead ReLU.

Problem 13mediumwide or deep

Two ReLU networks with one input and one output. A: one hidden layer of 16 neurons (1 → 16 → 1). B: five hidden layers of 2 neurons (1 → 2 → 2 → 2 → 2 → 2 → 1). (a) Count each network's numbers. (b) At most how many straight pieces can A make? (c) Using the tent fold, how many can B make?

What this tests. Parameter counting, and "width adds creases, depth folds the folds". Plan. Count per layer; A has one crease per neuron; B doubles per layer.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — count A. \(16\) weights + \(16\) biases in, \(16\) weights + \(1\) bias out: \(49\).
Step 2 — count B. First layer \(2+2=4\). Four \(2\to2\) layers, \(4+2=6\) each: \(24\). Output \(2+1=3\). Total \(31\).
Step 3 — pieces of A. 16 creases cut the line into at most \(17\) straight pieces.
Step 4 — pieces of B. Each layer of 2 ReLUs makes one tent, \(t(x)=2\operatorname{ReLU}(x)-4\operatorname{ReLU}(x-\tfrac12)\), and each tent doubles the pieces: \(2^5=32\).

answers at a glance: (a) A: 49, B: 31. (b) 17. (c) 32.

Remember

Width adds pieces one crease at a time. Depth multiplies them. That is one reason deep networks are worth their trouble.

Problem 14harddiagnose the run

The salary network of this unit was trained three times from the same start with three step sizes. The recorded losses:

\(\eta\)step 0step 1step 2step 3step 10
\(10^{-5}\)5018.897.072.620.0024
\(10^{-4}\)50428.811078.76841.740.0157
\(2\times10^{-3}\)50624412125012501250
(a) Which run would you trust, and why? (b) What is \(\hat y\) in the third run from step 2 on? (c) Why can the third run never recover? (d) What is the real fix?

What this tests. Reading a loss curve like a doctor (Unit 10), and connecting it to dead ReLUs. Plan. Look for monotone falls, jumps, and a loss that freezes at a suspicious value.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(\eta=10^{-5}\): the loss falls every step, by a factor of about 2.7 each time. The \(10^{-4}\) run jumps up to 429 and then 1079 — it overshoots and bounces; it happens to settle by step 10, but you would not bet on it. Trust \(10^{-5}\).
Step 2 — (b). A loss of exactly 1250 means \(\tfrac12(\hat y-50)^2=1250\), so \(\hat y=0\) or \(\hat y=100\). If it were 100, the blame \(\hat y-y=+50\) would move the weights and the loss would change. It is frozen, so nothing is moving: \(\hat y=0\).
Step 3 — (c). The giant first step (loss 624 412, \(\hat y\approx1168\)) pushed the first-layer weights so far that every hidden-1 neuron now has a negative \(z\): all asleep. Rule A blocks all blame, every gradient before them is 0, and \(\hat y\) stays 0. The network is dead.
Step 4 — (d). Scale the inputs (age/100, experience/10). Then the first-layer gradients are no longer 30 times inflated, and a much larger \(\eta\) (like \(5\times10^{-4}\)) trains smoothly.

answers at a glance: (a) \(\eta=10^{-5}\) (steady fall). (b) \(\hat y=0\). (c) every first-layer neuron is asleep, so all gradients are 0. (d) scale the inputs.

Remember

A loss frozen at a round number is a clue. \(\tfrac12y^2\) means the network is outputting 0 — often because its ReLUs have died.
