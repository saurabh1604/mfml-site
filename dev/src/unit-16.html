Words as Vectors

A computer cannot read. To a computer, a word is just a label, like a code number in a shopkeeper's register. In this unit we give every word a short list of numbers — a place in space — so that words with similar meanings live close together. We start by counting which words follow which, and learn how to score a guesser. Then we count which words keep company, squeeze that big table with the SVD, and let a machine learn the lists by playing a guessing game. At the end, meaning turns out to be a direction: king − man + woman lands next to queen.

Why a computer can't read a word

Imagine this

A new shopkeeper opens a stock register. He gives every item a code number: 1 is chai, 2 is coffee, 3 is a cricket bat.

Is 2 "closer" to 1 than 3 is? No. The numbers are just labels. They say nothing about what the items are. Chai and coffee are both hot drinks, but the register cannot know that.

A computer sees words the same way. To a computer, "chai" is just a label. So our first job is to turn each word into numbers the computer can work with.

The simplest way is to give each word its own slot. Take a tiny vocabulary of three words: chai, coffee, cricket. Each word becomes a list of three numbers with a single 1 in its own slot:

\[\text{chai}=(1,0,0),\quad \text{coffee}=(0,1,0),\quad \text{cricket}=(0,0,1).\]

A list of zeros with a single 1 — people call it a one-hot vector. With a real vocabulary of 50 000 words, each list is 50 000 numbers long, with one 1 and 49 999 zeros.

Now measure how alike two words are, the way Unit 3 taught us: with the dot product. Multiply slot by slot and add:

\[\text{chai}\cdot\text{coffee}=1\cdot0+0\cdot1+0\cdot0=0.\]

And chai with cricket? Also 0. Every pair of different one-hot words has dot product 0. They all stand at right angles to each other. The distance between any two of them is the same too: \(\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt2\approx1.414\).

So in one-hot land, chai is exactly as far from coffee as it is from cricket. The lists carry a name, but no meaning.

The realization

One-hot lists put every word at right angles to every other word, all the same distance apart. They tell words apart, but they cannot say "these two are alike". We want short lists where close means similar. The rest of this unit is about finding them.

Prove it · every pair of one-hot words is √2 apart

Claim. For one-hot vectors \(\mathbf e_i\) and \(\mathbf e_j\) with \(i\ne j\): \(\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf e_j=0\) and \(\lVert\mathbf e_i-\mathbf e_j\rVert=\sqrt2\), whatever the vocabulary size \(V\).

1
The dot product adds \(V\) products, one per slot. In every slot at least one of the two lists has a 0, because their single 1s sit in different slots. So every product is 0 and the sum is 0. Right angles: the cosine is 0 too.
2
The difference \(\mathbf e_i-\mathbf e_j\) has \(+1\) in slot \(i\), \(-1\) in slot \(j\) and 0 elsewhere. So \[\begin{aligned}&\lVert\mathbf e_i-\mathbf e_j\rVert^2\\ &=1^2+(-1)^2=2.\end{aligned}\] ∎ Or use \(\lVert\mathbf a-\mathbf b\rVert^2=\lVert\mathbf a\rVert^2+\lVert\mathbf b\rVert^2-2\,\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=1+1-0\).

The road ahead. We will find good word lists in four steps.

  1. Guess by counting (§2–§3): count which word follows which, and learn to score a guesser by how surprised it is — its perplexity.
  2. Know a word by its company (§4–§5): count which words appear near each other, then squeeze that big table with the SVD from Unit 5.
  3. Learn the lists (§6–§8): let a machine learn them by playing a yes/no guessing game (word2vec), see why that game is so cheap, and build a small neural network that reads two words and guesses the next.
  4. The geometry of meaning (§9–§11): meaning as a direction, king − man + woman, what the lists get wrong, and how best to measure "similar".

In one sentence: A one-hot list gives every word its own slot, so every pair of words is at right angles and \(\sqrt2\) apart — it names words but carries no meaning.

Guess the next word by counting

Imagine this

You type "good" on your phone, and the keyboard offers "morning". How does it know? It has seen you, and millions of others, type "good morning" many times. It simply remembers what usually comes next.

Let us build that keyboard by hand. Here is a tiny collection of text — people call a collection like this a corpus:

I drink chai .  ·  I drink coffee .  ·  I drink chai .  ·  I play cricket .

The full stop counts as a word too: it means "the sentence ends here". Now count every pair of neighbours. "I" is followed by "drink" 3 times and by "play" once. "drink" is followed by "chai" 2 times and by "coffee" once.

To guess what follows "drink", divide each count by the row total:

\[\begin{aligned}P(\text{chai}\mid\text{drink})&=\frac{2}{3},\\ P(\text{coffee}\mid\text{drink})&=\frac13.\end{aligned}\]

Read \(P(\text{chai}\mid\text{drink})\) as "the chance that the next word is chai, given that this word is drink". A model that looks only at the one word before is called a bigram model ("bi" = two words at a time). Looking at two words before gives a trigram, and in general an n-gram.

To score a whole sentence, multiply the guesses along it. Every sentence here starts with "I", so

\[\begin{aligned}&P(\text{I drink chai .})\\ &=P(\text{drink}\mid\text{I})\,P(\text{chai}\mid\text{drink})\,P(.\mid\text{chai})\\ &=\frac34\cdot\frac23\cdot1=\frac12.\end{aligned}\]

The zero problem. What does the model say about "I drink cricket ."? The pair "drink cricket" was never seen, so its count is 0, and the whole sentence gets probability 0. One unseen pair wipes out the whole sentence. With real text most possible pairs are never seen, so this happens all the time.

The simplest fix is add-one smoothing: pretend every pair was seen one extra time. Our vocabulary has 7 words (I, drink, play, chai, coffee, cricket and the full stop), so the row for "drink" gains 7 extra counts:

\[\begin{aligned}P(\text{chai}\mid\text{drink})&=\frac{2+1}{3+7}=0.3,\\ P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{drink})&=\frac{0+1}{3+7}=0.1.\end{aligned}\]

No zeros any more. The price: the words we really saw lose a little of their share.

The realization

\[P(w_{\text{next}}\mid w)=\frac{\text{count}(w\,w_{\text{next}})}{\text{count}(w)}\]

A table of counts is already a language model. But it has two weak spots. Any pair it never saw gets probability 0 (smoothing patches this). And it learns every word on its own: seeing "drink chai" teaches it nothing about "drink coffee", because to a count table, chai and coffee are just two different labels — the one-hot problem again.

If you want the algebra · 2 proofs, step by step
Prove it · count ÷ total is the best guess

Claim. After a word \(w\), the next words were seen with counts \(c_1,\dots,c_V\) (total \(n\)). The probabilities \(p_1,\dots,p_V\) that make the seen text most likely are \(p_j=c_j/n\).

1
The text's probability contains \(p_j\) once for every time word \(j\) followed \(w\). Take the log (Unit 14's likelihood): \[\begin{aligned}\ell=\ &c_1\ln p_1+\dots\\ &+c_V\ln p_V.\end{aligned}\] Maximise \(\ell\) with the rule \(p_1+\dots+p_V=1\).
2
Add a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\) for the rule and set each slope to zero: \[\frac{c_j}{p_j}-\lambda=0\ \Rightarrow\ p_j=\frac{c_j}{\lambda}.\] The same trick as fitting a Gaussian in Unit 14.
3
The rule \(\sum p_j=1\) gives \(\lambda=\sum c_j=n\). So \(p_j=c_j/n\). ∎ With our corpus: after "drink" the counts are chai 2, coffee 1, so \(p=(2/3,\,1/3)\).
Prove it · add-one still adds up to one

Claim. With add-one smoothing, \(p_j=\dfrac{c_j+1}{n+V}\), and these \(V\) numbers add up to 1.

1
Add the tops: \[\begin{aligned}&\sum_{j=1}^{V}(c_j+1)\\ &=\Big(\sum_j c_j\Big)+V\\ &=n+V.\end{aligned}\] One extra count for each of the \(V\) possible next words.
2
So \(\sum_j p_j=\dfrac{n+V}{n+V}=1\), and no \(p_j\) is zero because every top is at least 1. ∎ After "drink" (\(n=3\), \(V=7\)): \(\tfrac{3}{10}+\tfrac{2}{10}+5\cdot\tfrac{1}{10}=1\).

In one sentence: A bigram model guesses the next word as count(pair) ÷ count(word), multiplies the guesses along a sentence, and needs smoothing so that unseen pairs do not get probability zero.

Scoring a language model: perplexity

Imagine this

At the railway station the speaker says: "The train to Delhi is running…". Before the next word comes, you already guess "late". If it is "late", you are not surprised at all. If it is "early", you are very surprised.

A good language model is one that is rarely surprised by real text.

In Unit 14 we measured surprise. If the model gave the word that really came next a probability \(p\), its surprise is \(\log_2(1/p)\) bits. Probability \(\tfrac12\) → 1 bit. Probability \(\tfrac14\) → 2 bits. Probability 1 → 0 bits: no surprise at all.

Let a model read four words of real text. It gave the words that really came next these probabilities: \(0.5,\ 0.25,\ 0.5,\ 0.125\). The surprises are 1, 2, 1 and 3 bits. The average surprise is

\[\frac{1+2+1+3}{4}=1.75\text{ bits}.\]

This average is the cross-entropy of Unit 14. It is hard to feel what "1.75 bits" means, so we turn it back into a count of choices:

\[2^{1.75}\approx3.364.\]

This number is the perplexity. It says: on average, the model was as unsure as someone choosing between about 3.4 equally likely words. Lower is better. A perfect model has perplexity 1 (one sure choice).

A model that knows nothing and spreads its guess evenly over a vocabulary of \(V\) words gives every word \(1/V\). Its surprise is \(\log_2V\) every time, so its perplexity is exactly \(V\). That is the worst sensible score, and a useful yardstick: a model with perplexity 120 on a 50 000-word vocabulary has narrowed each guess from 50 000 choices to about 120.

The realization

\[\begin{aligned}\text{perplexity}&=2^{\,\frac1N\sum_{t}\log_2\frac{1}{p_t}}\\ &=\Big(\prod_{t=1}^{N}p_t\Big)^{-1/N}\end{aligned}\]

Perplexity is the average surprise, turned back into a number of equally likely choices. It is 1 for a perfect model and \(V\) for a model that guesses evenly over \(V\) words. Because it averages logs, one word the model thought nearly impossible can ruin the score — so a good model never says "impossible".

Prove it · perplexity is one over the average probability, the geometric way

Claim. \(2^{\frac1N\sum_t\log_2(1/p_t)}=\big(p_1p_2\cdots p_N\big)^{-1/N}\), and the answer is the same if you use natural logs and \(e\) instead of \(\log_2\) and 2.

1
A sum of logs is the log of a product: \[\begin{aligned}&\frac1N\sum_t\log_2\frac1{p_t}\\ &=\log_2\Big(\prod_t p_t\Big)^{-1/N}.\end{aligned}\] \(\log(xy)=\log x+\log y\) and \(k\log x=\log x^k\).
2
Raising 2 to \(\log_2(\text{something})\) gives back the something. The same holds with \(e\) and \(\ln\), so the base does not matter. ∎ Check: \((0.5\cdot0.25\cdot0.5\cdot0.125)^{-1/4}=(1/128)^{-1/4}=128^{1/4}\approx3.364\).
Prove it · guessing evenly gives perplexity V

Claim. A model that gives every one of \(V\) words probability \(1/V\) has perplexity exactly \(V\) on any text.

1
Every word, whatever it is, gets \(p_t=1/V\). So the product of \(N\) of them is \(V^{-N}\). The model does not look at the text at all.
2
\(\big(V^{-N}\big)^{-1/N}=V\). ∎ A fair die with \(V\) faces. Any model worth training should beat this.

In one sentence: Perplexity is 2 raised to the average surprise in bits — the number of equally likely words the model is as unsure as — so 1 is perfect, \(V\) is knowing nothing, and 0.5, 0.25, 0.5, 0.125 gives about 3.364.

You shall know a word by the company it keeps

Imagine this

You hear a word you do not know: "kadak". Then you hear it used: "a cup of kadak chai", "I like my chai kadak", "kadak and sweet, please". You still have no dictionary. But you already know it has something to do with tea.

You learned the word from its neighbours. A linguist named J. R. Firth put it in one line in 1957: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps."

Let us turn that idea into numbers. Pick some target words (chai, coffee, cricket, football) and some neighbour words (drink, hot, play, match). Read a corpus. Each time a neighbour word appears within 2 words of a target word, add 1 to their box. This window of "2 words either side" is called the window size.

Here is the table we get from a small made-up corpus (the widget below shows the sentences):

drinkhotplaymatch
chai5401
coffee4500
cricket0054
football0145

This is a co-occurrence matrix: "how often do these two words occur together". Now read each row as a vector. Chai is \((5,4,0,1)\). Coffee is \((4,5,0,0)\). Cricket is \((0,0,5,4)\). These are our first word vectors, and they are no longer one-hot: chai and coffee share neighbours, so their rows look alike.

How alike? Use the angle between the rows, from Unit 3. The cosine of that angle is

\[\cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf b\rVert}.\]

For chai and coffee: the dot product is \(5\cdot4+4\cdot5+0+0=40\). The lengths are \(\sqrt{42}\) and \(\sqrt{41}\). So

\[\cos(\text{chai},\text{coffee})=\frac{40}{\sqrt{42}\,\sqrt{41}}\approx0.964.\]

For chai and cricket the dot product is only \(1\cdot4=4\), so the cosine is \(4/(\sqrt{42}\sqrt{41})\approx0.096\). And cricket with football gives \(40/(\sqrt{41}\sqrt{42})\approx0.964\) again. The two tea words point almost the same way. The two sport words point almost the same way. The two groups are nearly at right angles.

Why the cosine and not the plain dot product? A word that appears ten times as often has counts ten times as big — a longer arrow — but the same kind of company. The cosine ignores the length and keeps only the direction: which company a word keeps, not how often it talks.

If you want the algebra · 1 proof, step by step
Prove it · the cosine ignores how often a word is used

Claim. For any numbers \(s,t>0\): \(\cos(s\mathbf a,\,t\mathbf b)=\cos(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)\).

1
Scaling pulls out of the dot product and out of the lengths: \[\begin{aligned}(s\mathbf a)\cdot(t\mathbf b)&=st\,(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b),\\ \lVert s\mathbf a\rVert\,\lVert t\mathbf b\rVert&=st\,\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf b\rVert.\end{aligned}\] Lengths of positive multiples scale by the same factor.
2
The factor \(st\) cancels in the ratio. ∎ So chai's row \((10,8,0,2)\) and \((5,4,0,1)\) have the same cosine with coffee: \(80/(\sqrt{168}\sqrt{41})=40/(\sqrt{42}\sqrt{41})\approx0.964\).

In one sentence: Count how often each word appears near each neighbour word; each row is a word vector, and the cosine of the angle between rows — 0.964 for chai and coffee, 0.096 for chai and cricket — measures how alike their company is.

Squeeze it with the SVD

Imagine this

A school gives a long survey with 100 questions. When the teacher reads the answers, she notices that most of them can be summed up in two scores: "loves sport" and "loves music". Two numbers per student say almost everything the 100 answers say.

A co-occurrence table is like that survey. It is huge, but most of what it says fits in a few numbers per word.

A real table has one row for every word and one column for every neighbour word: 50 000 × 50 000, and mostly zeros. Rows that long are clumsy. We want short rows that keep the pattern and drop the noise.

The tool is the SVD from Unit 5. It writes any matrix as \(C=U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}\): a turn, a stretch by the singular values \(\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots\), and another turn. The big singular values are the strong patterns. The small ones are mostly noise. Keep only the top \(k\) and you get the best rank-\(k\) copy of the table (Unit 5's layer cake).

For our 4 × 4 table the singular values are

\[\sigma\approx(9.528,\ 8.528,\ 1.143,\ 1.087).\]

Two big, two small. How much do the top two keep? Measure a table's "energy" as the sum of the squares of all its entries — here \(42+41+41+42=166\). That is also \(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2+\sigma_3^2+\sigma_4^2\). The top two give \(9.528^2+8.528^2\approx163.5\), which is

\[\frac{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2+\dots+\sigma_4^2}\approx\frac{163.5}{166}\approx98.5\%\]

of the energy. If we rebuild the table from just those two, the error (the Frobenius size of what is left over) is \(\sqrt{\sigma_3^2+\sigma_4^2}\approx1.578\) — small next to entries of 4 and 5.

Each word now gets just two numbers: its row of \(U_2\Sigma_2\).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{chai}&\approx(5.009,\,-4.032)\\ \text{coffee}&\approx(4.530,\,-4.452)\\ \text{cricket}&\approx(4.450,\,4.540)\\ \text{football}&\approx(5.037,\,4.004)\end{aligned}\]

(The SVD is free to flip the sign of any direction, so another program may print some of these with the opposite sign. The angles do not change.) Read them: the first number is about 5 for every word — it says "this word is used a lot". The second number is the interesting one: negative for tea, positive for sport. In two dimensions, \(\cos(\text{chai},\text{coffee})\approx0.995\) and \(\cos(\text{chai},\text{cricket})\approx0.097\). The squeeze made the tea words even more alike — it threw away the noise that kept them apart.

One more fix: common words shout. In real text the word "the" sits near everything. Its column is huge, so raw counts mostly measure "how common is this word". The fix is to ask a better question: does this pair appear together more often than chance would give? If chai and "hot" together are 3 times more common than you would expect from how common each one is alone, that is interesting; if "the" and chai are just as common together as chance predicts, that is nothing. Take the log of that ratio and keep only positive values — people call it PPMI (positive pointwise mutual information). The formula is in the drawer below. The realization

\[\begin{gathered}C\approx U_k\Sigma_kV_k^{\mathsf T},\\ \text{word vectors}=\text{rows of }U_k\Sigma_k\end{gathered}\]

The SVD finds the few strong directions hiding in a big table of counts. Keep those, drop the rest, and each word gets a short list of numbers. The energy kept, \(\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i^2/\sum_i\sigma_i^2\), tells you how much of the table survived. Weighting the counts by PPMI first stops common words from hogging the first direction.

If you want the algebra · 3 proofs, step by step
Prove it · the energy of a table is the sum of σ²

Claim. For any matrix \(C=U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}\), the sum of the squares of all entries equals \(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2+\cdots\).

1
The sum of squares of all entries is \(\operatorname{tr}(C^{\mathsf T}C)\): the diagonal of \(C^{\mathsf T}C\) holds the squared lengths of the columns. \(\operatorname{tr}\) means "add the diagonal".
2
Substitute the SVD and use \(U^{\mathsf T}U=I\): \[C^{\mathsf T}C=V\Sigma^{\mathsf T}\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}.\] A trace does not change when you turn the space (\(\operatorname{tr}(VMV^{\mathsf T})=\operatorname{tr}(M)\) for orthogonal \(V\)), so the trace is \(\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{\mathsf T}\Sigma)=\sum\sigma_i^2\). ∎ Our table: \(166\) entry-by-entry, and \(9.528^2+8.528^2+1.143^2+1.087^2\approx166\).
Prove it · what is lost when you keep k directions

Claim. Keeping the top \(k\) terms, \(C_k=\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i^{\mathsf T}\), leaves an error of size \(\lVert C-C_k\rVert_F=\sqrt{\sigma_{k+1}^2+\sigma_{k+2}^2+\cdots}\), and no other rank-\(k\) matrix does better.

1
The SVD is a sum of layers: \(C=\sum_i\sigma_i\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i^{\mathsf T}\). So the leftover is \(C-C_k=\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i^{\mathsf T}\), which is itself an SVD with singular values \(\sigma_{k+1},\sigma_{k+2},\dots\) The layers are the ones of Unit 5's layer cake.
2
By the energy proof above, its squared size is \(\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2\). That no rank-\(k\) matrix does better is the Eckart–Young theorem; its proof is in Unit 12.
3
For our table, \(k=2\): \(\sqrt{1.143^2+1.087^2}\approx1.578\). ∎ The energy kept is \(1-\text{error}^2/\text{total}=1-2.49/166\approx98.5\%\).
Prove it · PPMI, worked on one box

Claim. \(\operatorname{PMI}(w,c)=\log_2\dfrac{P(w,c)}{P(w)\,P(c)}\) measures "together more often than chance", and PPMI keeps only positive values: \(\max(0,\operatorname{PMI})\).

1
If \(w\) and \(c\) had nothing to do with each other, the chance of seeing them together would be \(P(w)P(c)\) (independence, Unit 14). The ratio says how many times more often they really appear together. Ratio 1 → log 0 → "nothing special".
2
In our 4 × 4 table the total count is 166. Chai's row adds to 42, the "drink" column to 9, and the box holds 5: \[\begin{aligned}&\frac{P(w,c)}{P(w)P(c)}\\ &=\frac{5/166}{(42/166)(9/166)}\\ &=\frac{5\cdot166}{42\cdot9}\approx2.196.\end{aligned}\] Chai and "drink" meet about 2.2 times more often than chance.
3
So \(\operatorname{PMI}\approx\log_2 2.196\approx1.135\) bits, and PPMI is the same. A box with count 0 would give \(\log 0=-\infty\), which PPMI turns into 0. ∎ A column like "the", which appears with everyone about as often as chance predicts, gets PMI near 0 everywhere — and stops dominating.

In one sentence: The SVD squeezes a co-occurrence table to its top \(k\) directions; the rows of \(U_k\Sigma_k\) are short word vectors, the energy kept is \(\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i^2/\sum\sigma_i^2\) (98.5% for our table at \(k=2\)), and PPMI stops common words from taking over.

Learning vectors instead of counting: word2vec

Imagine this

Two friends play a game on a long train ride. One reads a word from a newspaper — "chai" — and then a second word. Sometimes the second word really was next to "chai" in the paper ("hot"). Sometimes it is a random word picked from anywhere ("football"). The other friend must say: real neighbour, or random?

To win this game again and again, you have to learn what chai is like. That is the whole idea of word2vec.

Counting and squeezing works, but the table is enormous and the SVD of a 50 000 × 50 000 table is slow. In 2013, Tomas Mikolov and his team at Google tried another way: skip the table, and learn the short lists directly by playing the guessing game above. Their method is called word2vec, and the game version is called skip-gram with negative sampling.

Each word gets two lists of numbers: \(\mathbf v\) for when it is the middle word, and \(\mathbf u\) for when it is a neighbour. The game's score for a pair is the dot product \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v\), squeezed into a probability with the sigmoid from Unit 14:

\[P(\text{real neighbour})=\sigma(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}}.\]

A big positive dot product means "surely real". A big negative one means "surely random". For each real pair we also pick a few random words — the negatives — and want the model to say "random" to them.

A worked round. Use lists of just two numbers. Middle word chai: \(\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}=(1,0)\). A real neighbour, hot: \(\mathbf u_{\text{hot}}=(0.5,0.5)\). A random negative, football: \(\mathbf u_{\text{football}}=(-0.5,1)\).

  • Real pair: \(\mathbf u_{\text{hot}}\cdot\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}=0.5\), so \(\sigma(0.5)\approx0.622\). The model is 62% sure hot is real. Good, but not great.
  • Negative pair: \(\mathbf u_{\text{football}}\cdot\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}=-0.5\). The chance it says "random" is \(\sigma(+0.5)\approx0.622\).
  • The loss is the surprise of both right answers (in nats, as in Unit 14): \(-\ln0.622-\ln0.622\approx0.948\).

Now take one step downhill (Unit 9) with step size \(\eta=1\). Each guess is "wrong by" \(1-0.622\approx0.378\). The update rules turn out to be very simple (the drawer has the algebra):

  • Pull the real neighbour toward chai: \(\mathbf u_{\text{hot}}\leftarrow\mathbf u_{\text{hot}}+0.378\,\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}=(0.878,\ 0.5)\).
  • Push the random word away from chai: \(\mathbf u_{\text{football}}\leftarrow\mathbf u_{\text{football}}-0.378\,\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}=(-0.878,\ 1)\).
  • Turn chai toward hot and away from football: \(\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}\leftarrow\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}+0.378\,(\mathbf u_{\text{hot}}-\mathbf u_{\text{football}})=(1.378,\ -0.189)\).

After this one step, the real pair scores \(\sigma(1.114)\approx0.753\), the negative pair gives "random" with \(\sigma(1.398)\approx0.802\), and the loss has dropped from 0.948 to about 0.505.

Why not a softmax over every word?

Imagine this

A teacher wants to know if you understand who chai's friends are. She could ask: "Out of all 50 000 students in this city, rank how likely each one is to be chai's friend." That takes all day.

Or she could ask five quick yes/no questions: "Is hot a friend? Is football a friend? Is kettle?…" Five answers, and she learns nearly as much.

There is a more obvious way to play word2vec's game. Given the middle word chai, ask the model to pick the neighbour out of the whole vocabulary, with a softmax from Unit 14:

\[P(\text{hot}\mid\text{chai})=\frac{e^{\mathbf u_{\text{hot}}\cdot\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}}}{\sum_{w=1}^{V}e^{\mathbf u_{w}\cdot\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}}}.\]

This is correct, and it is exactly how a full language model ends (§8). The trouble is the bottom line. To compute it you need a dot product and an \(e^{(\cdot)}\) for every word in the vocabulary. With \(V=50\,000\), that is 50 000 dot products for one training pair. Worse, the gradient of a softmax touches every one of those 50 000 neighbour lists, so every step must update all of them.

Negative sampling replaces that one giant question with \(k+1\) small yes/no questions: the real neighbour plus \(k\) random words. With \(k=5\) that is 6 dot products and 6 sigmoids per pair:

\[\frac{50\,000}{6}\approx8\,333\ \text{times less work}.\]

word2vec was trained on billions of word pairs. That saving is the difference between a year and an afternoon.

Which random words? If negatives were picked in proportion to how often words appear, "the" and "is" would be picked all the time and teach little. word2vec picks a word with probability proportional to its count raised to the power \(\tfrac34\). That gently lifts rare words: a word seen 100 times and one seen once would share the picks 99% to 1%; after the \(\tfrac34\) power it is \(100^{0.75}\approx31.6\) against 1, about 97% to 3%.

A neural language model

Imagine this

Mumbai's dabbawalas pass a lunch box along a relay. One picks it up, one sorts it, one carries it, one hands it over. Nobody does the whole job, but the box always arrives.

A neural language model is a relay like that. Look up the words, glue them together, mix them in a small network, and hand over a probability for every possible next word.

In §2 we guessed the next word from a table of counts. The table could not share anything between "chai" and "coffee". In 2003, Yoshua Bengio and his team built a model that could. It works in five steps:

  1. Look up. Keep a table \(E\) with one row of \(d\) numbers for each word — an embedding table. Look up the row of each previous word.
  2. Glue. Put the rows side by side into one longer list (people say concatenate). Two previous words with \(d=2\) give a list of 4.
  3. Mix. Pass it through a hidden layer, exactly as in Unit 15: \(\mathbf h=\tanh(W\mathbf x+\mathbf b)\).
  4. Score every word. One more layer gives one score per vocabulary word, and a softmax (Unit 14) turns the scores into probabilities.
  5. Learn. Train with cross-entropy and backprop (Unit 15). The blame flows all the way back into the rows of \(E\), so the table is learned too.

The lookup is a matrix multiply. Write a word one-hot and multiply it by the table. With three words and \(d=2\):

\[\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\ 3&4\\ 5&6\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3&4\end{pmatrix}.\]

The single 1 picks out row 2. So an embedding table is nothing new: it is the first layer's weight matrix, fed with one-hot inputs. Computers skip the multiply and just fetch the row — same answer, far less work.

It guesses football after "I play" with probability about 0.256 — nearly what it gives after "we play" (0.258). That is sharing, and it is the whole point of word vectors.

The geometry of meaning

Imagine this

A friend gives you directions: "From the station, walk 2 blocks north and 3 blocks east." Those directions are not tied to the station. Start from the temple instead and follow the same walk — you land somewhere new, but in the same position relative to where you started.

In a good word space, the step from "man" to "woman" is a walk like that. Start the same walk from "king".

Here are four hand-made vectors with just two numbers each (we built them for this example; they are not trained):

\[\begin{aligned}\text{king}&=(3,2), & \text{man}&=(1,2),\\ \text{queen}&=(3,-2), & \text{woman}&=(1,-2).\end{aligned}\]

The step from man to woman is \(\text{woman}-\text{man}=(0,-4)\). The step from king to queen is \(\text{queen}-\text{king}=(0,-4)\). The same step. So the four points make a parallelogram, and we can use it to answer the puzzle "man is to woman as king is to ?":

\[\text{king}-\text{man}+\text{woman}=(3,2)-(1,2)+(1,-2)=(3,-2).\]

That is exactly queen. With real, trained vectors the answer is never exact; it lands near a word. So we search: which word's vector has the biggest cosine with the answer? For \((3,-2)\): queen 1, woman 0.868, king 0.385, man −0.124.

Skip the three words you started with. In trained vectors the step "woman − man" is small next to the words themselves, so the answer often lies closest to king itself. A search that allowed king would just return king — true, but useless. So the search always leaves out the three input words.

What embeddings get wrong

Imagine this

"He hit a six with his bat." "A bat flew out of the old fort at dusk." Same word, two completely different things.

Or: "We sat on the river bank." "I opened an account at the bank." You never mix them up, because the sentence tells you which one is meant.

Everything so far gives each word one vector, whatever the sentence. So what does "bat" get? The training game pulls it toward cricket words every time it appears in a cricket sentence, and toward animal words every time it appears in an animal sentence. It ends up roughly a blend of the two meanings, weighted by how often each one is used.

A tiny example. Let the cricket meaning point along \((1,0)\) and the animal meaning along \((0,1)\). If 80% of the sentences with "bat" are about cricket, the single vector is about

\[0.8\,(1,0)+0.2\,(0,1)=(0.8,\ 0.2).\]

Its cosine with the cricket meaning is \(0.8/\sqrt{0.68}\approx0.970\), and with the animal meaning only \(0.2/\sqrt{0.68}\approx0.243\). The rare meaning is almost drowned. And a word used half and half sits in the middle, close to neither group.

Vectors copy the text they learn from. The lists are a mirror of how words are used in the corpus — nothing more. If the training text more often writes "the doctor … he" and "the nurse … she", then "doctor" leans toward the male end of the female–male direction, and "nurse" toward the female end. That is a fact about the text, not about doctors or nurses. It matters because a system built on these vectors will repeat the pattern. People measure such leanings with exactly the directions you toggled in §9, and can remove a direction when it should not matter.

Both problems point to the same fix: a word's vector should depend on the sentence it sits in. That is where Units 17 and 18 go.

Measuring similarity well

Imagine this

Two customers at a tea stall. One buys 2 cups of chai and 1 biscuit. The other, buying for his family, takes 4 cups of chai and 2 biscuits. Their taste is identical — twice as much chai as biscuits. Only the size of the order differs.

A good "similar taste" measure should say they are the same.

We have met three ways to compare two lists. Take the orders \(\mathbf a=(2,1)\) and \(\mathbf b=(4,2)\), and a third customer who likes biscuits more, \(\mathbf c=(1,2)\).

  • Dot product: \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=10\), \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c=4\). It mixes taste with size: bigger orders give bigger numbers.
  • Distance (straight-line, Euclidean): \(\lVert\mathbf a-\mathbf b\rVert=\sqrt{2^2+1^2}\approx2.236\), but \(\lVert\mathbf a-\mathbf c\rVert=\sqrt{1^2+1^2}\approx1.414\). Distance says \(\mathbf a\) is closer to \(\mathbf c\) — the size difference beats the taste. (With an office order of 20 and 10 it would be worse: about 20.12 against 1.414.)
  • Cosine: \(\cos(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)=1\) exactly (same direction), \(\cos(\mathbf a,\mathbf c)=4/5=0.8\). Cosine sees only taste.

For word vectors, the length mostly tracks how often a word appears, and the direction tracks what it means. That is why cosine is the standard choice.

The peace treaty: normalise. Divide every vector by its length so it has length 1 (it sits on the unit circle). Then the dot product is the cosine, and the distance follows from it:

\[\lVert\hat{\mathbf a}-\hat{\mathbf b}\rVert^2=2-2\cos\theta.\]

Bigger cosine means smaller distance, so all three measures agree on who is nearest. Real systems normalise once and then use fast dot products.

Expand the square with the dot product: \[\begin{aligned}&\lVert\hat{\mathbf a}-\hat{\mathbf b}\rVert^2\\ &=\hat{\mathbf a}\cdot\hat{\mathbf a}-2\,\hat{\mathbf a}\cdot\hat{\mathbf b}+\hat{\mathbf b}\cdot\hat{\mathbf b}\\ &=1-2\cos\theta+1.\end{aligned}\] ∎ The distance grows exactly as the cosine shrinks, so the nearest word by distance is the nearest by cosine. At \(\cos\theta=0.5\): distance 1.

In one sentence: The dot product mixes size and direction, distance is fooled by size, cosine keeps only direction — and after normalising to length 1 all three agree, since \(\lVert\hat{\mathbf a}-\hat{\mathbf b}\rVert^2=2-2\cos\theta\).

What to carry forward

The whole unit fits on one card. Here it is, piece by piece.

One-hot is blind

Every pair of one-hot words has dot product 0 and distance \(\sqrt2\). Names, no meaning.

Count the next word

\(P(w'\mid w)=\text{count}(w\,w')/\text{count}(w)\). Add-one smoothing: \((c+1)/(n+V)\).

Perplexity

\(2^{\text{average surprise}}\): the number of equally likely words the model is as unsure as. 1 is perfect, \(V\) is clueless.

Company

A word's row of neighbour counts is a vector. Similar meaning = cosine near 1.

Squeeze

Keep the top \(k\) singular values: rows of \(U_k\Sigma_k\). Energy kept \(\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i^2/\sum\sigma_i^2\). PPMI tames common words.

Learn: word2vec

Score \(\sigma(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)\). Pull real neighbours in, push \(k\) random words out.

Cheap questions

A full softmax costs \(V\) dot products per pair; negative sampling costs \(k+1\).

Neural LM

Look up rows (one-hot × table), glue, hidden layer, softmax. The table is the first layer.

Meaning is a direction

\(A-B+C\), then nearest by cosine, skipping \(A,B,C\). One vector per word blends its meanings and copies its text.

Where this goes next.

  • Unit 17 · Machines with Memory. Our neural language model saw only the last two words. A recurrent network reads a sentence one word at a time and carries a running memory along, so it can use everything before — and the fading or exploding of that memory becomes an eigenvalue story (Unit 4).
  • Unit 18 · Attention and Transformers. The fix for "bat" and "bank": every word looks at every other word in its sentence, using the very dot products of this unit, and takes on a new vector that fits its context.
The realization

\[\text{king}-\text{man}+\text{woman}\approx\text{queen}\]

A word becomes a point in space, placed by the company it keeps — counted and squeezed, or learned by a guessing game. In that space, closeness is similarity and a difference of meaning is a direction. Every language model you will meet from here on starts by turning its words into such vectors.

In one sentence: Give each word a short list of numbers learned from the company it keeps — by counting and the SVD, or by word2vec's guessing game — and meaning becomes geometry: similar words point the same way and differences of meaning are directions.

Practice arena — fourteen problems, solved in full

Fourteen problems, easy to hard: one-hot lists, counting bigrams, perplexity forwards and backwards, cosines of count rows, singular values and a rank-1 copy, word2vec scores, a word2vec step, the cost of a softmax, an analogy, an embedding lookup, the size of a model, and two recorded models to judge. Every number here was checked by machine.

Three habits do most of the work. Write the counts or vectors in a small table first. Turn probabilities into surprises (bits) before averaging — never average the probabilities themselves. And for "similar", divide by the lengths: use the cosine, not the raw dot product.

Problem 1easyone-hot

A vocabulary has 5 words, in the order (tea, milk, bat, ball, run). (a) Write the one-hot vector of "bat". (b) Compute \(\mathbf e_{\text{milk}}\cdot\mathbf e_{\text{ball}}\) and \(\mathbf e_{\text{bat}}\cdot\mathbf e_{\text{bat}}\). (c) How far apart are "tea" and "run"? (d) With a vocabulary of 20 000 words, how many numbers does one one-hot vector hold, compared with a 300-number embedding?

What this tests. What a one-hot list is, and why it carries no meaning. Plan. One 1 in the word's own slot; dot products count shared slots.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). "bat" is word 3: \(\mathbf e_{\text{bat}}=(0,0,1,0,0)\).
Step 2 — (b). milk and ball have their 1s in different slots (2 and 4), so every product is 0: \(\mathbf e_{\text{milk}}\cdot\mathbf e_{\text{ball}}=0\). A word with itself shares its one slot: \(\mathbf e_{\text{bat}}\cdot\mathbf e_{\text{bat}}=1\).
Step 3 — (c). The difference is \((1,0,0,0,-1)\), so the distance is \(\sqrt{1+1}=\sqrt2\approx1.414\) — the same for every pair.
Step 4 — (d). One-hot: 20 000 numbers (19 999 of them zero). Embedding: 300 numbers, all useful. That is \(20\,000/300\approx66.7\) times shorter.

answers at a glance: (a) \((0,0,1,0,0)\). (b) 0 and 1. (c) \(\sqrt2\approx1.414\). (d) 20 000 against 300.

Remember

One-hot lists are all at right angles and all \(\sqrt2\) apart. They are names, not meanings.

Problem 2easybigrams

The corpus is "we play cricket .  we play football .  we watch cricket ." (every sentence starts with "we"; the full stop is a word). (a) Give \(P(\text{play}\mid\text{we})\), \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{play})\) and \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{watch})\). (b) What probability does the bigram model give "we play cricket ."? (c) With add-one smoothing over the 6-word vocabulary, give \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{play})\), \(P(\text{watch}\mid\text{play})\) and \(P(.\mid\text{cricket})\). (d) The smoothed probability of "we play cricket ."?

What this tests. Counting pairs, dividing by the row total, and add-one. Plan. Make the table of next-word counts first.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the counts. After "we": play 2, watch 1 (total 3). After "play": cricket 1, football 1 (total 2). After "watch": cricket 1 (total 1). After "cricket": "." 2 (total 2). After "football": "." 1.
Step 2 — (a). \(P(\text{play}\mid\text{we})=2/3\), \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{play})=1/2\), \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{watch})=1/1=1\).
Step 3 — (b). \(\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12\cdot P(.\mid\text{cricket})=\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12\cdot1=\tfrac13\).
Step 4 — (c). Add 1 to the count and \(V=6\) to the total: \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{play})=\tfrac{1+1}{2+6}=0.25\), \(P(\text{watch}\mid\text{play})=\tfrac{0+1}{2+6}=0.125\), \(P(.\mid\text{cricket})=\tfrac{2+1}{2+6}=0.375\).
Step 5 — (d). \(P(\text{play}\mid\text{we})=\tfrac{2+1}{3+6}=\tfrac13\), so the sentence gets \(\tfrac13\cdot0.25\cdot0.375=0.03125\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(2/3\), \(1/2\), \(1\). (b) \(1/3\). (c) 0.25, 0.125, 0.375. (d) 0.03125.

Remember

Smoothing moves probability from seen pairs to unseen ones — the seen sentence becomes less likely, but nothing is impossible any more.

Problem 3easyperplexity

A model reads five words. It gave the words that really came next the probabilities \(0.25,\ 0.5,\ 0.125,\ 0.5,\ 1\). (a) Find each surprise in bits and the average. (b) Find the perplexity. (c) Redo (a)–(b) in nats (natural logs, then \(e^{(\cdot)}\)) and check the perplexity is the same.

What this tests. Surprise, average surprise, perplexity. Plan. \(\log_2(1/p)\) for each word; average; raise 2 to it.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — surprises. \(\log_2 4=2\), \(\log_2 2=1\), \(\log_2 8=3\), \(1\), \(\log_2 1=0\).
Step 2 — average. \((2+1+3+1+0)/5=7/5=1.4\) bits.
Step 3 — perplexity. \(2^{1.4}\approx2.639\).
Step 4 — in nats. The average of \(\ln(1/p)\) is \(1.4\ln2\approx0.9704\) nats, and \(e^{0.9704}\approx2.639\). The same.

answers at a glance: (a) 2, 1, 3, 1, 0 bits; average 1.4 bits. (b) \(2^{1.4}\approx2.639\). (c) 0.9704 nats, \(e^{0.9704}\approx2.639\).

Remember

Perplexity does not depend on the base of the log — bits with 2, nats with \(e\).

Problem 4mediumperplexity, backwards

(a) A model guesses evenly over a vocabulary of 8 words. What is its perplexity? (b) A model always gives the true next word probability 0.2. Its perplexity? (c) On a 3-word text a model gave the first two true words probabilities 0.5 and 0.25. What probability must it give the third word for the perplexity to be exactly 4?

What this tests. Perplexity as \((p_1\cdots p_N)^{-1/N}\), and running it backwards. Plan. Write the product, set it equal, solve.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). Every word gets \(1/8\): perplexity \(8\).
Step 2 — (b). \((0.2^N)^{-1/N}=1/0.2=5\).
Step 3 — (c), set up. \((0.5\cdot0.25\cdot p)^{-1/3}=4\) means \(0.125\,p=4^{-3}=1/64\).
Step 4 — (c), solve. \(p=\dfrac{1/64}{1/8}=\dfrac18=0.125\). Check: surprises 1, 2, 3 bits, average 2, and \(2^2=4\).

answers at a glance: (a) 8. (b) 5. (c) \(p=0.125\).

Remember

A model that always gives the truth probability \(p\) has perplexity \(1/p\).

Problem 5easycosine of counts

Count rows over the neighbours (drink, hot, play): tea \(=(3,4,0)\), coffee \(=(6,8,0)\), cricket \(=(0,3,4)\). (a) Compute cos(tea, coffee) and cos(tea, cricket). (b) Compute the straight-line distances tea–coffee and tea–cricket. (c) Which measure says tea is closer to coffee?

What this tests. Cosine against distance on count rows. Plan. Lengths first: \(\lVert(3,4,0)\rVert=5\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — lengths. tea 5, coffee 10, cricket 5.
Step 2 — (a). tea·coffee \(=18+32=50\), so cos \(=50/(5\cdot10)=1\). tea·cricket \(=0+12+0=12\), so cos \(=12/25=0.48\).
Step 3 — (b). tea − coffee \(=(-3,-4,0)\): distance 5. tea − cricket \(=(3,1,-4)\): distance \(\sqrt{26}\approx5.099\).
Step 4 — (c). The cosine clearly says coffee (1 against 0.48). The distance barely prefers coffee (5 against 5.099) — coffee is just "tea, twice as often", and distance is confused by that size.

answers at a glance: (a) 1 and 0.48. (b) 5 and \(\sqrt{26}\approx5.099\). (c) the cosine, decisively.

Remember

Counts grow with how common a word is; the cosine divides that away.

Problem 6mediumsingular values

(a) Find the singular values of \(A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\ 1&3\end{pmatrix}\) and check that \(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2\) equals the sum of the squares of the entries. (b) A co-occurrence table has singular values \(6,\ 4,\ 2,\ 1\). What share of the energy do \(k=1\) and \(k=2\) keep? (c) What is the smallest \(k\) that keeps at least 90%? (d) What is the rank-2 rebuild error?

What this tests. Energy \(=\sum\sigma_i^2\), and the error of keeping \(k\). Plan. For a symmetric matrix with positive eigenvalues, the singular values are the eigenvalues.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(A\) is symmetric; its eigenvalues solve \((3-\lambda)^2=1\): \(\lambda=4,2\). Both positive, so \(\sigma=(4,2)\). Check: \(16+4=20=9+1+1+9\).
Step 2 — (b). Total \(36+16+4+1=57\). \(k=1\): \(36/57\approx63.2\%\). \(k=2\): \(52/57\approx91.2\%\).
Step 3 — (c). \(k=1\) is below 90%, \(k=2\) is above: the answer is 2.
Step 4 — (d). \(\sqrt{\sigma_3^2+\sigma_4^2}=\sqrt{4+1}=\sqrt5\approx2.236\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(\sigma=(4,2)\); \(16+4=20\). (b) 63.2% and 91.2%. (c) \(k=2\). (d) \(\sqrt5\approx2.236\).

Remember

Square the singular values before you add them — energy lives in \(\sigma^2\).

Problem 7mediumrank-1 copy

For \(A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\ 1&3\end{pmatrix}\) from Problem 6: (a) give the top singular vectors \(\mathbf u_1=\mathbf v_1\). (b) Build the best rank-1 copy \(A_1=\sigma_1\mathbf u_1\mathbf v_1^{\mathsf T}\). (c) Compute \(A-A_1\) and its Frobenius size, and compare with \(\sigma_2\). (d) Treat the rows of \(A\) as two word vectors. What single coordinate does each word get from \(U_1\Sigma_1\)?

What this tests. Building a low-rank copy by hand. Plan. The eigenvector for 4 solves \((A-4I)\mathbf x=0\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \((A-4I)\mathbf x=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\ 1&-1\end{pmatrix}\mathbf x=0\) gives \(\mathbf x\propto(1,1)\), so \(\mathbf u_1=\mathbf v_1=(1,1)/\sqrt2\).
Step 2 — (b). \(\mathbf u_1\mathbf v_1^{\mathsf T}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 1&1\end{pmatrix}\), so \(A_1=4\cdot\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\ 2&2\end{pmatrix}\).
Step 3 — (c). \(A-A_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\ -1&1\end{pmatrix}\), with size \(\sqrt{1+1+1+1}=2=\sigma_2\). Exactly as promised.
Step 4 — (d). \(U_1\Sigma_1=4\,\mathbf u_1=(4/\sqrt2,\ 4/\sqrt2)\approx(2.828,\ 2.828)\): both words get 2.828 — at \(k=1\) the difference between them is squeezed out.

answers at a glance: (a) \((1,1)/\sqrt2\). (b) \(\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\ 2&2\end{pmatrix}\). (c) \(\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\ -1&1\end{pmatrix}\), size 2 \(=\sigma_2\). (d) 2.828 each.

Remember

Each word's squeezed coordinates are its row of \(U_k\Sigma_k\); whatever the dropped directions carried is gone.

Problem 8mediumskip-gram scores

Middle word \(\mathbf v=(1,1)\). Real neighbour \(\mathbf u_+=(1,0)\). Two negatives: \(\mathbf u_1=(0,-1)\) and \(\mathbf u_2=(-1,-1)\). (a) Compute the three dot products. (b) The probability the model says "real" for the neighbour, and "random" for each negative. (c) The skip-gram loss in nats.

What this tests. The negative-sampling loss. Plan. Real pair: \(\sigma(z)\). Negative pair: \(\sigma(-z)\). Loss: minus the log of each, added.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(\mathbf u_+\cdot\mathbf v=1\), \(\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf v=-1\), \(\mathbf u_2\cdot\mathbf v=-2\).
Step 2 — (b). Real: \(\sigma(1)\approx0.7311\). Random for \(\mathbf u_1\): \(\sigma(1)\approx0.7311\). Random for \(\mathbf u_2\): \(\sigma(2)\approx0.8808\).
Step 3 — (c). \(-\ln0.7311-\ln0.7311-\ln0.8808\approx0.3133+0.3133+0.1269=0.7535\).

answers at a glance: (a) 1, −1, −2. (b) 0.7311, 0.7311, 0.8808. (c) \(\approx0.7535\).

Remember

For a negative, the right answer is "random", so its probability is \(\sigma(-\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)\) — the more negative the dot product, the better.

Problem 9hardone word2vec step

\(\mathbf v=(0,1)\), real neighbour \(\mathbf u_+=(1,1)\), one negative \(\mathbf u_-=(1,-1)\), step size \(\eta=0.5\). (a) Find \(g_+=1-\sigma(\mathbf u_+\cdot\mathbf v)\) and \(g_-=\sigma(\mathbf u_-\cdot\mathbf v)\). (b) Do one step of the pull-and-push rules for all three vectors. (c) Give the new scores \(\mathbf u_\pm\cdot\mathbf v\) and the loss before and after.

What this tests. The update rules \(\mathbf u_+\mathrel{+}=\eta g_+\mathbf v\), \(\mathbf u_-\mathrel{-}=\eta g_-\mathbf v\), \(\mathbf v\mathrel{+}=\eta(g_+\mathbf u_+-g_-\mathbf u_-)\), all using the OLD vectors. Plan. Compute the two "how wrong" numbers once, then update.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(\mathbf u_+\cdot\mathbf v=1\): \(g_+=1-\sigma(1)\approx0.2689\). \(\mathbf u_-\cdot\mathbf v=-1\): \(g_-=\sigma(-1)\approx0.2689\).
Step 2 — the neighbours. \(\mathbf u_+\leftarrow(1,1)+0.5(0.2689)(0,1)=(1,\ 1.1345)\). \(\mathbf u_-\leftarrow(1,-1)-0.5(0.2689)(0,1)=(1,\ -1.1345)\).
Step 3 — the middle word. \(g_+\mathbf u_+-g_-\mathbf u_-=0.2689\big((1,1)-(1,-1)\big)=(0,\ 0.5379)\), so \(\mathbf v\leftarrow(0,1)+0.5(0,0.5379)=(0,\ 1.2689)\).
Step 4 — (c). New scores: \(1.1345\times1.2689\approx1.4396\) and \(-1.4396\). Loss before: \(-2\ln\sigma(1)\approx0.6265\). After: \(-2\ln\sigma(1.4396)\approx0.4254\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(g_+=g_-\approx0.2689\). (b) \(\mathbf u_+=(1,1.1345)\), \(\mathbf u_-=(1,-1.1345)\), \(\mathbf v=(0,1.2689)\). (c) scores \(\pm1.4396\); loss 0.6265 → 0.4254.

Remember

Use the old vectors on the right-hand side of all three updates — compute first, then overwrite.

Problem 10mediumcost

Vocabulary \(V=30\,000\), vectors of \(d=300\) numbers, \(k=5\) negatives. A dot product of two 300-number lists costs 300 multiply-adds. (a) How many multiply-adds does one training pair cost with a full softmax (just the scores)? (b) With negative sampling? (c) The ratio. (d) For \(10^9\) training pairs, the totals.

What this tests. \(V\) dot products against \(k+1\). Plan. Multiply counts by 300.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(30\,000\times300=9\,000\,000\).
Step 2 — (b). \((5+1)\times300=1\,800\).
Step 3 — (c). \(9\,000\,000/1\,800=5\,000\) \(=V/(k+1)\).
Step 4 — (d). Full: \(9\times10^{15}\). Negative sampling: \(1.8\times10^{12}\).

answers at a glance: (a) 9 000 000. (b) 1 800. (c) 5 000. (d) \(9\times10^{15}\) against \(1.8\times10^{12}\).

Problem 11mediumanalogy

Toy vectors: India \(=(1,0,3)\), Delhi \(=(1,1.2,3)\), Japan \(=(3,0,1)\), Tokyo \(=(3,1,1)\), Kyoto \(=(3,1,2)\). (a) Compute \(\mathbf q=\) Delhi − India + Japan. (b) Compute the cosine of \(\mathbf q\) with Tokyo, Kyoto, Japan and Delhi. (c) Which word does the search return (skipping the inputs)? (d) Would the answer change if the inputs were allowed?

Step 2 — (b). Tokyo: \(11.2/(3.382\cdot\sqrt{11})\approx0.9984\). Kyoto: \(12.2/(3.382\cdot\sqrt{14})\approx0.9640\). Japan: \(10/(3.382\cdot\sqrt{10})\approx0.9349\). Delhi: \(7.44/(3.382\cdot\sqrt{11.44})\approx0.6503\).

Problem 12mediumlookup

A 4-word vocabulary has the table \(E=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\ 0&3\\ 1&1\\ 4&0\end{pmatrix}\). (a) Compute \(\mathbf e_3E\). (b) A model reads "word 2, word 4" and glues the rows. What is \(\mathbf x\)? (d) After one step with \(\eta=1\), give the new rows, and count how many of the 8 numbers of \(E\) changed.

Show the full solution

Step 2 — (b). \(\mathbf x=[\,(0,3);\,(4,0)\,]=(0,3,4,0)\).
Step 3 — (c). Row 2 gets \((0.1,-0.2)\), row 4 gets \((0.3,0.5)\); rows 1 and 3 get nothing.
