What survives the machine?

Imagine this

Your dataset has a thousand columns. Somewhere inside it, a handful of directions carry almost all the signal — customer taste, disease risk, market mood — and the rest is noise. PCA finds those directions by asking a strange-sounding question about a matrix: which directions does it refuse to turn, and how hard does it stretch along each one? By the end of this unit, that question — and its answer, \(A=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\) — will feel inevitable.

Units 1–3 gave us the machinery: matrices as machines that move space (Unit 1), the spaces they act on (Unit 2), and rulers to measure with (Unit 3). Now we interrogate the machine itself. If a matrix is a motion, what are its invariants — its fingerprints? We'll find exactly two kinds: numbers that summarize the whole motion (determinant, trace) and directions that pass through it unturned (eigenvectors, each with its own scale factor \(\lambda\)).

The route: ① the determinant — a volume dial with an alarm bell at zero; ② how to actually compute it (minors, cofactors, and the lazy-row trick); ③ the trace, an absurdly easy number with a hidden double life; ④ eigenvectors — hunting the directions a matrix cannot bend, first by dragging, then by algebra (the characteristic equation); ⑤ the Spectral Theorem — for symmetric matrices the fingerprints organize into a perfect perpendicular grain, \(A=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\); ⑥ two encores that ML uses daily: complex eigenvalues (rotations) and the Cholesky square root of a covariance matrix.

The determinant — a volume dial with an alarm bell

Before hunting special directions, we need one number for what a matrix does to space as a whole. Take the unit square, feed its corners through \(A\), and watch it land as a parallelogram — remember from Unit 1 that the columns of \(A\) are exactly where the basis arrows \(\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2\) go. The determinant is the signed area of that parallelogram:

\[\det\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}=ad-bc\]

Compute one to see how cheap it is. The widget's opening matrix \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&1.5\end{bmatrix}\) has \(\det A=(2)(1.5)-(1)(1)=2\): this machine doubles every area it touches.

Three readings of that one number:

Magnitude\(|\det A|\) is the scaling factor for area (volume in 3D). \(\det=2\) doubles every area; \(\det=\tfrac12\) halves it.
SignNegative means the motion flips space over, like a mirror — orientation reverses. Area is still scaled by \(|\det A|\).
ZeroThe alarm bell. The parallelogram has collapsed to a line: the columns are dependent, space is squashed flat, and no inverse can rebuild it. \(\det A=0\;\Leftrightarrow\;A\) singular \(\Leftrightarrow\;\operatorname{rank}A<n\).

So what Unit 1's "machine that squashes" now has a one-number detector — and in Section 6 that detector is exactly what will find eigenvalues.

In plain words

Think of rolling out pizza dough. Press it so it covers twice the area — that "×2" is the determinant. Flatten it paper-thin and its area hits zero, and no amount of un-pressing recovers the original ball: that irreversible flattening is \(\det A=0\). The sign just remembers whether you flipped the dough over.

Computing it — minors, cofactors, and the lazy row

Geometry says what the determinant means; the cofactor expansion is how we compute it. The idea is recursive: an \(n\times n\) determinant is a weighted sum of \((n-1)\times(n-1)\) determinants. Delete row \(i\) and column \(j\) and the small determinant that remains is the minor \(M_{ij}\); dress it with a checkerboard sign and it becomes the cofactor \(C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}\). Then, expanding along any row \(i\) — or any column \(j\) — gives the same number:

\[\det A=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\,C_{ij}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ij}\,C_{ij}\]

That "any row, any column, same answer" is not obvious — you can test it below on the lecture's \(3\times3\) example. And it hands you a strategy for free: expand along the line with the most zeros, because every zero entry deletes an entire cofactor's worth of work.

Prove it · why a triangle's determinant is its diagonal product — derived

Claim. If \(U\) is upper triangular (all zeros below the diagonal), then \(\det U=u_{11}u_{22}\cdots u_{nn}\).

1
Expand along the first column. Below \(u_{11}\) that column is all zeros, so only one term survives: \[\det U=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i1}C_{i1}=u_{11}C_{11}+0+\cdots+0=u_{11}\,(-1)^{1+1}M_{11}=u_{11}M_{11}\] Every other term has a factor \(u_{i1}=0\) (\(i\ge2\)). The sign of \(C_{11}\) is \((-1)^{2}=+1\).
2
Look at what \(M_{11}\) is: delete row 1 and column 1 of \(U\). What remains is an \((n-1)\times(n-1)\) matrix that is still upper triangular, with diagonal \(u_{22},\dots,u_{nn}\). Deleting the first row and column keeps every remaining entry in its relative position — zeros below the diagonal stay below the diagonal.
3
Apply step 1 to that smaller triangle, then again, and again: \[\det U=u_{11}\,M_{11}=u_{11}\,u_{22}\,M'_{11}=\cdots=u_{11}u_{22}\cdots u_{n-1,n-1}\det\begin{bmatrix}u_{nn}\end{bmatrix}\] Each round peels one diagonal entry off the front and leaves a triangle one size smaller.
4
The \(1\times1\) determinant is the entry itself, \(\det[u_{nn}]=u_{nn}\). Hence \(\det U=u_{11}u_{22}\cdots u_{nn}\). Done. ∎ For a lower triangle run the same argument along the first row (or use \(\det L=\det L^{\top}\)). Diagonal matrices are both, so their determinant is the diagonal product too.
Why does adding a row to another leave det unchanged? (the two-line induction)

Expand the altered determinant: each term splits into the original \(\det A\) plus \(c\) times a determinant whose rows \(i\) and \(j\) are identical. Swap those equal rows: the determinant must flip sign, yet it is unchanged — so it equals its own negative, which forces it to be \(0\). The extra piece vanishes, and \(\det\) survives elimination untouched. The same equal-rows trick powers half the determinant identities you'll ever meet.

The bridge theorem: full rank ⟺ det ≠ 0 (this is where eigenvalues will come from)

Eliminate \(A\) down to \(U\). Full rank means every column has a pivot, so every diagonal entry \(U_{ii}\) is non-zero, so \(\det A=\pm\prod U_{ii}\ne0\). Conversely, \(\det A\ne0\) forces every \(U_{ii}\ne0\): all pivots present, columns independent, only \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) solves \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\). Hold this thought: in Section 6 an eigenvalue will be born at exactly the values \(\lambda\) where the shifted matrix \(A-\lambda I\) loses full rank — and \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\) is the tripwire that detects it.

Useful properties, one line each

\(\det(AB)=\det A\cdot\det B\) (scaling factors of chained machines multiply) · \(\det A^{\top}=\det A\) (transposing preserves volume) · \(\det A^{-1}=1/\det A\) (the undo machine un-scales) · \(A\) triangular \(\Rightarrow\det A=\prod_i a_{ii}\) (the shortcut elimination cashes in).

The trace — a pulse reading

The determinant's understated sibling costs almost nothing: the trace is the sum of the diagonal,

\[\operatorname{tr}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}\]

and it behaves beautifully: \(\operatorname{tr}(A+B)=\operatorname{tr}A+\operatorname{tr}B\), \(\operatorname{tr}(\alpha A)=\alpha\operatorname{tr}A\), \(\operatorname{tr}(I_n)=n\), and — the workhorse — the cyclic property \(\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)\), which holds even when \(AB\ne BA\), even when the two products have different sizes. Countless ML derivations (gradients of \(\operatorname{tr}(X^{\top}AX)\), for one) lean on that single identity.

Why does a number this cheap deserve a section? Because of a fact we'll prove in Section 6: the trace is the sum of the eigenvalues, and the determinant is their product. Two numbers you can read straight off the matrix summarize the spectrum you haven't computed yet.

Prove it · why tr(AB) = tr(BA) — derived

Claim. For \(A\) of size \(m\times n\) and \(B\) of size \(n\times m\) (so both products exist), \(\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)\) — even though \(AB\) is \(m\times m\) and \(BA\) is \(n\times n\).

1
Write one diagonal entry of \(AB\) with the row-times-column rule: \[(AB)_{ii}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{ik}\,b_{ki}\] Row \(i\) of \(A\) dotted with column \(i\) of \(B\). The index \(k\) runs over the inner dimension \(n\).
2
Add the diagonal entries to get the trace — a double sum: \[\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{ik}\,b_{ki}\] Just the definition of trace applied to step 1. Every term is one product \(a_{ik}b_{ki}\).
3
Do the same for \(BA\), whose diagonal entries are \((BA)_{kk}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{ki}a_{ik}\): \[\operatorname{tr}(BA)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{ki}\,a_{ik}\] Row \(k\) of \(B\) dotted with column \(k\) of \(A\); now the inner index is \(i\), running to \(m\).
4
Compare: both are the sum of the same \(mn\) numbers \(a_{ik}b_{ki}\), listed in a different order. Finite sums don't care about order, so \(\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)\). Done. ∎ \(b_{ki}a_{ik}=a_{ik}b_{ki}\) because scalars commute; swapping the two \(\sum\) signs is just re-grouping the same list of products. Nothing about the sizes \(m,n\) ever mattered.

Eigenvectors — the directions a matrix cannot turn

Now the star of the unit. A matrix moves every vector — but a few special vectors it can only stretch or flip along their own line, never knock off it. Those unbendable directions are the eigenvectors, and each one carries a personal stretch factor, its eigenvalue:

\[A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}\qquad(\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0})\]

Positive \(\lambda\) stretches (\(\lambda>1\)) or squeezes (\(0<\lambda<1\)) along the direction; negative \(\lambda\) flips it end-over-end and then scales. The zero vector is banned from the club — \(A\mathbf{0}=\lambda\mathbf{0}\) holds for every \(\lambda\), so it points at nothing. But if \(\mathbf{x}\) qualifies, so does every non-zero multiple \(c\mathbf{x}\): eigenvectors are really eigen-directions, whole lines the matrix owns.

Three pictures to keep

A spinning globe: every city moves, but the axis through the poles stays put — an eigenvector with \(\lambda=1\). Stretching dough: pull it one way, it thins the other — the pull direction has \(\lambda>1\), the thinning direction \(0<\lambda<1\). A sheared deck of cards: push the top sideways and every card slides horizontally — horizontal vectors keep their direction (\(\lambda=1\)) while every other vector tilts. The "grain" of a transformation, made visible.

You can verify a claimed eigenvector with one multiplication. The lecture's example: for \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&6\\5&2\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{u}=\begin{bmatrix}6\\-5\end{bmatrix}\),

\[A\mathbf{u}=\begin{bmatrix}1(6)+6(-5)\\5(6)+2(-5)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-24\\20\end{bmatrix}=-4\begin{bmatrix}6\\-5\end{bmatrix}=-4\,\mathbf{u}\]

— so \(\mathbf{u}\) is an eigenvector with \(\lambda=-4\): \(A\) flips it and stretches it by 4, but the line through \(\mathbf{u}\) is untouched. Try \(\mathbf{v}=(3,-2)\) instead: \(A\mathbf{v}=(-9,11)\), which is on nobody's line through \(\mathbf{v}\) — not an eigenvector. Hunting eigenvalues — the characteristic equation

In plain words

An eigenvalue is like the one note that makes a wine glass ring. Sweep a dial through every pitch: mostly nothing — then at one special value it resonates. Our dial is \(\lambda\), and "resonance" means the shifted matrix \(A-\lambda I\) suddenly collapses space. Section 3 built the exact tripwire for that collapse: the determinant hitting zero.

The algebra takes three lines. Rearrange the eigen-equation:

\[A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}\;\Longrightarrow\;(A-\lambda I)\,\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0},\qquad \mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}\]

A non-zero vector mapped to zero means \(A-\lambda I\) is singular — full rank lost — and by the bridge theorem that happens precisely when

\[\boxed{\;\det(A-\lambda I)=0\;}\]

This is the characteristic equation. Its left side is a degree-\(n\) polynomial in \(\lambda\) — the characteristic polynomial \(p_A(\lambda)\) — and its roots are the complete list of eigenvalues. For each root, the eigenvectors are the non-zero solutions of \((A-\lambda I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\): Unit 1's elimination, pointed at the nullspace of the collapsed matrix. That nullspace is the eigenspace \(E_\lambda\), and the set of all eigenvalues is the matrix's spectrum.

For \(2\times2\) matrices the polynomial wears its meaning openly:

\[p_A(\lambda)=\lambda^2-\operatorname{tr}(A)\,\lambda+\det(A)\]

— trace and determinant are literally its coefficients. (In general \(p_A(\lambda)=(-1)^n\lambda^n+(-1)^{n-1}\operatorname{tr}(A)\lambda^{n-1}+\cdots+\det A\): the top two coefficients can only come from the diagonal product \(\prod(a_{ii}-\lambda)\), and setting \(\lambda=0\) gives the constant term \(p_A(0)=\det A\).)

Prove it · why the 2×2 characteristic polynomial is λ² − tr(A)λ + det A — derived

Claim. For \(A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\), \(\ \det(A-\lambda I)=\lambda^2-(a+d)\lambda+(ad-bc)\).

1
Subtract \(\lambda\) down the diagonal and apply the \(2\times2\) formula \(ad-bc\): \[\det\begin{bmatrix}a-\lambda&b\\c&d-\lambda\end{bmatrix}=(a-\lambda)(d-\lambda)-bc\] The off-diagonal entries \(b,c\) are untouched by the shift — only the diagonal moves.
2
Multiply out the product: \[(a-\lambda)(d-\lambda)=ad-a\lambda-d\lambda+\lambda^2=\lambda^2-(a+d)\lambda+ad\] Four terms from FOIL; the two \(\lambda\) terms collect into \(-(a+d)\lambda\).
3
Put the \(-bc\) back and name the coefficients: \(\lambda^2-(a+d)\lambda+(ad-bc)=\lambda^2-\operatorname{tr}(A)\,\lambda+\det A\). Done. ∎ \(a+d\) is the trace by definition and \(ad-bc\) is the determinant by definition. Check on the lecture's matrix: \(\lambda^2-3\lambda-28\).
Prove it · why the eigenvalues sum to the trace and multiply to the determinant — derived

Claim. If \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\) are the roots of \(p_A\), then \(\lambda_1+\lambda_2=\operatorname{tr}A\) and \(\lambda_1\lambda_2=\det A\).

1
A degree-2 polynomial with roots \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\) and leading coefficient 1 factors as \[p_A(\lambda)=(\lambda-\lambda_1)(\lambda-\lambda_2)\] Plugging in \(\lambda=\lambda_1\) or \(\lambda_2\) gives 0, and the \(\lambda^2\) coefficient is 1 — matching the box above. (If the roots are complex, this still holds over \(\mathbb{C}\).)
2
Expand the factored form: \[(\lambda-\lambda_1)(\lambda-\lambda_2)=\lambda^2-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\lambda+\lambda_1\lambda_2\] Same FOIL as before, with the roots in place of \(a,d\).
3
Two ways of writing the same polynomial must have the same coefficients. Compare with \(\lambda^2-\operatorname{tr}(A)\lambda+\det A\): \(\lambda_1+\lambda_2=\operatorname{tr}A\) and \(\lambda_1\lambda_2=\det A\). Done. ∎ For \(n\times n\): \(p_A(\lambda)=\prod_i(\lambda_i-\lambda)\); the \(\lambda^{n-1}\) coefficient of the product is \((-1)^{n-1}\sum_i\lambda_i\), which must equal \((-1)^{n-1}\operatorname{tr}A\); and setting \(\lambda=0\) gives \(\prod_i\lambda_i=p_A(0)=\det A\). Same idea, more terms.

The recipe card — every eigen-computation, ever

① Write \(A-\lambda I\) (subtract \(\lambda\) down the diagonal, touch nothing else). ② Set \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\) and solve — the roots are all the eigenvalues. ③ For each root \(\lambda_i\), run Unit 1's elimination on \((A-\lambda_i I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) and describe the nullspace — that's the eigenspace \(E_{\lambda_i}\). ④ Free audit: the roots must sum to \(\operatorname{tr}A\) and multiply to \(\det A\). Four steps, no exceptions — exam questions, PCA libraries, and Google's PageRank all walk this exact staircase.

Shortcut worth stealing

Triangular matrices hand you their eigenvalues for free. If \(A\) is triangular, so is \(A-\lambda I\), and a triangle's determinant is its diagonal product: \(p_A(\lambda)=(a_{11}-\lambda)(a_{22}-\lambda)\cdots(a_{nn}-\lambda)\). The eigenvalues are the diagonal entries, no computation at all. Two more freebies in the same spirit: \(A\) and \(A^{\top}\) share a spectrum (because \(\det M=\det M^{\top}\) makes their characteristic polynomials identical), and the eigenvalues of \(I_n\) are all 1 with eigenspace the whole of \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

The Spectral Theorem — symmetric matrices come apart cleanly

Why you should care

Nearly every matrix machine learning builds from data is symmetric: covariance matrices, Gram matrices \(A^{\top}A\), kernel matrices, graph Laplacians. A theorem about symmetric matrices is not a special case — it's a theorem about the matrices you'll actually meet.

Think of wood grain. A plank splits cleanly along its grain and resists across it: a hidden set of perpendicular directions built into the material. The Spectral Theorem says every symmetric matrix (\(A=A^{\top}\)) has exactly that structure:

Spectral theorem (real symmetric A)

① every eigenvalue is real; ② eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal; ③ there are enough of them to form an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) — so \(A\) is always diagonalisable, and by an orthonormal matrix at that:

\(A=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\),   \(Q\) = eigenvectors as columns (\(Q^{-1}=Q^{\top}\), Unit 3's honest motions),   \(\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\).

Read \(A=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\) right-to-left and a "complicated" matrix becomes three innocent moves: turn into the eigenbasis (\(Q^{\top}\) — a rotation or a reflection, lengths untouched), stretch along the now-straight axes (\(\Lambda\) — just numbers on a diagonal), turn back (\(Q\)). Every symmetric matrix is one honest motion away from being diagonal.

The worked example, start to finish: \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\) has \(p_A(\lambda)=(2-\lambda)^2-1=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-3)\), so \(\lambda_1=3,\lambda_2=1\). Eigenvectors \((1,1)\) and \((-1,1)\) — orthogonal, as promised — normalise to

\[\mathbf{q}_1=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf{q}_2=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad Q=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix},\quad \Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\]

and multiplying back: \(Q\Lambda Q^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\) ✓. Watch the three moves happen to a vector you control:

Prove it · why a symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues — derived

Claim. If \(A\) is real and \(A^{\top}=A\), every eigenvalue of \(A\) is a real number.

1
Allow \(\lambda\) and \(\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}\) to be complex for now, with \(A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}\). Multiply on the left by the conjugate-transpose \(\mathbf{x}^{H}=\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\): \[\mathbf{x}^{H}A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\,\mathbf{x}^{H}\mathbf{x}\] Section 9 introduces \(\mathbf{x}^{H}\) properly; all we use is that it's a row vector, so both sides are single numbers.
2
The right-hand factor \(\mathbf{x}^{H}\mathbf{x}\) is a positive real number: \[\mathbf{x}^{H}\mathbf{x}=\sum_i\overline{x_i}\,x_i=\sum_i|x_i|^2>0\] Each \(\overline{x_i}x_i=|x_i|^2\ge0\), and at least one \(x_i\ne0\) because \(\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}\).
3
The left-hand side \(s=\mathbf{x}^{H}A\mathbf{x}\) equals its own conjugate. Take the conjugate-transpose of the \(1\times1\) "matrix" \(s\): \[\overline{s}=s^{H}=(\mathbf{x}^{H}A\mathbf{x})^{H}=\mathbf{x}^{H}A^{H}(\mathbf{x}^{H})^{H}=\mathbf{x}^{H}A\mathbf{x}=s\] Reverse the order and conjugate-transpose each factor (the rule \((XYZ)^{H}=Z^{H}Y^{H}X^{H}\)). Then \(A^{H}=\overline{A}^{\top}=A^{\top}=A\) because \(A\) is real and symmetric. A number equal to its own conjugate is real.
4
Divide step 1 by the positive real number from step 2: \(\lambda=\dfrac{\mathbf{x}^{H}A\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^{H}\mathbf{x}}=\dfrac{\text{real}}{\text{positive real}}\), so \(\lambda\) is real. ∎ The same argument with \(A^{H}=A\) (Hermitian) instead of \(A^{\top}=A\) is what Section 9 uses.
Prove it · why eigenvectors of different eigenvalues are perpendicular — derived

Claim. If \(A^{\top}=A\), \(A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}\), \(A\mathbf{y}=\mu\mathbf{y}\) and \(\lambda\ne\mu\), then \(\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=0\).

1
Start from the number \(\mathbf{y}^{\top}A\mathbf{x}\) and evaluate it using \(\mathbf{x}\)'s eigen-equation: \[\mathbf{y}^{\top}A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}^{\top}(\lambda\mathbf{x})=\lambda\,\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\] Replace \(A\mathbf{x}\) by \(\lambda\mathbf{x}\), then pull the scalar out front.
2
Evaluate the same number using \(\mathbf{y}\)'s eigen-equation instead. First move \(A\) onto \(\mathbf{y}\): \[\mathbf{y}^{\top}A\mathbf{x}=(A^{\top}\mathbf{y})^{\top}\mathbf{x}=(A\mathbf{y})^{\top}\mathbf{x}=(\mu\mathbf{y})^{\top}\mathbf{x}=\mu\,\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\] \(\mathbf{y}^{\top}A=(A^{\top}\mathbf{y})^{\top}\) is the transpose rule; \(A^{\top}=A\) is the only place symmetry is used.
3
Subtract the two expressions for the same number: \[\lambda\,\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-\mu\,\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=(\lambda-\mu)\,\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=0\] Steps 1 and 2 both equal \(\mathbf{y}^{\top}A\mathbf{x}\), so their difference is zero.
4
Since \(\lambda-\mu\ne0\), divide by it: \(\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=0\) — perpendicular. ∎ A product of two numbers is zero only if one of them is; the first factor isn't. With a repeated eigenvalue the eigenspace has extra dimensions, and Gram–Schmidt (Unit 3) builds an orthonormal basis inside it. The one fact we take on faith is that for symmetric matrices those dimensions always add up to \(n\) — no direction goes missing.
Where it powers ML

PCA: the principal components are the eigenvectors of the (symmetric) covariance matrix; each eigenvalue is the variance captured along its component. Keep the top few and you've compressed the data with least loss — Units 12–13 build this in full. SVD: apply the spectral theorem to \(A^{\top}A\) and \(AA^{\top}\) and you get the singular value decomposition of any matrix — Unit 5's headline. Quadratic forms: \(\mathbf{x}^{\top}A\mathbf{x}=c\) draws an ellipsoid whose axes are the eigenvectors with semi-axes \(\propto1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}\) — the geometry behind confidence regions and Unit 3's unit balls. Dynamics: solutions of \(\dot{\mathbf y}=A\mathbf{y}\) are mixtures of \(e^{\lambda t}\mathbf{v}\) — the signs of eigenvalues decide decay, oscillation, or blow-up.

When nothing aligns — rotations and complex eigenvalues

Section 5 left a mystery: the rotation matrix resonated nowhere. As a merry-go-round spins, no horse keeps pointing the same way — no real direction survives, so there is no real eigenvector to find. Does the theory just fail?

It doesn't — it expands. Run the machinery honestly for \(R_\theta=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}\):

\[\begin{aligned}p(\lambda)&=\lambda^{2}-2\cos\theta\,\lambda+1\\ \lambda&=\cos\theta\pm i\sin\theta=e^{\pm i\theta}\end{aligned}\]

The roots exist — they've just stepped off the real line into the complex plane, landing on the unit circle as a conjugate pair. The eigenvalues didn't fail to describe the motion — they described it perfectly, in a language built for turning.

Cholesky — the square root of a matrix

One more factorisation, for the friendliest matrices of all: symmetric and positive-definite (all eigenvalues \(>0\) — Unit 3's engine-room matrices, wearing this unit's vocabulary). Just as \(9=3\times3\) gives the number 9 a square root, an SPD matrix has a tidy "square root" of its own:

\[A=LL^{\top}\]

with \(L\) lower-triangular and positive on its diagonal. And like ordinary square roots, it only works for "positive" inputs. The entries of \(L\) fall out one at a time, top-left to bottom-right — for \(2\times2\):

\[\ell_{11}=\sqrt{a_{11}},\qquad \ell_{21}=\frac{a_{21}}{\ell_{11}},\qquad \ell_{22}=\sqrt{a_{22}-\ell_{21}^{2}}\]

The lecture's example: \(A=\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&3\end{bmatrix}\) gives \(\ell_{11}=2\), \(\ell_{21}=1\), \(\ell_{22}=\sqrt{3-1}=\sqrt2\), and indeed \(\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\1&\sqrt2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\0&\sqrt2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&3\end{bmatrix}\) ✓. Notice every diagonal step takes a real square root — if the matrix isn't positive-definite, some radicand goes negative and the algorithm halts. Cholesky doubles as the cheapest positive-definiteness test in practice: don't check eigenvalues, just try to factor.

Prove it · where the 2×2 Cholesky recipe comes from — derived

Claim. If \(A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&a_{21}\\a_{21}&a_{22}\end{bmatrix}\) is SPD, then \(A=LL^{\top}\) with \(L=\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{11}&0\\\ell_{21}&\ell_{22}\end{bmatrix}\), \(\ \ell_{11}=\sqrt{a_{11}},\ \ell_{21}=a_{21}/\ell_{11},\ \ell_{22}=\sqrt{a_{22}-\ell_{21}^2}\).

1
Multiply out \(LL^{\top}\) with unknown entries: \[\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{11}&0\\\ell_{21}&\ell_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{11}&\ell_{21}\\0&\ell_{22}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{11}^2&\ell_{11}\ell_{21}\\\ell_{11}\ell_{21}&\ell_{21}^2+\ell_{22}^2\end{bmatrix}\] Row-times-column, four times. The result is automatically symmetric — which is why this shape can only ever match a symmetric \(A\).
2
Match the top-left entries: \(\ell_{11}^2=a_{11}\), so \(\ell_{11}=\sqrt{a_{11}}\). We take the positive root by convention. It exists because \(a_{11}=\mathbf{e}_1^{\top}A\mathbf{e}_1>0\) for a positive-definite \(A\).
3
Match the off-diagonal entries: \(\ell_{11}\ell_{21}=a_{21}\), so \(\ell_{21}=a_{21}/\ell_{11}\). Division is legal because \(\ell_{11}>0\) from step 2. This is the only equation that involves \(\ell_{21}\), so it pins it down.
4
Match the bottom-right entries: \(\ell_{21}^2+\ell_{22}^2=a_{22}\), so \(\ell_{22}=\sqrt{a_{22}-\ell_{21}^2}\). Done. ∎ The radicand is \(a_{22}-a_{21}^2/a_{11}=\det A/a_{11}\), which is positive exactly when \(\det A>0\) — i.e. when both eigenvalues are positive. A failed square root here is the algorithm detecting "not positive-definite". The \(n\times n\) recipe repeats this entry by entry, top-left to bottom-right.
The killer application

A multivariate Gaussian is governed by a covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) — symmetric positive-definite, as data-built matrices are. To sample from that distribution: factor \(\Sigma=LL^{\top}\), draw an easy uncorrelated standard-normal vector \(\mathbf{z}\) (each coordinate independently), and output \(\mathbf{x}=L\mathbf{z}\). The covariance of \(L\mathbf{z}\) is \(L\,I\,L^{\top}=\Sigma\) — exactly right. \(L\) is the lens that bends a featureless round cloud into the tilted ellipse your data actually lives on. Every simulation library you'll ever call does precisely this.

Prove it · why Lz has covariance Σ — derived

Claim. If \(\mathbf{z}\) has mean \(\mathbf{0}\) and covariance \(I\) (independent standard normals), and \(\Sigma=LL^{\top}\), then \(\mathbf{x}=L\mathbf{z}\) has covariance \(\Sigma\).

1
Covariance is the expected outer product of the centred vector. First, \(\mathbf{x}\) is centred: \[\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]=\mathbb{E}[L\mathbf{z}]=L\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}]=L\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}\] Expectation is linear, and \(L\) is a constant matrix, so it slides outside.
2
Write the covariance of \(\mathbf{x}\) and substitute \(\mathbf{x}=L\mathbf{z}\): \[\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}]=\mathbb{E}[(L\mathbf{z})(L\mathbf{z})^{\top}]=\mathbb{E}[L\,\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\top}L^{\top}]\] \((L\mathbf{z})^{\top}=\mathbf{z}^{\top}L^{\top}\) — the transpose reverses the order.
3
Pull the constant matrices outside the expectation: \[\mathbb{E}[L\,\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\top}L^{\top}]=L\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\top}]\,L^{\top}=L\,I\,L^{\top}\] Only \(\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\top}\) is random. \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\top}]=\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{z})=I\): each coordinate has variance 1, and different coordinates are uncorrelated.
4
\(LIL^{\top}=LL^{\top}=\Sigma\). ∎ Nothing used the triangular shape — any \(M\) with \(MM^{\top}=\Sigma\) works (e.g. \(Q\Lambda^{1/2}\) from Section 7). Cholesky is simply the cheapest such \(M\) to compute.

The fine print — defective matrices and the ML bridge

Does every \(n\times n\) matrix have \(n\) eigenvectors? No — and the failure is worth seeing once. The general recipe \(A=PDP^{-1}\) needs \(n\) independent eigenvectors to fill \(P\)'s columns. You are guaranteed them in two cases:

  • All \(n\) eigenvalues distinct. Eigenvectors of different eigenvalues are automatically independent — proved below.
  • \(A\) symmetric. That's the spectral theorem, with the bonus \(P^{-1}=Q^{\top}\), even when eigenvalues repeat.

With repeated eigenvalues and no symmetry, it can fail. The lecture's smallest example is \(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\): \(p(\lambda)=\lambda^2\), so \(\lambda=0\) twice — but \((A-0I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) forces \(x_2=0\), leaving only the single line \(\operatorname{span}\{(1,0)\}\). The shear \(\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) is the same story shifted by \(I\): \(\lambda=1\) counted twice by the polynomial, one eigen-line. Two counts, two names:

algebraic multiplicityhow many times \(\lambda\) appears as a root of \(p_A\) — for the shear, \(\operatorname{am}(1)=2\)
geometric multiplicitythe dimension of the eigenspace \(E_\lambda\) — for the shear, \(\operatorname{gm}(1)=1\)

Always \(1\le\operatorname{gm}\le\operatorname{am}\). When \(\operatorname{gm}<\operatorname{am}\) for some \(\lambda\), the matrix is one direction short of a basis: it is defective, and no change of basis will ever make it diagonal. You met this in Section 5's widget — the missing second resonance was this fact, felt through your fingertips.

Prove it · why distinct eigenvalues give independent eigenvectors — derived

Claim. If \(A\mathbf{v}_1=\lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1\), \(A\mathbf{v}_2=\lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2\), both non-zero, and \(\lambda_1\ne\lambda_2\), then \(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2\) are linearly independent. (The same trick, repeated, handles any number of distinct eigenvalues.)

1
Suppose some combination vanishes: \(c_1\mathbf{v}_1+c_2\mathbf{v}_2=\mathbf{0}\). We must show \(c_1=c_2=0\). That is the definition of independence: only the trivial combination gives zero.
2
Apply the matrix \(A-\lambda_2I\) to both sides: \[c_1(A-\lambda_2I)\mathbf{v}_1+c_2(A-\lambda_2I)\mathbf{v}_2=\mathbf{0}\] Multiplying \(\mathbf{0}\) by any matrix gives \(\mathbf{0}\); on the left we distribute.
3
Evaluate each piece with the eigen-equations: \[(A-\lambda_2I)\mathbf{v}_1=\lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1-\lambda_2\mathbf{v}_1=(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\mathbf{v}_1,\qquad (A-\lambda_2I)\mathbf{v}_2=\lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2-\lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2=\mathbf{0}\] The shift by \(\lambda_2\) is designed to annihilate \(\mathbf{v}_2\) and merely rescale \(\mathbf{v}_1\).
4
So step 2 collapses to \(c_1(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\mathbf{v}_1=\mathbf{0}\). Since \(\lambda_1-\lambda_2\ne0\) and \(\mathbf{v}_1\ne\mathbf{0}\), we get \(c_1=0\). A non-zero scalar times a non-zero vector is non-zero, so the only way out is \(c_1=0\).
5
Back in step 1, \(c_2\mathbf{v}_2=\mathbf{0}\) with \(\mathbf{v}_2\ne\mathbf{0}\) forces \(c_2=0\). Independent. ∎ For \(k\) distinct eigenvalues, apply \((A-\lambda_2I)(A-\lambda_3I)\cdots(A-\lambda_kI)\) to kill every vector but \(\mathbf{v}_1\), conclude \(c_1=0\), and repeat. Distinct eigenvalues ⇒ a full eigenbasis ⇒ diagonalisable.

The bridge into the next units. Take any data matrix \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) with independent columns (rank \(n\)) and form the Gram matrix \(A^{\top}A\). Two facts about it:

  • Symmetric: \((A^{\top}A)^{\top}=A^{\top}(A^{\top})^{\top}=A^{\top}A\).
  • Positive-definite: \(\mathbf{x}^{\top}A^{\top}A\mathbf{x}=(A\mathbf{x})^{\top}(A\mathbf{x})=\|A\mathbf{x}\|^{2}>0\) for \(\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}\), because full column rank means \(A\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf{0}\).

So everything in this unit applies to it: real positive eigenvalues, an orthonormal eigenbasis, a Cholesky factor.

So what that one observation is the engine of least squares (Unit 9's normal equations solve \(A^{\top}A\hat{\mathbf x}=A^{\top}\mathbf{b}\)), of SVD (Unit 5 strings together the eigen-structure of \(A^{\top}A\) and \(AA^{\top}\)), and of PCA (Units 12–13 eigendecompose the covariance matrix, which is a scaled Gram matrix of centred data). This unit is the load-bearing wall.

The whole story, in five lines

① The determinant is the volume dial — \(|\det|\) scales area/volume, the sign records a mirror flip, and \(\det=0\) is the alarm bell of collapse: singular, rank-deficient, irreversible. Compute it by cofactors along the laziest line, or by elimination to a triangle. ② The trace adds the diagonal in a heartbeat and is also the sum of the eigenvalues, while the determinant is their product — the free pair of sanity checks. ③ Eigenvectors are the directions a matrix cannot turn, \(A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}\); the characteristic equation \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\) hunts every \(\lambda\), and elimination on the collapsed matrix recovers each eigenspace. ④ For symmetric matrices the fingerprints organise perfectly: real eigenvalues on perpendicular directions, \(A=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\) — every symmetric matrix is just turn, stretch, turn back. Rotations opt out into the complex plane (\(e^{\pm i\theta}\)), where Hermitian matrices carry the theorem onward. ⑤ Cholesky \(A=LL^{\top}\) is the SPD square root — the working end of covariance matrices, Gaussian sampling, and the fastest positive-definiteness test there is.

The one thing to remember

A matrix is its spectrum, seen through a coordinate system. Find the directions it cannot turn and the numbers it stretches them by, and you have seen through the array of entries to the skeleton underneath — which is exactly what PCA, SVD, spectral clustering, and stability analysis do for a living.

Problem 1Medium

Consider

\[ A=\begin{bmatrix}4&1&1\\1&4&1\\1&1&4\end{bmatrix}. \]

(a) Find the characteristic polynomial and all eigenvalues with their algebraic multiplicities. (b) For every eigenvalue find a basis of its eigenspace and state the geometric multiplicity. (c) Hence decide, with reason, whether \(A\) has a full basis of eigenvectors.

What this tests. Computing a \(3\times 3\) characteristic polynomial cleanly, then comparing algebraic and geometric multiplicity eigenvalue by eigenvalue.

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 1 · Spot the structure. \(A=3I+J\), where \(J\) is the all-ones matrix — every row sums to 6, a strong hint that \((1,1,1)^{\top}\) will be an eigenvector with eigenvalue 6.
Step 2 · Characteristic polynomial by row reduction. Start from \[ \det(A-\lambda I)=\det\begin{bmatrix}4-\lambda&1&1\\1&4-\lambda&1\\1&1&4-\lambda\end{bmatrix}. \] Subtract row 1 from rows 2 and 3 (determinant unchanged). Entry by entry, the new row 2 is \(\big(1-(4-\lambda),\,(4-\lambda)-1,\,1-1\big)=(\lambda-3,\;3-\lambda,\;0)\) and the new row 3 is \((\lambda-3,\;0,\;3-\lambda)\). Factor \((\lambda-3)\) out of each of those rows: \[ \det(A-\lambda I)=(\lambda-3)^2\det\begin{bmatrix}4-\lambda&1&1\\1&-1&0\\1&0&-1\end{bmatrix}. \] Expand the remaining determinant along row 3: \(1\cdot\det\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\-1&0\end{bmatrix}+(-1)\cdot\det\begin{bmatrix}4-\lambda&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix} = 1\cdot(0+1)+(-1)\big(-(4-\lambda)-1\big)=1+(5-\lambda)=6-\lambda.\) Hence \[ \det(A-\lambda I)=(\lambda-3)^2(6-\lambda)=-(\lambda-6)(\lambda-3)^2. \] So \(\lambda=6\) with algebraic multiplicity \(\operatorname{am}(6)=1\), and \(\lambda=3\) with \(\operatorname{am}(3)=2\).
Step 3 · Eigenspace of \(\lambda=6\). \((A-6I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\) reads \(-2x+y+z=0,\; x-2y+z=0,\; x+y-2z=0\). Subtracting the second equation from the first gives \(-3x+3y=0\Rightarrow x=y\); subtracting the third from the second gives \(-3y+3z=0\Rightarrow y=z\). So \(x=y=z\): basis \(\{(1,1,1)^{\top}\}\), geometric multiplicity \(\operatorname{gm}(6)=1\).
Step 4 · Eigenspace of \(\lambda=3\). \(A-3I=J\) (all ones), so all three equations collapse to the single condition \(x+y+z=0\). Two free variables give \[ \mathbf{v}=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad\begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{gm}(3)=2. \]
Step 5 · Compare multiplicities. \(\operatorname{gm}(6)=1=\operatorname{am}(6)\) and \(\operatorname{gm}(3)=2=\operatorname{am}(3)\): the three eigenvectors together form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). (In fact \(A\) is symmetric, so the spectral theorem already guaranteed a full — even orthogonal — eigenbasis.)
Answer. \(\det(A-\lambda I)=-(\lambda-6)(\lambda-3)^2\). \(\lambda=6\): multiplicity 1, eigenvector \((1,1,1)^{\top}\); \(\lambda=3\): multiplicity 2, eigenspace \(\{x+y+z=0\}\) with basis \((-1,1,0)^{\top},(-1,0,1)^{\top}\). \(A\) has a full eigenbasis.
Watch out

A repeated eigenvalue is a question, not a verdict: you must actually solve \((A-3I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\) and count independent solutions. And keep the leading minus sign — for a \(3\times 3\), \(\det(A-\lambda I)\) starts with \(-\lambda^3\), which is why the factorization reads \(-(\lambda-6)(\lambda-3)^2\).

Key takeaway

Sanity-check eigenvalues instantly: \(\operatorname{tr}(A)=4+4+4=12=6+3+3\) and the row-sum trick hands you \((1,1,1)^{\top}\!\mapsto 6\) for free. For symmetric matrices, \(\operatorname{gm}=\operatorname{am}\) always holds — the spectral theorem forbids "missing" eigenvectors.

Wait — really?

All three rows of \(A-3I\) collapse into the single equation \(x+y+z=0\) — a whole plane of eigenvectors. And the \(\lambda=6\) eigenvector \((1,1,1)^{\top}\) is exactly the normal of that plane: orthogonality between eigenspaces showed up before anyone asked for it.

Problem 2Medium

Find all eigenvalues and corresponding eigenvectors of

\[ A=\begin{bmatrix}3&-2\\4&-1\end{bmatrix}, \]

and explain geometrically why no real eigenvector exists.

What this tests. Handling complex roots of the characteristic polynomial of a real \(2\times 2\) — and reading the geometry behind them.

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 1 · Characteristic polynomial. \[ \det(A-\lambda I)=\det\begin{bmatrix}3-\lambda&-2\\4&-1-\lambda\end{bmatrix}=(3-\lambda)(-1-\lambda)+8. \] Expand the product: \((3-\lambda)(-1-\lambda)=-3-3\lambda+\lambda+\lambda^{2}=\lambda^{2}-2\lambda-3\); adding 8 gives \[ \lambda^{2}-2\lambda+5=0. \]
Step 2 · Solve the quadratic. \[ \lambda=\frac{2\pm\sqrt{4-20}}{2}=\frac{2\pm\sqrt{-16}}{2}=1\pm 2i. \] The discriminant is negative, so the eigenvalues are a complex-conjugate pair.
Step 3 · Eigenvectors. For \(\lambda=1+2i\), the first row of \((A-\lambda I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\) gives \((3-\lambda)x-2y=0\), i.e. \((2-2i)x-2y=0\Rightarrow y=(1-i)x\). Taking \(x=1\), \[ \mathbf{v}_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1-i\end{bmatrix}. \] Its conjugate \(\mathbf{v}_2=(1,\,1+i)^{\top}\) belongs to the conjugate eigenvalue \(\lambda=1-2i\).
Step 4 · Verify the pair. Entry by entry: \(A\mathbf{v}_1=\big(3\cdot 1-2(1-i),\; 4\cdot 1-(1-i)\big)^{\top}=(1+2i,\;3+i)^{\top}\), while \(\lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1=(1+2i)\,(1,\,1-i)^{\top}=\big(1+2i,\;(1+2i)(1-i)\big)^{\top}\) and \((1+2i)(1-i)=1-i+2i-2i^{2}=3+i\). Both sides match. ✓
Step 5 · Geometric reason. A real \(2\times 2\) matrix with complex eigenvalues acts as a rotation combined with a scaling. A genuine rotation maps no nonzero real vector onto a multiple of itself — so no real eigenvector can exist.
Answer. \(\lambda=1\pm 2i\) with eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}=(1,\,1\mp i)^{\top}\). No real eigenvector exists because the eigenvalues are non-real: the map rotates the plane.
Watch out

The classic slip is expanding \((3-\lambda)(-1-\lambda)\) into \(\lambda^{2}-2\lambda-3\) and then forgetting the \(-(-2)(4)=+8\) from the off-diagonal — the constant term must be \(\det A=-3+8=5\). Also: use one row of \(A-\lambda I\) to find the eigenvector; the other row is automatically dependent, and checking that it is doubles as a free error check.

Key takeaway

For real matrices, complex eigenvalues always arrive in conjugate pairs, and so do their eigenvectors: compute one eigenvector, conjugate every entry to get the other. One computation, two eigenpairs.

Wait — really?

\(|\lambda|=\sqrt{1^{2}+2^{2}}=\sqrt{5}\) and \(\det A=5=|\lambda|^{2}\): this matrix is secretly "rotate, then stretch by \(\sqrt5\)". The determinant knew the stretch factor all along — even though no direction in the real plane is left pointing where it started.

Problem 3Easy

Consider

\[ A=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1&1\\1&0&3\\1&-1&4\end{bmatrix}, \qquad\text{whose characteristic polynomial is } (\lambda-1)(\lambda-2)(\lambda-3). \]

Without forming any matrix power explicitly, compute (a) \(\det(A^{8})\), (b) \(\operatorname{tr}(A^{6})\), and (c) \(\operatorname{tr}\!\left(A^{6}-7A^{2}\right)\).

What this tests. The scalar shortcuts \(\det(A^{k})=(\det A)^{k}\) and \(\operatorname{tr}(A^{k})=\sum_i\lambda_i^{k}\) — handling huge powers with three numbers.

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 0 · Read off the eigenvalues. The characteristic polynomial factors as \((\lambda-1)(\lambda-2)(\lambda-3)\), so the eigenvalues are \(\lambda_1=1,\ \lambda_2=2,\ \lambda_3=3\). Quick sanity check against the matrix: \(\operatorname{tr}A=2+0+4=6=1+2+3\) and \(\det A=6=1\cdot 2\cdot 3\). ✓
Part (a) · Determinant of a power. \(A^{k}\) has eigenvalues \(\lambda_i^{k}\), and the determinant is their product — which is \((\det A)^{k}\): \[ \det(A^{8})=(\det A)^{8}=6^{8}. \] Compute it in two squarings: \(6^{2}=36,\ 6^{4}=36^{2}=1296,\ 6^{8}=1296^{2}=1\,679\,616\).
Part (b) · Trace of a power. Trace is the sum of eigenvalues, taken after powering: \[ \operatorname{tr}(A^{6})=\sum_i \lambda_i^{6}=1^{6}+2^{6}+3^{6}=1+64+729=794. \]
Part (c) · Trace of a polynomial in \(A\). Trace is linear, and \(A^{6},A^{2}\) have eigenvalues \(\lambda_i^{6},\lambda_i^{2}\), so \[ \operatorname{tr}\!\left(A^{6}-7A^{2}\right)=\sum_i\big(\lambda_i^{6}-7\lambda_i^{2}\big)=794-7\,(1+4+9)=794-98=696. \] Every answer used only the three scalars \(\lambda_i\) — not a single \(3\times 3\) multiplication.
Answer. \(\det(A^{8})=6^{8}=1\,679\,616\); \(\operatorname{tr}(A^{6})=794\); \(\operatorname{tr}(A^{6}-7A^{2})=696\).
Watch out

\(\operatorname{tr}(A^{6})\neq(\operatorname{tr}A)^{6}\) — the trace of a power is the sum of powered eigenvalues, \(\sum\lambda_i^{6}\), not the power of the sum. The determinant is the multiplicative one: \(\det(A^{k})=(\det A)^{k}\) really does hold.

Key takeaway

\(\operatorname{tr}(A)=\sum_i\lambda_i\) and \(\det(A)=\prod_i\lambda_i\), and powers or polynomials of \(A\) simply apply themselves to each \(\lambda_i\). To handle a high power, find the eigenvalues first — then work entirely with scalars.

Wait — really?

\(A^{8}\) is a \(3\times 3\) matrix of six-to-seven-digit entries that we never wrote down — yet its determinant, \(1\,679\,616\), came from the single number \(\det A=6\). The eigenvalues compress everything the power will ever do to volume and trace.

Problem 4Medium

Let \(A\) be a \(3\times 3\) matrix whose eigenvalues are \(1,\,2,\,4\), and define

\[ B=A^{2}-5A+6I. \]

(a) Show that if \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) (with eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}\)), then \(\lambda^{2}-5\lambda+6\) is an eigenvalue of \(B\) (with the same eigenvector). (b) Hence find the eigenvalues of \(B\), then \(\det(B)\) and \(\operatorname{tr}(B)\), and state whether \(B\) is invertible. (c) For which eigenvalues of \(A\) would \(B\) fail to be invertible?

What this tests. The spectral mapping idea \(\lambda\mapsto p(\lambda)\): polynomials in \(A\) keep the eigenvectors and transform only the eigenvalues.

Show the worked solution — every step
Part (a) · Spectral mapping. Suppose \(A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}\). Then \(A^{2}\mathbf{v}=A(\lambda\mathbf{v})=\lambda^{2}\mathbf{v}\), and so \[ B\mathbf{v}=(A^{2}-5A+6I)\mathbf{v}=\lambda^{2}\mathbf{v}-5\lambda\mathbf{v}+6\mathbf{v}=(\lambda^{2}-5\lambda+6)\,\mathbf{v}. \] Thus \(\mathbf{v}\) is still an eigenvector — now with eigenvalue \(p(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-5\lambda+6\).
Part (b) · Apply \(p\) to each eigenvalue. \[ p(1)=1-5+6=2,\qquad p(2)=4-10+6=0,\qquad p(4)=16-20+6=2. \] So \(B\) has eigenvalues \(\{2,\,0,\,2\}\), and therefore \[ \det(B)=2\cdot 0\cdot 2=0,\qquad \operatorname{tr}(B)=2+0+2=4. \] Because \(0\) is an eigenvalue (equivalently \(\det B=0\)), \(B\) is not invertible — even though \(A\) itself is (none of \(1,2,4\) is zero).
Part (c) · When is \(B\) singular? \(B\) is singular exactly when \(p(\lambda)=0\) for some eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of \(A\). Factoring, \(p(\lambda)=(\lambda-2)(\lambda-3)\): this vanishes iff \(A\) has \(2\) or \(3\) as an eigenvalue. Here \(\lambda=2\) occurs — which is precisely why \(B\) is singular.
Remark · Cayley–Hamilton. If \(A\)'s characteristic polynomial were itself \(\lambda^{2}-5\lambda+\text{const}\), then \(p(A)\) collapses to a multiple of \(I\). E.g. \(A=\begin{bmatrix}4&5\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) has eigenvalues \(4,1\) and characteristic polynomial \(\lambda^{2}-5\lambda+4\); Cayley–Hamilton gives \(A^{2}-5A+4I=0\), hence \(A^{2}-5A+6I=2I\).
Answer. \(p(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-5\lambda+6\) maps each eigenvalue; \(B\) has eigenvalues \(2,\,0,\,2\). \(\det(B)=0\), \(\operatorname{tr}(B)=4\), so \(B\) is not invertible. \(B\) is singular iff \(A\) has eigenvalue \(2\) or \(3\) (the roots of \(p\)).
Watch out

The eigenvectors do not change — only the eigenvalues pass through \(p\). And invertibility does not transfer: an invertible \(A\) can easily produce a singular \(p(A)\), as it just did.

Key takeaway

Eigenvalues of \(p(A)\) are \(p(\text{eigenvalues of }A)\), with the same eigenvectors. So \(p(A)\) is singular \(\iff\) some eigenvalue of \(A\) is a root of \(p\).

Wait — really?

\(A\) is perfectly invertible, yet the innocent-looking combination \(A^{2}-5A+6I\) is singular — because \(p(t)=(t-2)(t-3)\) happens to have a root sitting exactly on one of \(A\)'s eigenvalues. Matrix polynomials can create zero out of nonzero ingredients.

Problem 5Hard

For the symmetric tridiagonal matrix

\[ A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&0\\1&2&1\\0&1&2\end{bmatrix}, \]

find its eigenvalues, an orthonormal set of eigenvectors, and hence write \(A=PDP^{\top}\) with \(P\) orthogonal.

What this tests. The spectral theorem in action: a symmetric matrix with irrational eigenvalues still yields an exact orthonormal eigenbasis and \(A=PDP^{\top}\).

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 1 · Eigenvalues. Expanding \(\det(A-\lambda I)\) along the first row: \[ \det(A-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)\big[(2-\lambda)^{2}-1\big]-1\cdot(2-\lambda)=(2-\lambda)\big[(2-\lambda)^{2}-2\big]=0. \] Hence \(\lambda=2\), or \((2-\lambda)^{2}=2\Rightarrow\lambda=2\pm\sqrt{2}\). The eigenvalues are \[ \lambda_1=2+\sqrt{2},\qquad \lambda_2=2,\qquad \lambda_3=2-\sqrt{2}. \]
Step 2 · Eigenvector for \(\lambda=2\). \((A-2I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\) reads \(y=0\) (rows 1 and 3) and \(x+z=0\) (row 2), so \(\mathbf{v}_2=(1,0,-1)^{\top}\).
Step 3 · Eigenvectors for \(\lambda=2\pm\sqrt{2}\). For \(\lambda=2+\sqrt{2}\): row 1 gives \(-\sqrt{2}\,x+y=0\Rightarrow y=\sqrt{2}\,x\), and row 3 gives \(y-\sqrt{2}\,z=0\Rightarrow y=\sqrt{2}\,z\), so \(x=z\). (Row 2 checks out: \(x-\sqrt{2}\,y+z=x-2x+x=0\). ✓) Thus \(\mathbf{v}_1=(1,\sqrt{2},1)^{\top}\). For \(\lambda=2-\sqrt{2}\): the same computation with \(-\sqrt2\) gives \(y=-\sqrt{2}\,x\) and \(x=z\), so \(\mathbf{v}_3=(1,-\sqrt{2},1)^{\top}\).
Step 4 · Orthogonality check. \(\mathbf{v}_1\cdot\mathbf{v}_2=1+0-1=0\), \(\mathbf{v}_1\cdot\mathbf{v}_3=1-2+1=0\), \(\mathbf{v}_2\cdot\mathbf{v}_3=1-0-1=0\) — mutually orthogonal, exactly as the spectral theorem guarantees for distinct eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix.
Step 5 · Normalise and assemble. \(\lVert\mathbf{v}_2\rVert=\sqrt{2}\) and \(\lVert\mathbf{v}_1\rVert=\lVert\mathbf{v}_3\rVert=\sqrt{1+2+1}=2\), so \[ P=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac12&\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}&\tfrac12\\[2pt] \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}&0&-\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\\[2pt] \tfrac12&-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}&\tfrac12\end{bmatrix},\qquad D=\begin{bmatrix}2+\sqrt2&0&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&2-\sqrt2\end{bmatrix},\qquad A=PDP^{\top}. \] Since the columns are orthonormal, \(P^{\top}P=I\), i.e. \(P^{-1}=P^{\top}\) — no matrix inversion needed.
Answer. Eigenvalues \(2+\sqrt2,\ 2,\ 2-\sqrt2\) with orthonormal eigenvectors \(\tfrac12(1,\sqrt2,1)^{\top}\), \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,0,-1)^{\top}\), \(\tfrac12(1,-\sqrt2,1)^{\top}\); \(A=PDP^{\top}\) with \(P\) orthogonal and \(D=\operatorname{diag}(2+\sqrt2,\,2,\,2-\sqrt2)\).
Watch out

Normalise each eigenvector by its own length: here \(\lVert\mathbf{v}_2\rVert=\sqrt2\) but \(\lVert\mathbf{v}_1\rVert=\lVert\mathbf{v}_3\rVert=2\) — one common scale factor will not do. Orthogonality comes free from symmetry; orthonormality is your job.

Key takeaway

Symmetric matrix \(\Rightarrow\) real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors, and \(A=PDP^{\top}\) with \(P^{-1}=P^{\top}\). The transpose replaces the inverse — the single biggest computational gift of the spectral theorem.

Wait — really?

An integer matrix produced irrational eigenvalues \(2\pm\sqrt2\) — and the eigenvector \((1,\sqrt2,1)^{\top}\) carries its own eigenvalue's \(\sqrt2\) inside its entries. Yet the factorization \(PDP^{\top}\) reproduces every integer entry of \(A\) exactly: the irrationals conspire and cancel.
