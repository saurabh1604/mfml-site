The quest for "similar"

Imagine this

Netflix wants to recommend your next film. Behind the screen, you are a vector; every movie is a vector; and the recommender's whole job is to find movies whose vectors are close to yours. Spotify does it with songs, search engines with documents, LLMs with meanings. One question runs the show: what exactly does "close" mean?

Your school reflex says: straight-line (Euclidean) distance. Perfectly valid — and just one option among many. If "director" matters more than "runtime" when comparing movies, you'd want a measure that weights the director dimension more heavily. The standard ruler isn't wrong; it's merely default. This unit is about building custom rulers — safely, so that geometry never breaks.

The route: ① pin down what any notion of length must satisfy (norms); ② properly meet the dot product — the one-number alignment meter you've been using since school; ③ reverse-engineer it into a blueprint and generalize it (inner products); ④ meet the engine that powers every legal ruler (symmetric positive-definite matrices); ⑤ collect the payoff — distances, angles, orthogonality, and the cleanest coordinate systems in mathematics (orthonormal bases, via Gram–Schmidt).

Norms — what "length" is allowed to mean

A norm is any function \(\|\cdot\|:V\to\mathbb{R}\) that behaves the way length must. Three non-negotiables: absolute homogeneity \(\|\lambda\mathbf{x}\|=|\lambda|\,\|\mathbf{x}\|\) (double the vector, double the length — flipping sign changes nothing); the triangle inequality \(\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|\le\|\mathbf{x}\|+\|\mathbf{y}\|\) (no detour is shorter than the direct route); and positive definiteness \(\|\mathbf{x}\|\ge 0\), with \(\|\mathbf{x}\|=0\) only for \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) (nothing real has negative size; only nothing has size zero).

Two rulers you already own:

\[\|\mathbf{x}\|_1=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_i| \quad\text{(Manhattan)}\qquad\qquad \|\mathbf{x}\|_2=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^2}\quad\text{(Euclidean)}\]

The dot product — one number that reads two vectors

Imagine this

You and a friend each rate four movies on a scale from \(-2\) (hated it) to \(+2\) (loved it). Do your tastes match? Here's a wonderfully cheap test: multiply your rating with theirs, movie by movie, and add everything up. Where you both loved or both hated, the product is positive — agreement scores points. Where one loved and the other hated, it's negative — a clash. Where either of you shrugged (0), the movie contributes nothing. One number out: positive means kindred spirits, negative means opposites, near zero means unrelated tastes. You have just re-invented the dot product — and, at scale, the engine inside every "customers like you also watched…".

Formally: for \(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^n\), the dot product (also written as the matrix product \(\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}\) — a row times a column, exactly Unit 1's row-times-column rule producing a \(1\times1\) result) is

\[\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}\;=\;\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\,y_i\]

One worked example, slowly. Let \(\mathbf{x}=(1,\,2,\,-1)\) and \(\mathbf{y}=(3,\,-1,\,2)\):

\[\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}=\underbrace{(1)(3)}_{+3}\;+\;\underbrace{(2)(-1)}_{-2}\;+\;\underbrace{(-1)(2)}_{-2}\;=\;-1\]

Coordinate 1 agrees (+3); coordinates 2 and 3 clash (−2 each); the tally lands slightly negative — these two vectors lean away from each other. That's the whole computation: pair up, multiply, add. What it means is richer, and two readings matter.

Aha — the ruler was hiding inside

Dot a vector with itself and watch Section 2's crow reappear: \(\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{x}=x_1^2+x_2^2+\cdots+x_n^2=\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^{\,2}\). The Euclidean norm is just the dot product measuring itself — length is self-similarity. This little identity is the hinge of the whole unit: it says whoever controls the product \(\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}\) automatically controls length too. Swap in a new product, get a new geometry — that's exactly where we're heading.

The second reading is geometric, and you should hold it as a promise for now: in the plane,

\[\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}\;=\;\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\|\mathbf{y}\|\cos\omega\]

where \(\omega\) is the angle between the vectors. Same general direction (\(\omega<90^\circ\)) → positive; perpendicular → exactly zero (that's where the tally of agreements and clashes cancels perfectly); opposed → negative. So the arithmetic tally and the geometric angle are the same number wearing different clothes. Inner products — the blueprint under the dot product

You now own the dot product: an alignment meter — positive when vectors point the same general way, negative when opposed, zero when perpendicular — that also secretly carries the ruler (\(\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{x}=\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2\)). Time for this unit's creative move. Take the formula apart like an engineer and ask: which properties of \(\sum_i x_iy_i\) actually do the work? Exactly three load-bearing properties appear. Bilinearity: linear in each slot — \(\Omega(\lambda\mathbf{x}+\psi\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})=\lambda\,\Omega(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})+\psi\,\Omega(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})\), and the same in the second argument. Symmetry: \(\Omega(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\Omega(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\) — x's similarity to y is y's similarity to x. Positive definiteness: \(\Omega(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})>0\) for every \(\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}\) — self-similarity is positive, because it's about to play the role of squared length.

Aha

The creative leap of the whole unit: stop treating those three properties as observations about the dot product, and start treating them as a blueprint. ANY function \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}\) that is bilinear, symmetric, and positive-definite is called an inner product, and the pair \((V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)\) an inner product space. The dot product becomes one citizen of a big family — and every member of the family will hand us lengths, distances, and angles that behave. The obvious next question: how do we manufacture new members?

5

The engine room — symmetric positive-definite matrices

Here's the master recipe. Notice first that the humble dot product is a matrix sandwich in disguise: \(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}^T I\,\mathbf{y}\) — the identity matrix is its engine. Now swap engines. The slides' theorem: on a finite-dimensional space with a chosen basis, \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\) is an inner product iff there is a symmetric, positive-definite (SPD) matrix \(A\) with

\[\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle=\hat{\mathbf{x}}^T A\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}.\]

Symmetric: \(A=A^T\) (inherits the symmetry axiom). Positive-definite: \(\mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{x}>0\) for all \(\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}\) (inherits the length axiom). Two facts fall out immediately, both from Unit 1's toolbox. SPD ⇒ full rank: \(\mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{x}>0\) for every non-zero \(\mathbf{x}\) means no non-zero vector is allowed in the null space — the blind spot is \(\{\mathbf{0}\}\), so \(A\) is invertible. Diagonals are positive: feed the canonical basis vector \(\mathbf{e}_i\) into both slots: \(\mathbf{e}_i^TA\mathbf{e}_i=A_{ii}>0\).

What does the engine do, geometrically? It reshapes space's sense of size. Watch the fingerprint — the unit ball — as you tune the engine:

Lengths, distances — and the inequality that runs everything

Every inner product ships with a free ruler: the induced norm \(\|\mathbf{x}\|=\sqrt{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle}\). (The traffic doesn't flow backwards: the Manhattan norm is a perfectly legal norm that comes from no inner product — inner-product geometry is the smoother, "round-ball" subfamily.) From the norm comes a distance: \(d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|=\sqrt{\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},\,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rangle}\) — with the dot product, that's the Euclidean distance. A distance function (a metric) keeps four promises: \(d\ge 0\); \(d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=0\iff\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}\); symmetry; and the triangle inequality \(d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\le d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+d(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})\).

Watch out — two gauges, opposite directions

Inner product and metric feel like twins but read in opposite directions: when \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) huddle close, the distance is small while the inner product is typically large; pull them apart and distance grows while the inner product shrinks. Recommender systems use both dialects — "minimize distance" and "maximize similarity" are the same instinct on different gauges. Don't mix them up mid-formula.

And now the inequality the whole unit leans on — Cauchy–Schwarz:

\[|\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle|\;\le\;\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\|\mathbf{y}\|\]

Alignment can never exceed the product of the sizes. It's the fact that makes "the angle between two vectors" a legal phrase — coming up next.

The slides' two-line proof — one clever vector does all the work

Take any \(\alpha\) and look at the vector \(\mathbf{u}-\alpha\mathbf{v}\). Its squared length can't be negative: \(\|\mathbf{u}-\alpha\mathbf{v}\|^2=\mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{u}-2\alpha\,\mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{v}+\alpha^2\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{v}\ge 0\). Now make the smartest possible choice, \(\alpha=\frac{\mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{v}}\) (you'll recognize it in Section 11 as the shadow coefficient), and the inequality collapses to \(\mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{u}-\frac{(\mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{v})^2}{\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{v}}\ge 0\), i.e. \((\mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{u})(\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{v})\ge(\mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{v})^2\) — Cauchy–Schwarz. Nothing used the dot product specifically: the proof runs verbatim for any inner product. Geometry: the residual after removing u's shadow along v has non-negative length — that's the whole theorem.

Angles — the similarity dial

Cauchy–Schwarz says the ratio below always lands in \([-1,1]\), so there is a unique angle \(\omega\in[0,\pi]\) with

\[\cos(\omega)=\frac{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\|\mathbf{y}\|}\]

— and this number, cosine similarity, is ML's favorite similarity dial: +1 same direction, 0 unrelated (perpendicular), −1 opposite. It measures orientation agreement while ignoring size — which is exactly why search engines compare a three-word query to a thousand-word document with it. Two vectors with \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle=0\) are orthogonal, written \(\mathbf{x}\perp\mathbf{y}\) (the zero vector, ever the freeloader, is orthogonal to everything).

Orthogonality is in the eye of the ruler

Here's the unit's sneakiest lesson, straight from the slides. Take \(\mathbf{x}=(1,1)\) and \(\mathbf{y}=(-1,1)\). Under the dot product: \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle=(1)(-1)+(1)(1)=0\) — perfectly orthogonal. Now switch the engine to \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\):

\[\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A=\mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{y}=2x_1y_1+x_2y_2=-2+1=-1,\qquad \cos\omega=\frac{-1}{\sqrt{3}\cdot\sqrt{3}}=-\frac13.\]

Same two arrows, new ruler — and they are no longer perpendicular (the angle is about \(109.5^\circ\)). Nothing moved except our definition of measurement. If you haven't already, hit the diag(2, 1) preset in Section 5's lens and watch the unit circle deform while the verdict flips. The deep point: "perpendicular" is not a property of two vectors; it's a property of two vectors plus a ruler. Choose the ruler to match the problem — that's feature weighting, Mahalanobis distance, and attention temperature, all in embryo.

Orthonormal matrices — the motions that don't lie

A square matrix whose columns are orthonormal (mutually \(\perp\), each of unit length) is called an orthogonal matrix, and it earns the cleanest identity in the subject:

\[A^TA=I=AA^T\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad A^T=A^{-1}.\]

The inverse is free — just the transpose. (And if the columns are orthonormal, so are the rows: for square matrices a left inverse is automatically the right inverse — if \(BA=I=AC\) then \(B=B(AC)=(BA)C=C\).) The payoff is behavioral: these transformations cannot distort measurement. Lengths survive: \(\|A\mathbf{x}\|^2=(A\mathbf{x})^TA\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^TA^TA\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{x}=\|\mathbf{x}\|^2\). Angles survive by the same cancellation inside \(\cos\omega\). They are geometry's rigid motions — rotations and reflections, like the rotation matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\) — and everything else (shears, stretches) is a liar that bends the ruler as it moves things.

The shadow machine — projection

Imagine this

A streetlamp shines straight down onto a road that runs along direction \(\mathbf{b}\). You hold a stick \(\mathbf{a}\) at an angle. The stick's shadow on the road is the part of \(\mathbf{a}\) that lies along \(\mathbf{b}\); the rest of the stick sticks straight up, perpendicular to the road. That split — "the part along, plus the part across" — is projection, and it's the single move Gram–Schmidt repeats to build perfect coordinate systems.

The formula falls out of one demand: the leftover must be orthogonal to \(\mathbf{b}\). Write the shadow as \(\alpha\mathbf{b}\) and require \(\langle\mathbf{a}-\alpha\mathbf{b},\,\mathbf{b}\rangle=0\); linearity gives \(\alpha=\frac{\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}\rangle}\), so

\[\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{a})=\frac{\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}\rangle}\,\mathbf{b}\qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf{a}-\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{a})\;\perp\;\mathbf{b}.\]

(You met \(\alpha\) already — it's the "smartest choice" from the Cauchy–Schwarz proof. Same coefficient, same geometry.)

Gram–Schmidt — cleaning a messy basis

Unit 2 taught you to find a basis. This unit's finale upgrades it to the best kind: an orthonormal basis \(\{\mathbf{e}_1,\dots,\mathbf{e}_n\}\) with \(\langle\mathbf{e}_i,\mathbf{e}_j\rangle=0\) for \(i\neq j\) and \(\langle\mathbf{e}_i,\mathbf{e}_i\rangle=1\) — every direction perpendicular, every direction unit length. (Only orthogonal, not unit length? That's an orthogonal basis — one normalization away.) Why chase it? Because in an orthonormal basis, coordinates stop requiring equation-solving: the address of any \(\mathbf{v}\) is just \(\lambda_i=\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{e}_i\rangle\) — read off by inner product. The canonical basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is one example; rotate it and you get infinitely many others.

The cleaning algorithm is the shadow machine on repeat: take your messy basis in order; normalize the first vector; from each later vector, subtract its shadows on everything already cleaned, then normalize what's left. Step through the companion's example in 3D:

The slides' power tool: Gram–Schmidt by Gaussian elimination (and why it works)

The course adds a beautiful industrial route. Stack the messy basis as columns of \(A\), then run plain Gaussian elimination on the augmented matrix \([A^TA \mid A^T]\) — when the left half reaches echelon form, the rows on the right are an orthogonal basis. The slides' example: \(\mathbf{v}_1=(3,1)\), \(\mathbf{v}_2=(2,2)\) give \(A^TA=\begin{pmatrix}10&8\\8&8\end{pmatrix}\), and elimination lands on

\[\left(\begin{array}{cc|cc}1&0.8&0.3&0.1\\0&1&-0.25&0.75\end{array}\right)\]

Check the right-hand rows: \((0.3,0.1)\cdot(-0.25,0.75)=-0.075+0.075=0\) — orthogonal (the first is \(\mathbf{v}_1\) rescaled; the second is what's left of \(\mathbf{v}_2\) after removing its \(\mathbf{v}_1\)-shadow). Normalize and you have an orthonormal basis. Why it works, in three moves: ① full column rank makes \(A^TA\) positive-definite (\(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\iff A^TA\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\), so the blind spots match and are trivial) — elimination needs no row swaps; ② elimination is multiplication by elementary matrices, i.e. \(A^TA=LU\) with \(L\) lower-triangular (Unit 1's recorded elimination!), and the right half becomes \(Q^T=L^{-1}A^T\); ③ then \(Q^TQ=L^{-1}(A^TA)L^{-T}=UL^{-T}\) is upper-triangular and symmetric — hence diagonal — which is precisely the statement that \(Q\)'s columns are orthogonal. Elimination doesn't just solve systems; run on \(A^TA\), it quietly performs Gram–Schmidt.

The whole story, in five lines

① A norm is any length that scales, obeys the triangle inequality, and vanishes only at \(\mathbf{0}\) — the taxi's ℓ₁ and the crow's ℓ₂ both qualify. ② An inner product is the dot product's blueprint made general — bilinear, symmetric, positive-definite — and every SPD matrix \(A\) builds one: \(\mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{y}\). ③ Each inner product induces a norm, a distance, and — thanks to Cauchy–Schwarz — an angle: \(\cos\omega=\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle/\|\mathbf{x}\|\|\mathbf{y}\|\), ML's similarity dial. ④ Orthogonality is relative to the ruler, orthonormal matrices are the motions that preserve every measurement, and random high-dimensional vectors are nearly orthogonal by default. ⑤ Gram–Schmidt (shadow-subtraction, or elimination on \([A^TA|A^T]\)) turns any basis into an orthonormal one — the cleanest grid a geometry can have.

The one thing to remember

Geometry is a choice. The inner product is the lens: pick the matrix, and you have picked what "long," "near," and "perpendicular" mean. Machine learning's obsession with similarity is exactly the craft of choosing that lens well — and Gram–Schmidt guarantees that whatever lens you choose, a perfect coordinate grid exists for it.

Problem 1Easy

On \(\mathbb{R}^3\) with the standard (Euclidean) inner product, let \(\mathbf{u} = (1,1,0)^\top\) and \(\mathbf{v} = (0,1,1)^\top\).
(a) Find all vectors \(\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^3\) orthogonal to both \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\), and write down the unit vectors of this form. [2 M]
(b) Compute the angle \(\omega\) between \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\). [1 M]
(c) Show that \(\{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\}\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and explain geometrically why \(\mathbf{w}\) is perpendicular to the plane spanned by \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\). [1 M] (4 marks)

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 1 · Turn "orthogonal to both" into equations (a). Idea: "orthogonal to both" means two dot-product equations. Write \(\mathbf{w} = (w_1, w_2, w_3)^\top\): \[ \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{u} = w_1 + w_2 = 0, \qquad \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{v} = w_2 + w_3 = 0. \]
Step 2 · Solve the little system. From these, \(w_1 = -w_2\) and \(w_3 = -w_2\). Letting the free variable be \(w_2 = t\), \[ \mathbf{w} = t\,(-1,\,1,\,-1)^\top, \qquad t\in\mathbb{R}. \]
Step 3 · Normalise for the unit vectors. Divide by the length \(\|(-1,1,-1)^\top\| = \sqrt{(-1)^2 + 1^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{3}\): \[ \mathbf{w} = \pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\,(-1,\,1,\,-1)^\top. \] Both signs: the orthogonal line carries exactly two unit vectors.
Step 4 · The angle (b). Use \(\cos\omega = \dfrac{\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}\). Here \(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{v} = 0 + 1 + 0 = 1\) and \(\|\mathbf{u}\| = \|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{2}\), so \[ \cos\omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \omega = 60^\circ. \]
Step 5 · Basis test (c). Three vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) form a basis exactly when their determinant is nonzero. With \(\mathbf{w} = (-1,1,-1)^\top\), \[ \det[\,\mathbf{u}\ \mathbf{v}\ \mathbf{w}\,] = \det\!\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = 1\,\big(1\cdot(-1) - 1\cdot 1\big) - 0 + (-1)\,\big(1\cdot 1 - 1\cdot 0\big) = -2 - 1 = -3 \neq 0, \] so \(\{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\}\) is a basis.
Step 6 · The geometric picture. \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) span a plane. Since \(\mathbf{w}\) was constructed to be orthogonal to both \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\), it is orthogonal to every vector in that plane — any such vector is \(\alpha\mathbf{u} + \beta\mathbf{v}\), and \(\mathbf{w}^\top(\alpha\mathbf{u} + \beta\mathbf{v}) = \alpha\cdot 0 + \beta\cdot 0 = 0\). So \(\mathbf{w}\) is the normal direction to the plane.

(Similarity vs. dissimilarity of data objects.) Three data points live in \(\mathbb{R}^3\): \[ \mathbf{p}_1 = (2,1,0)^\top, \qquad \mathbf{p}_2 = (0,1,2)^\top, \qquad \mathbf{p}_3 = (2,1,1)^\top. \] (a) Build the \(3\times 3\) dissimilarity matrix \(D\) using Euclidean distance. [2 M]
(b) Which two points are the most similar? [1 M]
(c) Compute the cosine similarity between \(\mathbf{p}_1\) and \(\mathbf{p}_3\). [1 M] (4 marks)

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 1 · Pairwise distances (a). Dissimilarity between two points is just the distance \(d(\mathbf{p}_i,\mathbf{p}_j) = \|\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j\|\). Compute each pair: \[ d(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_2) = \|(2,0,-2)^\top\| = \sqrt{4+0+4} = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}, \qquad d(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_3) = \|(0,0,-1)^\top\| = 1, \] \[ d(\mathbf{p}_2,\mathbf{p}_3) = \|(-2,0,1)^\top\| = \sqrt{4+0+1} = \sqrt{5}. \]
Step 2 · Assemble the matrix. Place these in a symmetric matrix with zeros on the diagonal (a point's distance to itself is \(0\)): \[ D = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2\sqrt{2} & 1 \\ 2\sqrt{2} & 0 & \sqrt{5} \\ 1 & \sqrt{5} & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]
Step 3 · Most similar pair (b). "Most similar" \(=\) smallest dissimilarity. The smallest off-diagonal entry is \(d(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_3) = 1\), so \(\mathbf{p}_1\) and \(\mathbf{p}_3\) are the most similar.
Step 4 · Cosine similarity (c). Cosine similarity is \(\cos\theta = \dfrac{\mathbf{p}_1^\top\mathbf{p}_3}{\|\mathbf{p}_1\|\,\|\mathbf{p}_3\|}\): \[ \mathbf{p}_1^\top\mathbf{p}_3 = 2\cdot 2 + 1\cdot 1 + 0\cdot 1 = 5, \qquad \|\mathbf{p}_1\| = \sqrt{5}, \qquad \|\mathbf{p}_3\| = \sqrt{6}, \] \[ \cos\theta = \frac{5}{\sqrt{5}\,\sqrt{6}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{30}} = \frac{\sqrt{30}}{6} \approx 0.913. \] (Close to \(1\), confirming \(\mathbf{p}_1\) and \(\mathbf{p}_3\) point in nearly the same direction.)
Answer. (a) \(D\) as above, symmetric with zero diagonal. (b) \(\mathbf{p}_1\) and \(\mathbf{p}_3\), at distance \(1\). (c) \(\cos\theta = 5/\sqrt{30} = \sqrt{30}/6 \approx 0.913\).
Watch out

In a dissimilarity matrix, small means similar — scanning for the largest entry answers the opposite question. And the diagonal zeros are not data: every point is at distance \(0\) from itself, so only off-diagonal entries compete.

Two different rulers for "similar": Euclidean distance measures closeness in position, cosine similarity measures alignment of direction. Here they happen to agree (\(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_3\) win on both); in general they need not.

Wait — really?

Cosine similarity is blind to length: \((2,1,0)^\top\) and \((4,2,0)^\top\) have cosine similarity exactly \(1\) even though they sit \(\sqrt{5}\) apart. That is why it is the default in text retrieval — a tweet and an essay about the same topic point the same way; they just differ in length.

Problem 3Medium

Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) and define a new inner product on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) by \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = \mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{y}\).
(a) Show \(A\) is symmetric positive definite, so \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_A\) is a genuine inner product. [1 M]
(b) The vectors \(\mathbf{x} = (1,0)^\top\) and \(\mathbf{y} = (0,1)^\top\) are orthogonal in the dot product. Are they still orthogonal under \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_A\)? Find the angle between them in this new inner product. [2 M]
(c) Find a vector \(\mathbf{y}' = (a,1)^\top\) that is orthogonal to \(\mathbf{x}\) with respect to \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_A\). [1 M] (4 marks)

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 1 · Certify the inner product (a). \(A\) is symmetric since \(A^\top = A\). Sylvester's criterion (all leading minors positive): \[ D_1 = 2 > 0, \qquad D_2 = \det A = 2\cdot 2 - 1\cdot 1 = 3 > 0. \] So \(A\) is positive definite. A symmetric, positive-definite matrix always makes \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{y}\) a valid inner product: it is bilinear, symmetric, and \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{x} > 0\) for \(\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}\).
Step 2 · Test orthogonality (b). Compute \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = \mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{y}\). First \(A\mathbf{y} = A(0,1)^\top = (1,2)^\top\), hence \[ \langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = \mathbf{x}^\top(A\mathbf{y}) = (1,0)\cdot(1,2) = 1 \neq 0. \] They are not orthogonal here.
Step 3 · The \(A\)-norms. To get the angle we also need \[ \|\mathbf{x}\|_A = \sqrt{\mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{x}} = \sqrt{A_{11}} = \sqrt{2}, \qquad \|\mathbf{y}\|_A = \sqrt{A_{22}} = \sqrt{2}. \]
Step 4 · The \(A\)-angle. \[ \cos\theta = \frac{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_A\,\|\mathbf{y}\|_A} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \theta = 60^\circ. \] Takeaway (from the source): orthogonality depends on which inner product you use.
Step 5 · Force orthogonality (c). We need \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}'\rangle_A = 0\). Note \(\mathbf{x}^\top A = (1,0)\,A = (2,1)\), so \[ \langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}'\rangle_A = (2,1)\cdot(a,1) = 2a + 1 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; a = -\tfrac{1}{2}. \] Thus \(\mathbf{y}' = \left(-\tfrac{1}{2},\,1\right)^\top\).
Answer. (a) SPD by Sylvester: \(D_1 = 2 > 0\), \(D_2 = 3 > 0\). (b) No — \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = 1 \neq 0\); the angle in the \(A\)-geometry is \(\theta = 60^\circ\). (c) \(a = -\tfrac12\), i.e. \(\mathbf{y}' = (-\tfrac12,\,1)^\top\).
Watch out

When you switch inner products, switch everything: the cosine formula needs the \(A\)-inner product on top and the \(A\)-norms below. Mixing \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = 1\) with the Euclidean norms \(\|\mathbf{x}\| = \|\mathbf{y}\| = 1\) would give \(\cos\theta = 1\), i.e. a \(0^\circ\) angle — badly wrong.

Key takeaway

Orthogonality is relative to the inner product. The same pair of vectors can be perpendicular in one geometry and at \(60^\circ\) in another — and part (c) shows you can always re-engineer a vector to restore orthogonality in the new geometry.

Wait — really?

The coordinate axes themselves — \(\mathbf{e}_1\) and \(\mathbf{e}_2\), the gridlines of the plane — meet at \(60^\circ\), not \(90^\circ\), the moment \(A\) defines the geometry. Perpendicularity is not a property of two arrows; it is a property of the inner product that measures them.

Problem 4Medium

On \(\mathbb{R}^3\) use the weighted inner product \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = \mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{y}\) with \[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad(\text{SPD}). \] Let \(\mathbf{a} = (1,-1,0)^\top\) and \(\mathbf{b} = (1,0,2)^\top\).
(a) Compute \(\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\rangle_A\), \(\|\mathbf{a}\|_A\), and \(\|\mathbf{b}\|_A\). [2 M]
(b) Find the orthogonal projection \(\mathbf{p}\) of \(\mathbf{b}\) onto the line spanned by \(\mathbf{a}\), in this inner product. [2 M]
(c) Verify that the residual \(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p}\) is \(A\)-orthogonal to \(\mathbf{a}\) (this is what makes \(\mathbf{p}\) the orthogonal projection). [1 M] (5 marks)

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 1 · One matrix–vector product does most of the work (a). Trick: compute the matrix–vector product once, then take dot products. With \[ A\mathbf{a} = A(1,-1,0)^\top = (2-1,\ 1-2,\ 0)^\top = (1,-1,0)^\top: \] \[ \langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\rangle_A = \mathbf{b}^\top(A\mathbf{a}) = (1,0,2)\cdot(1,-1,0) = 1, \qquad \|\mathbf{a}\|_A^2 = \mathbf{a}^\top(A\mathbf{a}) = (1,-1,0)\cdot(1,-1,0) = 2. \]
Step 2 · The norm of \(\mathbf{b}\). For \(\|\mathbf{b}\|_A\), use \(A\mathbf{b} = A(1,0,2)^\top = (2,1,2)^\top\): \[ \|\mathbf{b}\|_A^2 = \mathbf{b}^\top(A\mathbf{b}) = (1,0,2)\cdot(2,1,2) = 2 + 0 + 4 = 6. \] So \(\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\rangle_A = 1\), \(\|\mathbf{a}\|_A = \sqrt{2}\), \(\|\mathbf{b}\|_A = \sqrt{6}\).
Step 3 · Project (b). Projection formula (same as usual, but every inner product is the \(A\)-one): \[ \mathbf{p} = \frac{\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\rangle_A}{\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}\rangle_A}\,\mathbf{a} = \frac{1}{2}\,(1,-1,0)^\top = \left(\tfrac{1}{2},\,-\tfrac{1}{2},\,0\right)^\top. \]
Step 4 · Check the residual (c). The residual is \[ \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p} = (1,0,2)^\top - \left(\tfrac{1}{2},\,-\tfrac{1}{2},\,0\right)^\top = \left(\tfrac{1}{2},\ \tfrac{1}{2},\ 2\right)^\top. \] Check \(A\)-orthogonality using the same \(A\mathbf{a} = (1,-1,0)^\top\): \[ \langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle_A = \mathbf{r}^\top(A\mathbf{a}) = \left(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2},2\right)\cdot(1,-1,0) = \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2} + 0 = 0. \;\checkmark \] Since \(\mathbf{r} \perp_A \mathbf{a}\), \(\mathbf{p}\) is indeed the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf{b}\) onto the line through \(\mathbf{a}\).
Answer. (a) \(\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\rangle_A = 1\), \(\|\mathbf{a}\|_A = \sqrt{2}\), \(\|\mathbf{b}\|_A = \sqrt{6}\). (b) \(\mathbf{p} = \left(\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, 0\right)^\top\). (c) \(\langle\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{p},\,\mathbf{a}\rangle_A = 0\) ✓ — the residual is \(A\)-orthogonal to \(\mathbf{a}\).
Watch out

Verify with the same inner product you projected in: the defining property is \(\langle\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{p},\,\mathbf{a}\rangle_A = 0\). In this particular problem a plain dot-product check happens to give \(0\) too — pure luck, because \(A\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{a}\) here (see below). In a general weighted geometry the residual is \(A\)-orthogonal, not dot-orthogonal, to \(\mathbf{a}\).

Key takeaway

The projection recipe never changes: \(\mathbf{p} = \dfrac{\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}\rangle}\,\mathbf{a}\) — swap in whichever inner product defines the geometry. The residual-orthogonality check is the certificate that \(\mathbf{p}\) really is the closest point on the line.

Wait — really?

\(A\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{a}\): the vector \(\mathbf{a} = (1,-1,0)^\top\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(1\). That is why every \(A\)-computation involving \(\mathbf{a}\) collapsed to a plain dot product — and why the Euclidean projection of \(\mathbf{b}\) onto \(\mathbf{a}\) would land on the same \(\mathbf{p}\) here. Move \(\mathbf{a}\) off an eigendirection and the two projections part ways.

Consider the symmetric matrix \(A(t) = \begin{bmatrix} t & 3 \\ 3 & t \end{bmatrix}\), \(t\in\mathbb{R}\).
(a) Classify \(A(t)\) for all real \(t\) as positive definite, positive semidefinite, indefinite, negative semidefinite, or negative definite. [3 M]
(b) For which \(t\) does \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle = \mathbf{x}^\top A(t)\,\mathbf{y}\) define a valid inner product? At the borderline value \(t = 3\), exhibit a nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v}\) with \(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle = 0\). [2 M] (5 marks)

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 1 · Find the eigenvalues (a). Method: a symmetric matrix is classified by the signs of its eigenvalues. Solve \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\): \[ (t-\lambda)^2 - 9 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; t - \lambda = \pm 3 \;\Longrightarrow\; \lambda = t - 3 \ \text{ or } \ \lambda = t + 3. \]
Step 2 · Read off the signs. Now just read off the signs of the two eigenvalues \(t-3\) and \(t+3\): \[ \begin{aligned} t > 3 &: \ \text{both positive} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{positive definite}\\ t = 3 &: \ \lambda = 6,\,0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{positive semidefinite}\\ -3 < t < 3 &: \ \text{one } +,\ \text{one } - \;\Rightarrow\; \text{indefinite}\\ t = -3 &: \ \lambda = 0,\,-6 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{negative semidefinite}\\ t < -3 &: \ \text{both negative} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{negative definite} \end{aligned} \]

Semidefinite is not "almost good enough": at \(t = 3\) the axiom that dies is exactly the strict inequality \(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle > 0\) for \(\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}\). A geometry in which a nonzero vector has length zero cannot measure distances — so the borderline value is excluded, not included.

For a symmetric matrix, definiteness = the sign pattern of its eigenvalues, full stop. Wait — really?

One dial sweeps all five classes — and the eigenvectors never move. For every \(t\), \(A(t)\,(1,1)^\top = (t+3)(1,1)^\top\) and \(A(t)\,(1,-1)^\top = (t-3)(1,-1)^\top\). Turning \(t\) just slides the two eigenvalues \(t \pm 3\) along the number line past zero, while the axes of the quadratic form stay frozen at \(45^\circ\).

Problem 6Hard

(Positive definiteness over \(\mathbb{C}\).) For a real parameter \(k\), consider \[ A(k) = \begin{bmatrix} k & 1+i \\ 1-i & 2 \end{bmatrix}. \] For \(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^2\), let \(\mathbf{u}^*\) denote the conjugate transpose and set \(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle = \mathbf{u}^* A(k)\,\mathbf{v}\).
(a) Show \(A(k)\) is Hermitian for every real \(k\). [1 M]
(b) Find all real \(k\) for which \(A(k)\) is positive definite — equivalently, for which \(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle = \mathbf{u}^* A(k)\,\mathbf{v}\) is a valid inner product on \(\mathbb{C}^2\). [2 M]
(c) At the borderline value \(k = 1\) the matrix is only positive semidefinite. Find a nonzero vector \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^2\) with \(\mathbf{z}^* A(1)\,\mathbf{z} = 0\), showing the form degenerates there. [2 M] (5 marks)

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 1 · Hermitian check (a). A matrix is Hermitian when \(A^* = A\), i.e. the diagonal entries are real and the two off-diagonal entries are complex conjugates of each other. Here the diagonal is \(k,\,2\) (real, because \(k\) is real) and the off-diagonals satisfy \(\overline{1+i} = 1-i\). Hence \(A(k)^* = A(k)\) for every real \(k\).
Step 2 · Sylvester over \(\mathbb{C}\) (b). Sylvester's criterion also works for Hermitian matrices: positive definite \(\iff\) all leading principal minors are positive (they come out real automatically). The minors are \[ D_1 = k, \qquad D_2 = \det A(k) = 2k - (1+i)(1-i). \]
Step 3 · The crucial complex product. It is a squared modulus: \[ (1+i)(1-i) = 1 - i^2 = 2 = |1+i|^2, \qquad\text{so}\qquad D_2 = 2k - 2 = 2(k-1). \]
Step 4 · Solve the inequalities. Requiring \(D_1 > 0\) and \(D_2 > 0\) gives \(k > 0\) and \(k > 1\), so \[ A(k) \ \text{is positive definite} \iff k > 1. \]
Step 5 · The degenerate direction at \(k = 1\) (c). At \(k = 1\) we have \(D_2 = 0\), so \(A(1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1+i \\ 1-i & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) is singular (\(D_1 = 1 > 0\), \(D_2 = 0\): positive semidefinite). Idea: any nonzero null-space vector works, since \(A(1)\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0} \Rightarrow \mathbf{z}^* A(1)\,\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{z}^*\mathbf{0} = 0\). From the first row, \(z_1 + (1+i)z_2 = 0\); choosing \(z_2 = 1\) gives \(z_1 = -(1+i)\), so \[ \mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix} -(1+i) \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]
Step 6 · Verify it is a null vector. Using \((1-i)(1+i) = 2\): \[ A(1)\,\mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\cdot\big({-(1+i)}\big) + (1+i)\cdot 1 \\ (1-i)\cdot\big({-(1+i)}\big) + 2\cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2+2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \] Hence \(\mathbf{z}^* A(1)\,\mathbf{z} = 0\) with \(\mathbf{z} \neq \mathbf{0}\): at \(k = 1\) the form is not positive definite, so it is not an inner product.
Answer. (a) \(A(k)^* = A(k)\) for all real \(k\). (b) \(A(k)\) is positive definite \(\iff k > 1\). (c) \(\mathbf{z} = (-(1+i),\,1)^\top \neq \mathbf{0}\) satisfies \(A(1)\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}\), hence \(\mathbf{z}^* A(1)\,\mathbf{z} = 0\).
Watch out

Over \(\mathbb{C}\), transpose is not enough — use the conjugate transpose \(\mathbf{u}^*\). And the off-diagonal product is \((1+i)(1-i) = |1+i|^2 = 2\), not \((1+i)^2 = 2i\). Writing \(D_2 = 2k - 2i\) (a complex determinant for a Hermitian matrix — impossible) is the classic slip.

Key takeaway

Hermitian + positive definite is the complex analogue of symmetric + positive definite: Sylvester's criterion still runs, the leading minors are automatically real, and only \(k > 1\) — both minors strictly positive — buys a genuine inner product on \(\mathbb{C}^2\).

Wait — really?

At \(k = 1\) there exists a nonzero vector of length zero: \(\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}\rangle = 0\) although \(\mathbf{z} \neq \mathbf{0}\). The geometry has a direction it simply cannot see — which is precisely the disease the positive-definiteness axiom exists to rule out.

Step 1 · Two matrix–vector products (a). Compute \(A\mathbf{x}\) and \(A\mathbf{y}\) once: \[ A\mathbf{x} = A(1,1)^\top = (3+1,\ 1+2)^\top = (4,3)^\top, \qquad A\mathbf{y} = A(1,-1)^\top = (3-1,\ 1-2)^\top = (2,-1)^\top. \]
Step 2 · Norms and inner product. \[ \|\mathbf{x}\|_A^2 = \mathbf{x}^\top(A\mathbf{x}) = (1,1)\cdot(4,3) = 7, \qquad \|\mathbf{y}\|_A^2 = \mathbf{y}^\top(A\mathbf{y}) = (1,-1)\cdot(2,-1) = 2 + 1 = 3, \] \[ \langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = \mathbf{x}^\top(A\mathbf{y}) = (1,1)\cdot(2,-1) = 1. \] So \(\|\mathbf{x}\|_A = \sqrt{7}\), \(\|\mathbf{y}\|_A = \sqrt{3}\), \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = 1\).
Step 3 · Cauchy–Schwarz (b). \[ |\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A| = 1 \ \le\ \|\mathbf{x}\|_A\,\|\mathbf{y}\|_A = \sqrt{7}\cdot\sqrt{3} = \sqrt{21} \approx 4.58. \;\checkmark \]
Step 4 · Distance and triangle inequality (c). Distance uses the same norm on the difference. With \(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y} = (0,2)^\top\) and \(A(0,2)^\top = (2,4)^\top\): \[ \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_A^2 = (0,2)\cdot(2,4) = 8 \;\Longrightarrow\; d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83. \] Triangle inequality: \[ \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_A = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83 \ \le\ \|\mathbf{x}\|_A + \|\mathbf{y}\|_A = \sqrt{7} + \sqrt{3} \approx 4.38. \;\checkmark \]
Answer. (a) \(\|\mathbf{x}\|_A = \sqrt{7}\), \(\|\mathbf{y}\|_A = \sqrt{3}\), \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = 1\). (b) \(1 \le \sqrt{21} \approx 4.58\) ✓. (c) \(d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83 \le \sqrt{7}+\sqrt{3} \approx 4.38\) ✓.
Watch out

The distance must be measured with \(A\) on the difference: \((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^\top A\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}) = 8\), so \(d = 2\sqrt{2}\) — not the Euclidean \(\|(0,2)^\top\| = 2\). Every length in this problem, the distance included, is an \(A\)-length.

Key takeaway

Cauchy–Schwarz and the triangle inequality are theorems of every inner-product space: if \(A\) is genuinely SPD they cannot fail. So "verifying" them is really a sanity check on your arithmetic — if such a check ever fails, either the computation or the claimed positive-definiteness is wrong.

Wait — really?

In the plain dot product these two vectors are orthogonal — \(\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{y} = 1 - 1 = 0\) — yet under \(A\) their inner product is \(1\), giving \(\cos\theta_A = 1/\sqrt{21} \approx 0.218\): an angle of about \(77.4^\circ\). The matrix \(A\) literally bends the right angle.
