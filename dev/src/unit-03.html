The quest for "similar"

Imagine this

You finish a film on Netflix. Two seconds later it says: "Because you watched this, try…". How does it choose?

Behind the screen, you are a list of numbers: how much you like action, romance, comedy, and so on. Every film is a list of numbers too. The app looks for films whose list is close to yours. Spotify does the same with songs. Google does it with web pages. ChatGPT does it with the meaning of words.

So everything rests on one small question: what does "close" actually mean?

At school, "close" meant one thing: put a ruler between the two points and read the distance. That works. But it is only one way to measure.

Think of choosing a film. Maybe the director matters to you far more than the running time. Then a good "closeness" score should care more about the director and less about the running time. That is a different ruler. It is not wrong. It just measures what you care about.

This unit teaches you how to build your own rulers, and how to make sure they never break. Here is the path, one step at a time:

  • ①Length — what rules must any "length" follow? (norms)
  • ②The dot product — one number that tells you if two lists agree.
  • ③Your own ruler — the recipe for making new ones (inner products and the matrices behind them).
  • ④Distance and angle — "how far apart?" and "pointing the same way?"
  • ⑤Perfect grids — how to turn any set of directions into clean, square ones (Gram–Schmidt).

In one sentence: machine learning keeps asking "how similar are these two things?", and this unit is about the rulers that answer it.

Norms — what "length" is allowed to mean

Imagine this

You stand at a street corner. Your friend is 3 blocks east and 4 blocks north. How far away is she?

A crow flies straight over the buildings: 5 blocks. A taxi has to follow the streets, 3 blocks and then 4 blocks: 7 blocks.

Who is right? Both. They just measure "far" in different ways.

So "length" is not one fixed thing. It is a choice of rule. Mathematicians call such a rule a norm, and they write the length of an arrow \(\mathbf{x}\) as \(\|\mathbf{x}\|\) (read it: "the length of x").

You can invent any rule you like. But it must keep three common-sense promises, or it is not really a length:

The promiseIn everyday wordsIn symbols
ScalingWalk twice as far, and the length doubles. Walking backwards is still the same distance.\(\|\lambda\mathbf{x}\|=|\lambda|\,\|\mathbf{x}\|\)
No shortcutA detour is never shorter than going straight.\(\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|\le\|\mathbf{x}\|+\|\mathbf{y}\|\)
Only "nowhere" is zeroLengths are never negative. Only staying put has length 0.\(\|\mathbf{x}\|\ge0\), and \(=0\) only for \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\)

The two rules from our story, written out. For the point \((3,4)\):

\(\displaystyle \|\mathbf{x}\|_1=|x_1|+|x_2|+\cdots\)
taxi: add up the blocks · \(3+4=7\)
\(\displaystyle \|\mathbf{x}\|_2=\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2+\cdots}\)
crow: Pythagoras · \(\sqrt{9+16}=5\)

Each rule has a "fingerprint": the shape made by all the points at length exactly 1. We call it the unit ball. For the crow it is a round circle (a sphere in 3D). For the taxi it is a diamond. Why a diamond? Because for a taxi, one block east costs the same as one block north, so the points with "1 block total" form a tilted square.

The dot product — one number that reads two vectors

Imagine this

You and a friend rate four films from \(-2\) (hated it) to \(+2\) (loved it). Do you have the same taste?

Here is a quick test. For each film, multiply your rating by your friend's rating. Then add the four answers.

  • 😀You both loved it, or you both hated it → the product is positive. Agreement adds points.
  • 😠One loved it, one hated it → the product is negative. A clash takes points away.
  • 😐One of you didn't care (0) → the product is 0. That film doesn't count.

Big positive total: same taste. Big negative total: opposite taste. Near zero: your tastes are unrelated.

That score has a name: the dot product. You just invented it. For two lists \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) it is

\[\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}\;=\;\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}\;=\;x_1y_1+x_2y_2+\cdots+x_ny_n\]

Pair up, multiply, add. (The \(\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}\) form is Unit 1's "row times column", giving one number.) One example, slowly. Take \(\mathbf{x}=(1,\,2,\,-1)\) and \(\mathbf{y}=(3,\,-1,\,2)\):

\[\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}=\underbrace{(1)(3)}_{+3\ \text{agree}}\;+\;\underbrace{(2)(-1)}_{-2\ \text{clash}}\;+\;\underbrace{(-1)(2)}_{-2\ \text{clash}}\;=\;-1\]

One agreement, two clashes. The total is a little below zero, so these two lean slightly away from each other.

Aha — the ruler was hiding inside

Now compare a list with itself: \(\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{x}=x_1^2+x_2^2+\cdots\). That is exactly the crow's length, squared! For \((3,4)\): \(9+16=25=5^2\).

So length is just "how much you agree with yourself." This tiny fact is the key to the whole unit. Whoever decides how two lists are multiplied also decides what "length" means. Change the multiplication, and you get a new geometry.

There is also a picture behind the score. For two arrows with an angle \(\omega\) between them:

\[\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}\;=\;\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\|\mathbf{y}\|\cos\omega\]

Read it like this. Pointing the same general way (angle below 90°): positive. At a perfect right angle: exactly zero, because the agreements and the clashes cancel. Pointing opposite ways: negative. So the "rating game" number and the angle are one and the same thing. Sections 6 and 7 show why this is always allowed.

The realization

\[\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}=\sum_i x_iy_i\]

The dot product is an agreement score. Every "people like you also watched…" system is built on this one line of arithmetic.

In one sentence: multiply matching entries and add them up; the total says how much two lists agree, and a list's agreement with itself is its length squared.

Inner products — the blueprint under the dot product

Imagine this

A chef tastes a great dish and asks: "What makes this work? Salt, acid, heat." Once she knows the three things that matter, she can cook new dishes that also work.

We will do the same with the dot product. What makes it such a good agreement score? Find the key ingredients. Then we can cook up new scores that work just as well.

It turns out only three ingredients matter. Call our score \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\) (read it: "the score of x with y").

  • ①Fair with sums and scaling. Double one list, and the score doubles. Split a list into two parts, and the score splits too. Why we need it: it lets us do ordinary algebra with the score. (The formal name is bilinear: \(\langle\lambda\mathbf{x}+\psi\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\rangle=\lambda\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\rangle+\psi\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\rangle\), and the same for the second slot.)
  • ②Same both ways. How much you agree with me equals how much I agree with you: \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}\rangle\). (Name: symmetric.)
  • ③Agreeing with yourself is always positive. \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle>0\) for every list that is not all zeros. Why we need it: this number is about to become "length squared", and a length squared can't be negative. (Name: positive definite.)
The realization

Any score with these three ingredients is called an inner product. The dot product is just the most famous one. Every inner product gives you its own length, its own distance and its own angle, and they all behave sensibly. The natural next question: how do we cook up new ones? (Next section.)

Pause & predict

Try this score on 2-number lists: \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle=x_1y_1-x_2y_2\) (the second pair counts negative). It is fair with sums and the same both ways. Which ingredient does it break?

In one sentence: an inner product is any agreement score that is fair with sums, the same both ways, and always positive for a list with itself.

The engine room — symmetric positive-definite matrices

Imagine this

A mixing desk in a music studio has a slider for each channel. Push the bass slider up and the bass counts for more in the final sound.

A matrix can be a mixing desk for our agreement score. It decides how much each direction counts, and even how directions mix together.

Here is the trick. The ordinary dot product secretly has a matrix inside it, the identity \(I\) (all sliders at 1):

\[\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}^T I\,\mathbf{y}\]

Now swap \(I\) for another matrix \(A\). You get a new score:

\[\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A=\mathbf{x}^T A\,\mathbf{y}\]

Example: \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\) gives \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A=2x_1y_1+x_2y_2\). The first direction now counts double.

Does every matrix give a good score? No. It needs the two properties from the last section, turned into matrix language:

  • ②Symmetric: \(A=A^T\) (the matrix is its own mirror image across the diagonal). This gives "same both ways".
  • ③Positive definite: \(\mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{x}>0\) for every non-zero \(\mathbf{x}\). This gives "lengths are positive".

Such a matrix is called SPD (symmetric positive-definite). The big fact from the slides: every inner product on \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is \(\mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{y}\) for some SPD matrix \(A\), and every SPD matrix gives one. So "design a ruler" means "pick an SPD matrix".

Two quick facts follow straight away:

  • •The diagonal is positive. Test the first direction \(\mathbf{e}_1=(1,0,\dots)\) against itself: \(\mathbf{e}_1^TA\mathbf{e}_1=A_{11}\). That is a length squared, so \(A_{11}>0\). Same for every diagonal entry.
  • •\(A\) can be undone (it is invertible). If \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) for some non-zero \(\mathbf{x}\), then \(\mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{x}=0\), which is not allowed. So no non-zero arrow is squashed to zero.

The realization

The matrix \(A\) reshapes the unit ball. The identity gives a round ball. A diagonal \(A\) squashes it into an egg along the axes. Off-diagonal numbers tilt the egg. A matrix that is not positive-definite tears the ball open, because some arrows would get a negative length squared.

How do you test a matrix for SPD?

"Positive for every arrow" sounds like infinitely many checks. For a 2×2 symmetric matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{pmatrix}\) it boils down to two quick checks:

  • 1the top-left number is positive: \(a>0\);
  • 2the determinant is positive: \(ad-b^2>0\).

Example: \(\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\): \(2>0\) and \(4-1=3>0\). SPD ✓. And \(\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}\): \(1>0\) but \(1-4=-3\). Not SPD ✗. For bigger matrices, check every top-left square block ("leading minor"). The drawer shows where this comes from: the school trick of completing the square.

Prove it · the 2×2 SPD test (Sylvester's criterion) — derived

Claim. A symmetric \(A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{pmatrix}\) is positive-definite iff \(a>0\) and \(ad-b^2>0\) (the two leading minors are positive).

1
Write out the quadratic form for \(\mathbf{x}=(x_1,x_2)\): \[\mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{x}=a\,x_1^2+2b\,x_1x_2+d\,x_2^2\] Multiply the sandwich out: the two off-diagonal \(b\)'s give the same cross term \(b\,x_1x_2\) twice.
2
Test the first axis. Put \(\mathbf{x}=(1,0)\): the form equals \(a\). So positive-definiteness forces \(a>0\). One well-chosen test vector settles the sign of \(a\). From now on we may divide by \(a\).
3
Complete the square in \(x_1\): \[a\,x_1^2+2b\,x_1x_2+d\,x_2^2=a\Big(x_1+\frac{b}{a}x_2\Big)^2+\Big(d-\frac{b^2}{a}\Big)x_2^2\] Expand the right side to check: \(a(x_1+\tfrac{b}{a}x_2)^2=a x_1^2+2b\,x_1x_2+\tfrac{b^2}{a}x_2^2\); the extra \(\tfrac{b^2}{a}x_2^2\) is cancelled by the second bracket. Nothing lost, nothing added.
4
Read the sign. The first term is \(a\cdot(\text{square})\ge0\). If \(d-\tfrac{b^2}{a}>0\), both terms are \(\ge0\) and they vanish together only when \(x_2=0\) and \(x_1+\tfrac{b}{a}x_2=0\), i.e. \(x_1=0\). So the form is \(>0\) for every \(\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}\). A weighted sum of squares with positive weights is positive unless every square is zero — and that only happens at the zero vector.
5
Conversely, if \(d-\tfrac{b^2}{a}\le0\), the vector \(\mathbf{x}=(-\tfrac{b}{a},1)\) kills the first square and leaves \(\mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{x}=d-\tfrac{b^2}{a}\le0\): not positive-definite. Multiply the condition by \(a>0\): \(d-\tfrac{b^2}{a}>0\iff ad-b^2>0\). So SPD \(\iff a>0\) and \(\det A>0\). ∎ The witness vector in step 5 is exactly how the practice problems "exhibit a vector with negative squared length". The same completing-the-square idea, repeated column by column, gives the \(n\times n\) version: all leading minors positive.
Worked example · a 3×3 matrix, proved SPD by completing the square (the exam technique)

Take \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0\\-1&2&-1\\0&-1&2\end{pmatrix}\). Multiply out the form and complete squares left to right, one variable at a time:

\[\begin{aligned}\mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{x}&=2x_1^2-2x_1x_2+2x_2^2-2x_2x_3+2x_3^2\\&=2\Big(x_1-\tfrac12x_2\Big)^2+\tfrac32x_2^2-2x_2x_3+2x_3^2\\&=2\Big(x_1-\tfrac12x_2\Big)^2+\tfrac32\Big(x_2-\tfrac23x_3\Big)^2+\tfrac43x_3^2.\end{aligned}\]

Three squares with positive weights \(2,\tfrac32,\tfrac43\): the form is \(>0\) unless \(x_3=0\), then \(x_2=0\), then \(x_1=0\). So \(A\) is SPD. Cross-check with Sylvester: the leading minors are \(2,\ 3,\ 4\) — all positive — and the three weights are their ratios \(2,\ 3/2,\ 4/3\). Completing the square and the minors test are the same computation in two costumes.

Lengths, distances — and the inequality that runs everything

Every inner product comes with a free ruler and a free tape measure:

  • 📏Length: agree with yourself, then take the square root: \(\|\mathbf{x}\|=\sqrt{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle}\).
  • 📍Distance between two points: the length of the gap between them, \(d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|\).

With the ordinary dot product these are the usual length and distance from school. Any sensible distance (a metric) keeps four promises: it is never negative; it is zero only from a point to itself; A to B equals B to A; and a stop along the way never makes a trip shorter.

Distance and agreement are linked

Expand the distance and the agreement score pops out:

\[\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^2=\|\mathbf{x}\|^2+\|\mathbf{y}\|^2-2\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\]

Keep the two lengths fixed. Then more agreement means less distance. Every point the score goes up, the squared distance goes down by two. So "find the nearest" and "find the most similar" are really the same search. Just don't mix the two up in the middle of a formula: one goes up when the other goes down.

One warning: it doesn't work the other way round. Every inner product gives a length, but not every length comes from an inner product. The taxi's length does not. The picture shows the tell-tale sign.

The tell-tale sign. Take \(\mathbf{x}=(1,0)\) and \(\mathbf{y}=(0,1)\) and look at the two diagonals of the square they make. A length that comes from an inner product always passes the parallelogram rule: \(\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|^2+\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^2=2(\|\mathbf{x}\|^2+\|\mathbf{y}\|^2)\). The crow passes: \(2+2=4\) ✓. The taxi fails: each diagonal costs 2 blocks, so \(4+4=8\neq4\) ✗. No inner product can make the diamond.

Imagine this

At noon the sun is straight overhead. You hold a stick at a slant. Its shadow on the ground is never longer than the stick. It matches the stick only when you lay the stick flat.

That everyday fact is the most useful inequality in this unit. It is called Cauchy–Schwarz:

\[|\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle|\;\le\;\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\|\mathbf{y}\|\]

In words: the agreement score can never be bigger than the two lengths multiplied. Why is this the shadow fact? The score is \(\|\mathbf{x}\|\) times the shadow of \(\mathbf{y}\) on \(\mathbf{x}\)'s line. The shadow is never longer than \(\mathbf{y}\) itself. So the score is never more than \(\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\|\mathbf{y}\|\). The two sides are equal only when the stick lies flat, that is, when \(\mathbf{y}\) points exactly along \(\mathbf{x}\) (or exactly opposite).

Prove it · distance and inner product are wired together — derived

Claim. \(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^2=\|\mathbf{x}\|^2+\|\mathbf{y}\|^2-2\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\) in every inner-product space.

1
Start from the definition of the induced norm, applied to the difference: \[\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^2=\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},\,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rangle\] Squared length is the inner product of a vector with itself — no step yet, just the definition.
2
Open the left slot with bilinearity, then the right slot: \[=\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}\rangle+\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\rangle\] Linearity in each argument lets the minus signs and the sums pass through, exactly like multiplying out \((p-q)(p-q)\).
3
Use symmetry to merge the two middle terms, and the definition of the norm on the outer two: \(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^2=\|\mathbf{x}\|^2+\|\mathbf{y}\|^2-2\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle.\) ∎ \(\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\) by axiom ②. So what: with the lengths fixed, distance goes down exactly as the inner product goes up — the two gauges are one equation.
Prove it · Cauchy–Schwarz — derived from one clever vector

Claim. For any \(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\) in an inner-product space, \(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle^2\le\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}\rangle\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle\), i.e. \(|\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle|\le\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\).

1
If \(\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\) both sides are \(0\) and there is nothing to prove. So assume \(\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}\), which gives \(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle>0\). Positive definiteness (axiom ③) is what lets us divide by \(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle\) later.
2
For any number \(\alpha\), the vector \(\mathbf{u}-\alpha\mathbf{v}\) has a squared length, and squared lengths are never negative: \[0\le\langle\mathbf{u}-\alpha\mathbf{v},\,\mathbf{u}-\alpha\mathbf{v}\rangle\] Axiom ③ again — this single inequality is the entire input; the rest is algebra.
3
Multiply out with bilinearity and symmetry (the same expansion as the identity above): \[0\le\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}\rangle-2\alpha\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle+\alpha^2\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle\] A quadratic in \(\alpha\) that is never negative — for every choice of \(\alpha\), including the smartest one.
4
Choose \(\alpha=\dfrac{\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle}\) (the shadow coefficient of Section 11) and substitute: \[0\le\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}\rangle-2\frac{\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle^2}{\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle}+\frac{\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle^2}{\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle}=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}\rangle-\frac{\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle^2}{\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle}\] Check the middle term: \(2\alpha\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle=2\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle^2/\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle\); and \(\alpha^2\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle^2/\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle\). Two of the same thing minus one leaves minus one.
5
Multiply through by \(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle>0\) and rearrange: \(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle^2\le\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}\rangle\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle\); take square roots: \(|\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle|\le\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\). ∎ Equality holds exactly when step 2 was an equality, i.e. \(\mathbf{u}-\alpha\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\): u is a multiple of v. Nothing used the dot product specifically — the proof runs verbatim for every inner product, which is why Problem 8 can "verify" it under a weighted ruler.
Prove it · the triangle inequality follows from Cauchy–Schwarz — derived

Claim. \(\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|\le\|\mathbf{x}\|+\|\mathbf{y}\|\) for the norm induced by any inner product — so every inner product really does produce a norm (and a metric).

1
Expand the squared length of the sum (same identity as before, with a plus sign): \[\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|^2=\|\mathbf{x}\|^2+2\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle+\|\mathbf{y}\|^2\] Bilinearity opens both slots; symmetry merges the two cross terms into \(2\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\).
2
Bound the cross term with Cauchy–Schwarz, \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\le|\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle|\le\|\mathbf{x}\|\|\mathbf{y}\|\): \[\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|^2\le\|\mathbf{x}\|^2+2\|\mathbf{x}\|\|\mathbf{y}\|+\|\mathbf{y}\|^2=(\|\mathbf{x}\|+\|\mathbf{y}\|)^2\] Replacing a term by something at least as large can only make the right side bigger; the result is a perfect square.
3
Both sides are non-negative, so taking square roots keeps the inequality: \(\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|\le\|\mathbf{x}\|+\|\mathbf{y}\|\). ∎ For distances, apply this to \(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})+(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y})\) and promise ④ of a metric drops out.

Angles — the similarity dial

Imagine this

Two people type into a search box. One types "cheap flights to Goa". The other writes a long paragraph about cheap flights to Goa. Their word counts are very different. Their topic is the same.

Divide the agreement score by the two lengths. Cauchy–Schwarz promises the answer lies between \(-1\) and \(+1\), so it is the cosine of exactly one angle \(\omega\) between 0° and 180°:

Orthonormal matrices — the motions that don't lie

Imagine this

Turn a photo on your phone. The faces in it keep their shapes. Nothing gets longer, nothing gets squashed. Now stretch the photo with two fingers. The faces get wider and the angles in them change.

Turning is an honest motion: every length and angle survives. Stretching or slanting is a lying motion: it moves things and changes their measurements.

Why machine learning cares: turn an embedding space and every similarity, every distance and every nearest neighbour stays exactly the same. So the honest motions are safe to use anywhere.

Which matrices are honest? The ones whose columns are at right angles to each other and each of length 1. They are called orthogonal (or orthonormal) matrices, and they satisfy one neat rule:

\[A^TA=I\qquad\text{so}\qquad A^{-1}=A^T\]

In words: to undo the motion, just flip the matrix (transpose it). No hard work needed. A turn by an angle \(\theta\), \(\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\), is one example. So is a mirror flip.

Why does \(A^TA=I\) keep lengths? Because the new length squared is \(\|A\mathbf{x}\|^2=\mathbf{x}^TA^TA\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^TI\mathbf{x}=\|\mathbf{x}\|^2\). The \(A^TA\) in the middle just disappears. The same happens inside the angle formula, so angles survive too.

A slant (a shear) \(\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\) pushes every point sideways by \(k\) times its height. Flat arrows stay put and everything else leans over. Watch both motions:

2

3

4

A shear has \(\det=1\) yet \(A^TA\neq I\) — and the widget shows it bending both gauges.

The shadow machine — projection

Imagine this

A streetlamp shines straight down on a road. You hold a stick \(\mathbf{a}\) at a slant. The road runs in the direction \(\mathbf{b}\).

The stick's shadow on the road is the part of the stick that goes along the road. The rest of the stick points straight up, at a right angle to the road. So the stick = shadow + leftover.

That split is called projection. How long is the shadow? The shadow is some multiple of the road direction: \(\alpha\mathbf{b}\). We want the leftover \(\mathbf{a}-\alpha\mathbf{b}\) to stand at a right angle to the road. That one wish fixes \(\alpha\):

\[\alpha=\frac{\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}\rangle},\qquad \text{shadow}=\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{a})=\alpha\,\mathbf{b}\]

Quick example: \(\mathbf{a}=(2,3)\) and the road \(\mathbf{b}=(1,0)\). Then \(\alpha=\frac{2}{1}=2\), the shadow is \((2,0)\) and the leftover is \((0,3)\), pointing straight up. If \(\mathbf{b}\) has length 1, it is even simpler: \(\alpha=\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle\).

Gram–Schmidt — cleaning a messy basis

Imagine this

In a city with square blocks, directions are easy: "3 blocks east, 2 blocks north." Now imagine a city whose streets meet at odd angles. Every direction turns into a puzzle.

Square streets make life easy. Gram–Schmidt is a machine that turns any crooked set of streets into square ones.

Unit 2 taught you to find a basis: a set of directions that can reach every point. The best kind is an orthonormal basis: every direction at a right angle to every other, and each of length 1.

Why is it the best? Because finding coordinates becomes easy. With a crooked basis, you must solve equations to find how much of each direction you need. With an orthonormal basis, you just take shadows: the amount of \(\mathbf{e}_i\) in \(\mathbf{v}\) is \(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{e}_i\rangle\). No equations.

Coordinates by shadow. With the orthonormal pair \(\mathbf{e}_1=(\cos30^\circ,\sin30^\circ)\), \(\mathbf{e}_2=(-\sin30^\circ,\cos30^\circ)\), the vector \(\mathbf{v}\approx(1.13,2.04)\) has coordinates \(\lambda_1=\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{e}_1\rangle=2\) and \(\lambda_2=\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{e}_2\rangle=1.2\): each coordinate is just the shadow of \(\mathbf{v}\) on that axis. In a non-orthonormal basis you would have to solve a \(2\times2\) system instead.

If you want the algebra · why coordinates are just shadows
Prove it · coordinates in an orthonormal basis are inner products — derived

Claim. If \(\{\mathbf{e}_1,\dots,\mathbf{e}_n\}\) is orthonormal and \(\mathbf{v}=\lambda_1\mathbf{e}_1+\cdots+\lambda_n\mathbf{e}_n\), then \(\lambda_i=\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{e}_i\rangle\).

1
Take the inner product of both sides of the expansion with one basis vector \(\mathbf{e}_i\): \[\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{e}_i\rangle=\Big\langle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_j\mathbf{e}_j,\ \mathbf{e}_i\Big\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_j\langle\mathbf{e}_j,\mathbf{e}_i\rangle\] Bilinearity pulls the sum and the scalars \(\lambda_j\) out of the first slot.
2
Orthonormality kills every term but one: \(\langle\mathbf{e}_j,\mathbf{e}_i\rangle=0\) for \(j\neq i\) and \(=1\) for \(j=i\). So \(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{e}_i\rangle=\lambda_i\cdot1=\lambda_i\). ∎ The "1" is where unit length earns its keep; with a merely orthogonal basis you would get \(\lambda_i\|\mathbf{e}_i\|^2\) and have to divide. The canonical basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is one orthonormal basis; rotate it and you get infinitely many others.

The cleaning machine is the shadow machine used over and over. Take your crooked directions one at a time:

  • 1Take the first direction. Shrink or stretch it to length 1. Done: that is \(\mathbf{e}_1\).
  • 2Take the next direction. Cut off its shadow on every clean direction you already have. What is left stands at a right angle to all of them.
  • 3Stretch that leftover to length 1. That is your next clean direction. Repeat step 2 for the rest.

For two directions it is just: \(\mathbf{e}_1=\dfrac{\mathbf{x}_1}{\|\mathbf{x}_1\|}\), then \(\mathbf{u}_2=\mathbf{x}_2-\langle\mathbf{x}_2,\mathbf{e}_1\rangle\,\mathbf{e}_1\), then \(\mathbf{e}_2=\dfrac{\mathbf{u}_2}{\|\mathbf{u}_2\|}\). Watch it happen in 3D:

Bonus · the slides' shortcut: Gram–Schmidt by elimination (and why it works)

The course adds a beautiful industrial route. Stack the messy basis as columns of \(A\), then run plain Gaussian elimination on the augmented matrix \([A^TA \mid A^T]\) — when the left half reaches echelon form, the rows on the right are an orthogonal basis. The slides' example: \(\mathbf{v}_1=(3,1)\), \(\mathbf{v}_2=(2,2)\) give \(A^TA=\begin{pmatrix}10&8\\8&8\end{pmatrix}\), and elimination lands on

\[\left(\begin{array}{cc|cc}1&0.8&0.3&0.1\\0&1&-0.25&0.75\end{array}\right)\]

Check the right-hand rows: \((0.3,0.1)\cdot(-0.25,0.75)=-0.075+0.075=0\) — orthogonal (the first is \(\mathbf{v}_1\) rescaled; the second is what's left of \(\mathbf{v}_2\) after removing its \(\mathbf{v}_1\)-shadow). Normalize and you have an orthonormal basis. Why it works, in three moves: ① full column rank makes \(A^TA\) positive-definite (\(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\iff A^TA\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\), so the blind spots match and are trivial) — elimination needs no row swaps; ② elimination is multiplication by elementary matrices, i.e. \(A^TA=LU\) with \(L\) lower-triangular (Unit 1's recorded elimination!), and the right half becomes \(Q^T=L^{-1}A^T\); ③ then \(Q^TQ=L^{-1}(A^TA)L^{-T}=UL^{-T}\) is upper-triangular and symmetric — hence diagonal — which is precisely the statement that \(Q\)'s columns are orthogonal. Elimination doesn't just solve systems; run on \(A^TA\), it quietly performs Gram–Schmidt.

Same geometry, industrial route. \(q_1=0.1\,\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(q_2=0.625\times(-0.4,1.2)\) — the second row is precisely the leftover after removing \(\mathbf{v}_2\)'s shadow \(\tfrac{8}{10}\mathbf{v}_1=(2.4,0.8)\), rescaled. Elimination found the shadow coefficient \(0.8\) on its own: it is the multiplier it used to clear the first column.

The whole story, in five lines

  • ①Length is a choice. A norm is any length rule that scales, allows no shortcuts, and is zero only for "nowhere". The crow (round ball) and the taxi (diamond) both qualify.
  • ②The dot product is an agreement score. Its blueprint (fair with sums, same both ways, positive with itself) defines an inner product. Every SPD matrix \(A\) makes one: \(\mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{y}\). Test SPD with the top-left numbers and determinants.
  • ③Free gifts: length, distance, and (because a shadow never beats the stick) an angle: \(\cos\omega=\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle/\|\mathbf{x}\|\|\mathbf{y}\|\), ML's similarity dial.
  • ④Right angles depend on the ruler. Honest motions (\(A^TA=I\)) keep every measurement. Random arrows in high dimensions are almost always at right angles.
  • ⑤Shadows build square grids. Projection splits an arrow into along + across. Gram–Schmidt repeats it to turn any basis into an orthonormal one.
The one thing to remember

Geometry is a choice. Pick the matrix, and you have picked what "long", "near" and "at a right angle" mean. Much of machine learning is the craft of picking that ruler well. And whatever ruler you pick, Gram–Schmidt can always build a perfect square grid for it.

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 1 · Turn "orthogonal to both" into equations (a). Idea: "orthogonal to both" means two dot-product equations. Write \(\mathbf{w} = (w_1, w_2, w_3)^\top\): \[ \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{u} = w_1 + w_2 = 0, \qquad \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{v} = w_2 + w_3 = 0. \]
Step 2 · Solve the little system. From these, \(w_1 = -w_2\) and \(w_3 = -w_2\). Letting the free variable be \(w_2 = t\), \[ \mathbf{w} = t\,(-1,\,1,\,-1)^\top, \qquad t\in\mathbb{R}. \]
Step 3 · Normalise for the unit vectors. Divide by the length \(\|(-1,1,-1)^\top\| = \sqrt{(-1)^2 + 1^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{3}\): \[ \mathbf{w} = \pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\,(-1,\,1,\,-1)^\top. \] Both signs: the orthogonal line carries exactly two unit vectors.
Step 4 · The angle (b). Use \(\cos\omega = \dfrac{\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}\). Here \(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{v} = 0 + 1 + 0 = 1\) and \(\|\mathbf{u}\| = \|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{2}\), so \[ \cos\omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \omega = 60^\circ. \]
Step 5 · Basis test (c). Three vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) form a basis exactly when their determinant is nonzero. Watch out

Key takeaway

You just computed a cross product without naming it: \(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} = (1,-1,1)^\top\), which is exactly the \(t = -1\) member of your family \(t\,(-1,1,-1)^\top\). Solving "orthogonal to both" as a linear system is precisely what the cross product does for a living in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Problem 2Easy

(Similarity vs. dissimilarity of data objects.) Three data points live in \(\mathbb{R}^3\): \[ \mathbf{p}_1 = (2,1,0)^\top, \qquad \mathbf{p}_2 = (0,1,2)^\top, \qquad \mathbf{p}_3 = (2,1,1)^\top. \] (a) Build the \(3\times 3\) dissimilarity matrix \(D\) using Euclidean distance. [2 M]
(b) Which two points are the most similar? [1 M]
(c) Compute the cosine similarity between \(\mathbf{p}_1\) and \(\mathbf{p}_3\). [1 M] (4 marks)

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 1 · Pairwise distances (a). Dissimilarity between two points is just the distance \(d(\mathbf{p}_i,\mathbf{p}_j) = \|\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j\|\). Compute each pair: \[ d(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_2) = \|(2,0,-2)^\top\| = \sqrt{4+0+4} = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}, \qquad d(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_3) = \|(0,0,-1)^\top\| = 1, \] \[ d(\mathbf{p}_2,\mathbf{p}_3) = \|(-2,0,1)^\top\| = \sqrt{4+0+1} = \sqrt{5}. \]
Step 2 · Assemble the matrix. Place these in a symmetric matrix with zeros on the diagonal (a point's distance to itself is \(0\)): \[ D = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2\sqrt{2} & 1 \\ 2\sqrt{2} & 0 & \sqrt{5} \\ 1 & \sqrt{5} & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]
Step 3 · Most similar pair (b). "Most similar" \(=\) smallest dissimilarity. The smallest off-diagonal entry is \(d(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_3) = 1\), so \(\mathbf{p}_1\) and \(\mathbf{p}_3\) are the most similar.
Step 4 · Cosine similarity (c). Cosine similarity is \(\cos\theta = \dfrac{\mathbf{p}_1^\top\mathbf{p}_3}{\|\mathbf{p}_1\|\,\|\mathbf{p}_3\|}\): \[ \mathbf{p}_1^\top\mathbf{p}_3 = 2\cdot 2 + 1\cdot 1 + 0\cdot 1 = 5, \qquad \|\mathbf{p}_1\| = \sqrt{5}, \qquad \|\mathbf{p}_3\| = \sqrt{6}, \] \[ \cos\theta = \frac{5}{\sqrt{5}\,\sqrt{6}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{30}} = \frac{\sqrt{30}}{6} \approx 0.913. \] (Close to \(1\), confirming \(\mathbf{p}_1\) and \(\mathbf{p}_3\) point in nearly the same direction.)
Answer. (a) \(D\) as above, symmetric with zero diagonal. (b) \(\mathbf{p}_1\) and \(\mathbf{p}_3\), at distance \(1\). (c) \(\cos\theta = 5/\sqrt{30} = \sqrt{30}/6 \approx 0.913\).
Watch out

In a dissimilarity matrix, small means similar — scanning for the largest entry answers the opposite question. And the diagonal zeros are not data: every point is at distance \(0\) from itself, so only off-diagonal entries compete.

Key takeaway

Two different rulers for "similar": Euclidean distance measures closeness in position, cosine similarity measures alignment of direction. Here they happen to agree (\(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_3\) win on both); in general they need not.

Wait — really?

Cosine similarity is blind to length: \((2,1,0)^\top\) and \((4,2,0)^\top\) have cosine similarity exactly \(1\) even though they sit \(\sqrt{5}\) apart. That is why it is the default in text retrieval — a tweet and an essay about the same topic point the same way; they just differ in length.

Problem 3Medium

Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) and define a new inner product on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) by \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = \mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{y}\).
(a) Show \(A\) is symmetric positive definite, so \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_A\) is a genuine inner product. [1 M]
(b) The vectors \(\mathbf{x} = (1,0)^\top\) and \(\mathbf{y} = (0,1)^\top\) are orthogonal in the dot product. Are they still orthogonal under \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_A\)? Find the angle between them in this new inner product. [2 M]
(c) Find a vector \(\mathbf{y}' = (a,1)^\top\) that is orthogonal to \(\mathbf{x}\) with respect to \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_A\). [1 M] (4 marks)

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 1 · Certify the inner product (a). \(A\) is symmetric since \(A^\top = A\). Sylvester's criterion (all leading minors positive): \[ D_1 = 2 > 0, \qquad D_2 = \det A = 2\cdot 2 - 1\cdot 1 = 3 > 0. \] So \(A\) is positive definite. A symmetric, positive-definite matrix always makes \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{y}\) a valid inner product: it is bilinear, symmetric, and \(\mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{x} > 0\) for \(\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}\).
Step 2 · Test orthogonality (b). Compute \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = \mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{y}\). First \(A\mathbf{y} = A(0,1)^\top = (1,2)^\top\), hence \[ \langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = \mathbf{x}^\top(A\mathbf{y}) = (1,0)\cdot(1,2) = 1 \neq 0. \] They are not orthogonal here.
Step 3 · The \(A\)-norms. To get the angle we also need \[ \|\mathbf{x}\|_A = \sqrt{\mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{x}} = \sqrt{A_{11}} = \sqrt{2}, \qquad \|\mathbf{y}\|_A = \sqrt{A_{22}} = \sqrt{2}. \]
Step 4 · The \(A\)-angle. \[ \cos\theta = \frac{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_A\,\|\mathbf{y}\|_A} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \theta = 60^\circ. \] Takeaway (from the source): orthogonality depends on which inner product you use.
Step 5 · Force orthogonality (c). We need \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}'\rangle_A = 0\). Note \(\mathbf{x}^\top A = (1,0)\,A = (2,1)\), so \[ \langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}'\rangle_A = (2,1)\cdot(a,1) = 2a + 1 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; a = -\tfrac{1}{2}. \] Thus \(\mathbf{y}' = \left(-\tfrac{1}{2},\,1\right)^\top\).
Answer. (a) SPD by Sylvester: \(D_1 = 2 > 0\), \(D_2 = 3 > 0\). (b) No — \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = 1 \neq 0\); the angle in the \(A\)-geometry is \(\theta = 60^\circ\). (c) \(a = -\tfrac12\), i.e. \(\mathbf{y}' = (-\tfrac12,\,1)^\top\).
Watch out

When you switch inner products, switch everything: the cosine formula needs the \(A\)-inner product on top and the \(A\)-norms below. Mixing \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = 1\) with the Euclidean norms \(\|\mathbf{x}\| = \|\mathbf{y}\| = 1\) would give \(\cos\theta = 1\), i.e. a \(0^\circ\) angle — badly wrong.

Key takeaway

Orthogonality is relative to the inner product. The same pair of vectors can be perpendicular in one geometry and at \(60^\circ\) in another — and part (c) shows you can always re-engineer a vector to restore orthogonality in the new geometry.

Wait — really?

The coordinate axes themselves — \(\mathbf{e}_1\) and \(\mathbf{e}_2\), the gridlines of the plane — meet at \(60^\circ\), not \(90^\circ\), the moment \(A\) defines the geometry. Perpendicularity is not a property of two arrows; it is a property of the inner product that measures them.

Problem 4Medium

On \(\mathbb{R}^3\) use the weighted inner product \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = \mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{y}\) with \[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad(\text{SPD}). \] Let \(\mathbf{a} = (1,-1,0)^\top\) and \(\mathbf{b} = (1,0,2)^\top\).
(a) Compute \(\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\rangle_A\), \(\|\mathbf{a}\|_A\), and \(\|\mathbf{b}\|_A\). [2 M]
(b) Find the orthogonal projection \(\mathbf{p}\) of \(\mathbf{b}\) onto the line spanned by \(\mathbf{a}\), in this inner product. [2 M]
(c) Verify that the residual \(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p}\) is \(A\)-orthogonal to \(\mathbf{a}\) (this is what makes \(\mathbf{p}\) the orthogonal projection). [1 M] (5 marks)

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 1 · One matrix–vector product does most of the work (a). Trick: compute the matrix–vector product once, then take dot products. With \[ A\mathbf{a} = A(1,-1,0)^\top = (2-1,\ 1-2,\ 0)^\top = (1,-1,0)^\top: \] \[ \langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\rangle_A = \mathbf{b}^\top(A\mathbf{a}) = (1,0,2)\cdot(1,-1,0) = 1, \qquad \|\mathbf{a}\|_A^2 = \mathbf{a}^\top(A\mathbf{a}) = (1,-1,0)\cdot(1,-1,0) = 2. \]
Step 2 · The norm of \(\mathbf{b}\). For \(\|\mathbf{b}\|_A\), use \(A\mathbf{b} = A(1,0,2)^\top = (2,1,2)^\top\): \[ \|\mathbf{b}\|_A^2 = \mathbf{b}^\top(A\mathbf{b}) = (1,0,2)\cdot(2,1,2) = 2 + 0 + 4 = 6. \] So \(\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\rangle_A = 1\), \(\|\mathbf{a}\|_A = \sqrt{2}\), \(\|\mathbf{b}\|_A = \sqrt{6}\).
Step 3 · Project (b). Projection formula (same as usual, but every inner product is the \(A\)-one): \[ \mathbf{p} = \frac{\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\rangle_A}{\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}\rangle_A}\,\mathbf{a} = \frac{1}{2}\,(1,-1,0)^\top = \left(\tfrac{1}{2},\,-\tfrac{1}{2},\,0\right)^\top. \]
Step 4 · Check the residual (c). The residual is \[ \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p} = (1,0,2)^\top - \left(\tfrac{1}{2},\,-\tfrac{1}{2},\,0\right)^\top = \left(\tfrac{1}{2},\ \tfrac{1}{2},\ 2\right)^\top. \] Check \(A\)-orthogonality using the same \(A\mathbf{a} = (1,-1,0)^\top\): \[ \langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle_A = \mathbf{r}^\top(A\mathbf{a}) = \left(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2},2\right)\cdot(1,-1,0) = \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2} + 0 = 0. \;\checkmark \] Since \(\mathbf{r} \perp_A \mathbf{a}\), \(\mathbf{p}\) is indeed the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf{b}\) onto the line through \(\mathbf{a}\).
Answer. (a) \(\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\rangle_A = 1\), \(\|\mathbf{a}\|_A = \sqrt{2}\), \(\|\mathbf{b}\|_A = \sqrt{6}\). (b) \(\mathbf{p} = \left(\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, 0\right)^\top\). (c) \(\langle\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{p},\,\mathbf{a}\rangle_A = 0\) ✓ — the residual is \(A\)-orthogonal to \(\mathbf{a}\).
Watch out

Verify with the same inner product you projected in: the defining property is \(\langle\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{p},\,\mathbf{a}\rangle_A = 0\). In this particular problem a plain dot-product check happens to give \(0\) too — pure luck, because \(A\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{a}\) here (see below). In a general weighted geometry the residual is \(A\)-orthogonal, not dot-orthogonal, to \(\mathbf{a}\).

Key takeaway

The projection recipe never changes: \(\mathbf{p} = \dfrac{\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}\rangle}\,\mathbf{a}\) — swap in whichever inner product defines the geometry. The residual-orthogonality check is the certificate that \(\mathbf{p}\) really is the closest point on the line.

Wait — really?

\(A\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{a}\): the vector \(\mathbf{a} = (1,-1,0)^\top\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(1\). That is why every \(A\)-computation involving \(\mathbf{a}\) collapsed to a plain dot product — and why the Euclidean projection of \(\mathbf{b}\) onto \(\mathbf{a}\) would land on the same \(\mathbf{p}\) here. Move \(\mathbf{a}\) off an eigendirection and the two projections part ways.

Problem 5Medium

Consider the symmetric matrix \(A(t) = \begin{bmatrix} t & 3 \\ 3 & t \end{bmatrix}\), \(t\in\mathbb{R}\).
(a) Classify \(A(t)\) for all real \(t\) as positive definite, positive semidefinite, indefinite, negative semidefinite, or negative definite. [3 M]
(b) For which \(t\) does \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle = \mathbf{x}^\top A(t)\,\mathbf{y}\) define a valid inner product? At the borderline value \(t = 3\), exhibit a nonzero vector \(\mathbf{v}\) with \(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle = 0\). [2 M] (5 marks)

Show the worked solution — every step
Looking ahead

This solution uses eigenvalues, which Unit 4 introduces properly: for a symmetric matrix, positive-definite means all eigenvalues are positive. If you have not met them yet, you can classify \(A(t)\) with this unit's tools instead — Sylvester's minors \(D_1=t\) and \(D_2=t^2-9\) give "positive definite \(\iff t>3\)" — and read the eigenvalue route as a preview.

Step 1 · Find the eigenvalues (a). Method: a symmetric matrix is classified by the signs of its eigenvalues. Solve \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\): \[ (t-\lambda)^2 - 9 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; t - \lambda = \pm 3 \;\Longrightarrow\; \lambda = t - 3 \ \text{ or } \ \lambda = t + 3. \]
Step 2 · Read off the signs. Now just read off the signs of the two eigenvalues \(t-3\) and \(t+3\): \[ \begin{aligned} t > 3 &: \ \text{both positive} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{positive definite}\\ t = 3 &: \ \lambda = 6,\,0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{positive semidefinite}\\ -3 < t < 3 &: \ \text{one } +,\ \text{one } - \;\Rightarrow\; \text{indefinite}\\ t = -3 &: \ \lambda = 0,\,-6 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{negative semidefinite}\\ t < -3 &: \ \text{both negative} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{negative definite} \end{aligned} \]
Step 3 · Inner-product range and the degenerate vector (b). An inner product needs a positive-definite matrix, so we need \(t > 3\). At \(t = 3\) the matrix is only semidefinite, which fails the rule \(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle > 0\) for \(\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}\). Concretely, take \(\mathbf{v} = (1,-1)^\top\). With \(A(3) = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\), \[ \langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle = \mathbf{v}^\top A(3)\,\mathbf{v} = (1,-1)\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = (1,-1)\cdot(0,0) = 0, \] even though \(\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}\). So \(t = 3\) does not give an inner product.
Answer. (a) Positive definite for \(t > 3\); positive semidefinite at \(t = 3\) (\(\lambda = 6, 0\)); indefinite for \(-3 < t < 3\); negative semidefinite at \(t = -3\) (\(\lambda = 0, -6\)); negative definite for \(t < -3\). (b) Valid inner product \(\iff t > 3\); at \(t = 3\), \(\mathbf{v} = (1,-1)^\top \neq \mathbf{0}\) has \(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle = 0\).
Watch out

Semidefinite is not "almost good enough": at \(t = 3\) the axiom that dies is exactly the strict inequality \(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle > 0\) for \(\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}\). A geometry in which a nonzero vector has length zero cannot measure distances — so the borderline value is excluded, not included.

Key takeaway

For a symmetric matrix, definiteness = the sign pattern of its eigenvalues, full stop. For the pattern \(\begin{bmatrix} t & c \\ c & t \end{bmatrix}\) the eigenvalues are always \(t + c\) and \(t - c\) — diagonal plus-or-minus off-diagonal — which makes the whole classification a one-line read-off.

Wait — really?

One dial sweeps all five classes — and the eigenvectors never move. For every \(t\), \(A(t)\,(1,1)^\top = (t+3)(1,1)^\top\) and \(A(t)\,(1,-1)^\top = (t-3)(1,-1)^\top\). Turning \(t\) just slides the two eigenvalues \(t \pm 3\) along the number line past zero, while the axes of the quadratic form stay frozen at \(45^\circ\).

Problem 6Hard

(Positive definiteness over \(\mathbb{C}\).) For a real parameter \(k\), consider \[ A(k) = \begin{bmatrix} k & 1+i \\ 1-i & 2 \end{bmatrix}. \] For \(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^2\), let \(\mathbf{u}^*\) denote the conjugate transpose and set \(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle = \mathbf{u}^* A(k)\,\mathbf{v}\).
(a) Show \(A(k)\) is Hermitian for every real \(k\). [1 M]
(b) Find all real \(k\) for which \(A(k)\) is positive definite — equivalently, for which \(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle = \mathbf{u}^* A(k)\,\mathbf{v}\) is a valid inner product on \(\mathbb{C}^2\). [2 M]
(c) At the borderline value \(k = 1\) the matrix is only positive semidefinite. Find a nonzero vector \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^2\) with \(\mathbf{z}^* A(1)\,\mathbf{z} = 0\), showing the form degenerates there. [2 M] (5 marks)

Show the worked solution — every step
Step 1 · Hermitian check (a). A matrix is Hermitian when \(A^* = A\), i.e. the diagonal entries are real and the two off-diagonal entries are complex conjugates of each other. Here the diagonal is \(k,\,2\) (real, because \(k\) is real) and the off-diagonals satisfy \(\overline{1+i} = 1-i\). Hence \(A(k)^* = A(k)\) for every real \(k\).
Step 2 · Sylvester over \(\mathbb{C}\) (b). Sylvester's criterion also works for Hermitian matrices: positive definite \(\iff\) all leading principal minors are positive (they come out real automatically). The minors are \[ D_1 = k, \qquad D_2 = \det A(k) = 2k - (1+i)(1-i). \]
Step 3 · The crucial complex product. It is a squared modulus: \[ (1+i)(1-i) = 1 - i^2 = 2 = |1+i|^2, \qquad\text{so}\qquad D_2 = 2k - 2 = 2(k-1). \]
Step 4 · Solve the inequalities. Requiring \(D_1 > 0\) and \(D_2 > 0\) gives \(k > 0\) and \(k > 1\), so \[ A(k) \ \text{is positive definite} \iff k > 1. \]
Step 5 · The degenerate direction at \(k = 1\) (c). At \(k = 1\) we have \(D_2 = 0\), so \(A(1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1+i \\ 1-i & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) is singular (\(D_1 = 1 > 0\), \(D_2 = 0\): positive semidefinite). Idea: any nonzero null-space vector works, since \(A(1)\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0} \Rightarrow \mathbf{z}^* A(1)\,\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{z}^*\mathbf{0} = 0\). From the first row, \(z_1 + (1+i)z_2 = 0\); choosing \(z_2 = 1\) gives \(z_1 = -(1+i)\), so \[ \mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix} -(1+i) \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]
Step 6 · Verify it is a null vector. Using \((1-i)(1+i) = 2\): \[ A(1)\,\mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\cdot\big({-(1+i)}\big) + (1+i)\cdot 1 \\ (1-i)\cdot\big({-(1+i)}\big) + 2\cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2+2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \] Hence \(\mathbf{z}^* A(1)\,\mathbf{z} = 0\) with \(\mathbf{z} \neq \mathbf{0}\): at \(k = 1\) the form is not positive definite, so it is not an inner product.
Answer. (a) \(A(k)^* = A(k)\) for all real \(k\). (b) \(A(k)\) is positive definite \(\iff k > 1\). (c) \(\mathbf{z} = (-(1+i),\,1)^\top \neq \mathbf{0}\) satisfies \(A(1)\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}\), hence \(\mathbf{z}^* A(1)\,\mathbf{z} = 0\).
Watch out

Over \(\mathbb{C}\), transpose is not enough — use the conjugate transpose \(\mathbf{u}^*\). And the off-diagonal product is \((1+i)(1-i) = |1+i|^2 = 2\), not \((1+i)^2 = 2i\). Writing \(D_2 = 2k - 2i\) (a complex determinant for a Hermitian matrix — impossible) is the classic slip.

Key takeaway

Hermitian + positive definite is the complex analogue of symmetric + positive definite: Sylvester's criterion still runs, the leading minors are automatically real, and only \(k > 1\) — both minors strictly positive — buys a genuine inner product on \(\mathbb{C}^2\).

Wait — really?

At \(k = 1\) there exists a nonzero vector of length zero: \(\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}\rangle = 0\) although \(\mathbf{z} \neq \mathbf{0}\). The geometry has a direction it simply cannot see — which is precisely the disease the positive-definiteness axiom exists to rule out.

Matrix (c) satisfies \(\mathbf{x}^\top M\,\mathbf{x} = x_1^2 + x_1x_2 + x_2^2 = \left(x_1 + \tfrac{x_2}{2}\right)^2 + \tfrac{3}{4}x_2^2 > 0\) for every \(\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}\) — genuinely positive "lengths" — and it is still not an inner product. Problem 8Medium

On \(\mathbb{R}^2\) use \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = \mathbf{x}^\top A\,\mathbf{y}\) with \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) (SPD), and let \(\mathbf{x} = (1,1)^\top\), \(\mathbf{y} = (1,-1)^\top\).

Step 1 · Two matrix–vector products (a). Compute \(A\mathbf{x}\) and \(A\mathbf{y}\) once: \[ A\mathbf{x} = A(1,1)^\top = (3+1,\ 1+2)^\top = (4,3)^\top, \qquad A\mathbf{y} = A(1,-1)^\top = (3-1,\ 1-2)^\top = (2,-1)^\top. \]
Step 2 · Norms and inner product. \[ \|\mathbf{x}\|_A^2 = \mathbf{x}^\top(A\mathbf{x}) = (1,1)\cdot(4,3) = 7, \qquad \|\mathbf{y}\|_A^2 = \mathbf{y}^\top(A\mathbf{y}) = (1,-1)\cdot(2,-1) = 2 + 1 = 3, \] \[ \langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = \mathbf{x}^\top(A\mathbf{y}) = (1,1)\cdot(2,-1) = 1. \] So \(\|\mathbf{x}\|_A = \sqrt{7}\), \(\|\mathbf{y}\|_A = \sqrt{3}\), \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = 1\).
Step 3 · Cauchy–Schwarz (b). \[ |\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A| = 1 \ \le\ \|\mathbf{x}\|_A\,\|\mathbf{y}\|_A = \sqrt{7}\cdot\sqrt{3} = \sqrt{21} \approx 4.58. \;\checkmark \]
Step 4 · Distance and triangle inequality (c). Distance uses the same norm on the difference. With \(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y} = (0,2)^\top\) and \(A(0,2)^\top = (2,4)^\top\): \[ \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_A^2 = (0,2)\cdot(2,4) = 8 \;\Longrightarrow\; d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83. \] Triangle inequality: \[ \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_A = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83 \ \le\ \|\mathbf{x}\|_A + \|\mathbf{y}\|_A = \sqrt{7} + \sqrt{3} \approx 4.38. \;\checkmark \]
Answer. (a) \(\|\mathbf{x}\|_A = \sqrt{7}\), \(\|\mathbf{y}\|_A = \sqrt{3}\), \(\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle_A = 1\). (b) \(1 \le \sqrt{21} \approx 4.58\) ✓. (c) \(d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83 \le \sqrt{7}+\sqrt{3} \approx 4.38\) ✓.
Watch out

The distance must be measured with \(A\) on the difference: \((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^\top A\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}) = 8\), so \(d = 2\sqrt{2}\) — not the Euclidean \(\|(0,2)^\top\| = 2\). Every length in this problem, the distance included, is an \(A\)-length.

Key takeaway

Cauchy–Schwarz and the triangle inequality are theorems of every inner-product space: if \(A\) is genuinely SPD they cannot fail. So "verifying" them is really a sanity check on your arithmetic — if such a check ever fails, either the computation or the claimed positive-definiteness is wrong.

Wait — really?

In the plain dot product these two vectors are orthogonal — \(\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{y} = 1 - 1 = 0\) — yet under \(A\) their inner product is \(1\), giving \(\cos\theta_A = 1/\sqrt{21} \approx 0.218\): an angle of about \(77.4^\circ\). The matrix \(A\) literally bends the right angle.
