The million-knob problem

Imagine this

You are playing darts. Your first throw misses. Your brain notes how far off you were and in which direction — that is the error — and quietly adjusts your stance, grip, and force for the next throw. You learn from the miss. A neural network learns the same way: it makes a prediction (the throw), a loss function measures the miss (the error), and then every parameter gets adjusted to shrink that miss. The question this unit answers: with a million knobs, how does each one find out its own share of the blame — and how can that possibly be cheap?

From Unit 6 you already know what each knob needs: the partial derivative of the loss with respect to that knob. Gradient descent does the rest. So the whole problem is computing a million derivatives, fast.

Try the honest way first, on one small function from the lecture:

\[f(x)=\sqrt{x^2+e^{x^2}}+\cos\!\big(x^2+e^{x^2}\big)\]

Differentiating by hand, the chain rule sprays copies of \(2x+2xe^{x^2}\) everywhere:

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{df}{dx}&=\frac{2x+2xe^{x^2}}{2\sqrt{x^2+e^{x^2}}}\\[.3em]&\quad-\sin\!\big(x^2+e^{x^2}\big)\big(2x+2xe^{x^2}\big)\end{aligned}\]

One input, and the expression is already a mouthful — long to write, slow to evaluate, easy to get wrong. Now scale the ambition: a modern network has billions of parameters, and we need the derivative with respect to every one of them, at every training step. Writing formulas by hand is not just impractical — it is impossible.

Backpropagation is the way out, and its two ideas fit in one breath: break the function into baby steps, and send the error backwards through them. Everything else in this unit is those two ideas, carefully.

The whole algorithm is two rules

In plain words

Rule 1 — along a path, multiply. Each edge of the graph has a local derivative (how much the child moves per unit of parent). Following a path from \(x\) to \(f\), the sensitivities multiply — Unit 6's amplifiers in series. Rule 2 — where paths merge, add. If \(x\) can reach \(f\) by several routes, each route contributes its product, and the contributions add. That is all backpropagation is: multiply along paths, add across paths — organised so no path is ever walked twice.

See both rules in the smallest graph that has two paths — the practice set's own example: \(f=uv\) with \(u=x^2+y\) and \(v=x-y\). The input \(x\) reaches \(f\) through \(u\) and through \(v\); click each path and watch its product, then their sum:

Why you can trust the rules — the chain rule, proved

"Multiply along paths, add across paths" has been doing a lot of work, so it had better be true — a theorem, not a slogan. The proof needs exactly one idea, and it is the idea this course keeps returning to: a derivative is a promise about a straight line. Saying "\(g\) has slope \(g'(x_0)\) at \(x_0\)" means precisely this:

\[g(x_0+h)=g(x_0)+g'(x_0)\,h+r(h),\qquad\text{where }\ \frac{r(h)}{h}\to 0\ \text{ as }h\to 0.\]

In words: near \(x_0\) the function is a straight line plus a leftover \(r(h)\) so small that even after dividing by the tiny number \(h\), it still shrinks to nothing. That last clause is the entire content of differentiability. Hold on to it — both rules fall out of it in a few lines each.

Pause & predict

The promise demands \(r(h)/h\to0\), not merely \(r(h)\to0\). What would go wrong if we only asked for \(r(h)\to0\)?

Prove it · Rule 1 — along a path, multiply

Claim. If \(u=g(x)\) and \(y=f(u)\), then at \(x_0\) (writing \(u_0=g(x_0)\)): \(\ \dfrac{dy}{dx}=f'(u_0)\,g'(x_0)\) — the two slopes multiply.

1
Nudge the input by \(h\) and ask how much \(u\) moves. That is \(g\)'s own promise: \[\Delta u \;=\; g(x_0+h)-g(x_0)\;=\;g'(x_0)\,h+r_g(h)\] Line plus leftover, with \(r_g(h)/h\to0\). Nothing new — just the definition, applied to \(g\).
2
Feed that movement to \(f\). That is \(f\)'s promise, written at \(u_0\) with nudge \(\Delta u\): \[f(u_0+\Delta u)\;=\;f(u_0)+f'(u_0)\,\Delta u+r_f(\Delta u)\] Same definition, applied to \(f\). Note the nudge \(f\) receives is \(\Delta u\), not \(h\).
3
Substitute step 1 into step 2 and multiply out: \[f\big(g(x_0+h)\big)=f(u_0)+\underbrace{f'(u_0)\,g'(x_0)}_{\text{the clean slope}}\,h\;+\;\underbrace{f'(u_0)\,r_g(h)+r_f(\Delta u)}_{\text{call it }R(h)}\] One term proportional to \(h\), and everything else swept into a single bracket \(R(h)\).
4
Check that \(R(h)\) is a legal leftover — that \(R(h)/h\to0\). Piece by piece: \[\frac{f'(u_0)\,r_g(h)}{h}\to f'(u_0)\cdot 0=0,\qquad \frac{r_f(\Delta u)}{h}=\frac{r_f(\Delta u)}{\Delta u}\cdot\frac{\Delta u}{h}\to 0\cdot g'(x_0)=0\] The first piece is a constant times something dying. The second: \(\Delta u/h\to g'(x_0)\) by step 1, while \(r_f(\Delta u)/\Delta u\to0\) because \(\Delta u\to0\). (Fine point: if \(\Delta u\) happens to be exactly \(0\), then \(r_f(\Delta u)=r_f(0)=0\) and the term is already zero — no division needed.)
5
Read off the conclusion. We have shown \[f\big(g(x_0+h)\big)=f\big(g(x_0)\big)+\big[f'(u_0)\,g'(x_0)\big]\,h+R(h),\qquad \frac{R(h)}{h}\to0\] — one clean slope, one dying leftover. That is exactly the shape of a derivative's promise, so the number in the bracket is the derivative of the composite. ∎ Two promises composed make a third promise, and slopes multiply while the leftovers stay harmless. "Multiply along a path" is now a theorem.

Now the second rule. Suppose \(f\) reaches the output through several intermediates at once — \(f\) is a function of \(u_1,\dots,u_m\), and each \(u_k\) is a function of \(x\). This is precisely the fork situation in the graph: one input, many routes.

Prove it · Rule 2 — across paths, add

Claim. \(\ \dfrac{df}{dx}=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_k}\,\frac{du_k}{dx}\) — each route contributes its product, and the contributions add.

1
The many-input promise (Unit 6's total derivative): nudging all inputs at once, \[f(u_1+\Delta u_1,\dots,u_m+\Delta u_m)=f(\mathbf u)+\sum_{k}\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_k}\,\Delta u_k+r,\qquad \frac{r}{\|\Delta \mathbf u\|}\to0\] A multivariable derivative is a plane-promise: one slope per input, one shared leftover that dies faster than the total nudge. This is what "differentiable" means in several variables.
2
Each intermediate keeps its own one-variable promise: \[\Delta u_k=\frac{du_k}{dx}\,h+r_k(h),\qquad \frac{r_k(h)}{h}\to0\] Every route's first leg is an honest derivative — \(m\) copies of step 1 from Rule 1.
3
Substitute step 2 into step 1 and collect the terms proportional to \(h\): \[f(\cdots)=f(\mathbf u)+\Big[\sum_{k}\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_k}\frac{du_k}{dx}\Big]h+R(h),\qquad R(h)=\sum_k\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_k}\,r_k(h)+r\] The bracket is the claimed slope. Everything not proportional to \(h\) — one leftover per route, plus the shared plane-leftover \(r\) — is swept into \(R(h)\). Nothing has been dropped yet.
4
The route leftovers die: for each \(k\), \[\frac{1}{h}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_k}\,r_k(h)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_k}\cdot\frac{r_k(h)}{h}\to\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_k}\cdot 0=0\] A fixed number times something that dies — the same move as step 4 of Rule 1, once per route.
5
The shared leftover dies too. By step 2, \(\|\Delta\mathbf u\|/h\to\|\mathbf u'(x)\|\), where \(\mathbf u'=(du_1/dx,\dots,du_m/dx)\). So \[\frac{r}{h}=\frac{r}{\|\Delta\mathbf u\|}\cdot\frac{\|\Delta\mathbf u\|}{h}\to 0\cdot\|\mathbf u'(x)\|=0\] The constant is \(\|\mathbf u'(x)\|\), the size of the combined nudge per unit \(h\). Fine point: if \(\Delta\mathbf u\) is exactly \(\mathbf 0\) then \(r=r(\mathbf 0)=0\) and the term is already gone — no division needed. Hence \(R(h)/h\to0\).
6
Same shape, same conclusion: the composite keeps a promise whose slope is the bracket — the sum over routes of (slope out) × (slope in). ∎ "Add across paths" is not a new fact about graphs. It is what a multi-input derivative means, read one route at a time.
The bridge

The two rules are matrix multiplication in disguise. For vector maps \(\mathbf x\xrightarrow{F}\mathbf u\xrightarrow{G}\mathbf y\), the chain rule reads \(J_{G\circ F}=J_G\,J_F\) — Jacobians multiply. Now look at one entry of that product, using the row-times-column rule:

\[\big(J_G J_F\big)_{ij}=\sum_{k}\big(J_G\big)_{ik}\big(J_F\big)_{kj}\]

Each term is one path: input \(j\) nudges intermediate \(k\) (that is \((J_F)_{kj}\)), which nudges output \(i\) (that is \((J_G)_{ik}\)) — multiply along the path. The \(\sum_k\) adds one product per intermediate stop — add across paths. The row-times-column rule you have used since Unit 1 is literally the two rules of backpropagation, written at once. Unit 6 promised "the chain rule becomes matrix multiplication"; you have now watched the promise come true, entry by entry.

The recipe, written once and for all

The lecture states the two rules formally, and it is worth reading slowly because this half-line is automatic differentiation. Number every quantity in the graph: \(x_1,\dots,x_d\) are the inputs, \(x_{d+1},\dots,x_{D-1}\) the intermediate baby steps, \(x_D\) the output. Each is computed from its parents by an elementary function:

\[x_i = g_i\big(x_{\mathrm{Pa}(x_i)}\big)\]

Now walk backwards. The output blames itself fully, and every other node collects blame from the nodes it feeds — its children:

\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_D}=1,\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}=\sum_{j:\,x_i\in \mathrm{Pa}(x_j)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}\,\frac{\partial g_j}{\partial x_i}\]

Read it like a postal system: node \(j\) already knows its own blame \(\partial f/\partial x_j\). It mails each parent \(i\) a copy, scaled by the local derivative \(\partial g_j/\partial x_i\) of its own baby step. A parent with several children just adds up its mail. One sweep from right to left and every node in the graph — every input, every weight — holds its derivative.

Two things make this cheap. Each blame \(\partial f/\partial x_j\) is computed once and reused by all of \(j\)'s parents. And each local derivative \(\partial g_j/\partial x_i\) is trivial, because each baby step is trivial — the derivative of \(e^a\) or \(\sqrt c\) or \(a+b\), never of the whole monster. This is why the lecture can conclude: computing all the derivatives costs about the same as computing the function itself.

Prove it · the recipe is correct

Claim. Process the nodes from right to left; then the recipe's \(\bar x_i\) equals the true \(\partial f/\partial x_i\) for every node in the graph.

1
Base case. The rightmost node: \(\bar x_D=1=\partial f/\partial f\) — the output's sensitivity to itself. Nudge \(f\) by \(h\) and \(f\) changes by \(h\). Slope 1, no leftover at all.
2
The order does the bookkeeping. Working right to left means: by the time we reach node \(x_i\), every child \(x_j\) (every node that uses \(x_i\)) has already been processed and — by induction — already holds its correct blame \(\bar x_j=\partial f/\partial x_j\). Children sit to the right of their parents in the graph, so a right-to-left sweep always finds the mail ready before it must be sent.
3
Apply Rule 2 at node \(x_i\), with its children as the intermediates. Any nudge of \(x_i\) can reach \(f\) only through the children, so §4's theorem says \[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}=\sum_{j:\,x_i\in\mathrm{Pa}(x_j)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}\,\frac{\partial g_j}{\partial x_i}\] — and every factor on the right is one the recipe possesses: the child's (already correct) blame, times the local slope of the child's baby step. So the value the recipe writes into \(\bar x_i\) is the true derivative. Right-to-left induction covers the whole graph. ∎ The recipe is not a new algorithm to take on trust — it is Rule 2, applied once per node, in an order that guarantees the mail is always ready.

In one sentence: Set \(\bar f=1\), then let every node mail each parent its own blame times a local derivative — one right-to-left sweep prices every knob.

A whole layer at once — backprop in matrix form

A real layer is the same neuron, vectorised: \(m\) neurons share the input vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n\),

\[Z=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b},\qquad \mathbf{a}=\sigma(Z)\]

with \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) the weights, \(\mathbf b\in\mathbb{R}^m\) the biases, and the sigmoid \(\sigma(z)=\tfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\) applied entry by entry. The blame arriving from above is \(\boldsymbol\delta^{(a)}=\partial\mathrm{Loss}/\partial \mathbf a\), one entry per neuron.

Read the answer as people first, then as symbols. Passing blame back through the layer gives four lines. Through the sigmoid: each neuron's blame is scaled by its own slope — no mixing between neurons. A weight: gets the blame of its neuron times the activity of its input — big only when both are big. A bias: gets its neuron's blame straight. The input: gets blame collected back through the weights, and that becomes the mail for the layer below.

Now the symbols. Step one, through the sigmoid: \(a_i=\sigma(Z_i)\) touches only its own \(Z_i\), so the Jacobian \(\partial\mathbf a/\partial Z\) is diagonal — \(\operatorname{diag}\!\big(\mathbf a\odot(1-\mathbf a)\big)\), using \(\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\) — and the blame is scaled entrywise (\(\odot\) is the Hadamard, entrywise, product):

\[\boldsymbol\delta^{(Z)}=\boldsymbol\delta^{(a)}\odot \mathbf a\odot(1-\mathbf a)\]

Step two, through \(Z=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b\). Since \(Z_i=\sum_j A_{ij}x_j+b_i\), each weight sees \(\partial Z_i/\partial A_{ij}=x_j\), and the chain rule assembles into three matrix formulas:

\[\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial\mathrm{Loss}}{\partial A}=\boldsymbol\delta^{(Z)}\mathbf{x}^{\top},\qquad \frac{\partial\mathrm{Loss}}{\partial \mathbf b}=\boldsymbol\delta^{(Z)},\\[.4em] \frac{\partial\mathrm{Loss}}{\partial \mathbf x}=A^{\top}\boldsymbol\delta^{(Z)}\end{gathered}\]

The weights: \(\boldsymbol\delta^{(Z)}\mathbf x^\top\) is an outer product — blame of my row, times activity of my column. The biases: the blame itself. The input: \(A^\top\boldsymbol\delta^{(Z)}\) re-mixes the blame back through the transposed weights — and this vector is precisely the \(\boldsymbol\delta^{(a)}\) the previous layer receives as its mail. Layer after layer, the same four lines: that is deep learning's inner loop.

Prove it · where σ(1−σ) comes from

Claim. \(\sigma(z)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\) satisfies \(\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\) — so the backward pass needs no new function evaluations.

1
Differentiate with Rule 1: \(\sigma=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}\), outer slope \(-(1+e^{-z})^{-2}\), inner slope \(-e^{-z}\!:\) \[\sigma'=-(1+e^{-z})^{-2}\cdot(-e^{-z})=\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^{2}}\] The two minus signs cancel — sigmoid always rises.
2
Split the fraction into two factors you can recognise: \[\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^{2}}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}}_{=\ \sigma}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}}_{=\ 1-\sigma} \qquad\text{since}\quad 1-\sigma=\frac{(1+e^{-z})-1}{1+e^{-z}}=\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}\] So \(\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\). ∎ Output in, derivative out: \(\mathbf a\odot(1-\mathbf a)\) uses only the \(\mathbf a\) the forward pass cached. This is why frameworks store activations — the way back is pure arithmetic.
Prove it · why the sigmoid step is entrywise — the Jacobian is diagonal

Claim. \(\dfrac{\partial\mathbf a}{\partial Z}=\operatorname{diag}\!\big(\mathbf a\odot(1-\mathbf a)\big)\), and therefore \(\boldsymbol\delta^{(Z)}=\boldsymbol\delta^{(a)}\odot\mathbf a\odot(1-\mathbf a)\) — a multiplication per neuron, no matrix at all.

1
Who depends on whom. \(a_i=\sigma(Z_i)\) reads exactly one input, \(Z_i\). Nudge a different \(Z_j\) and \(a_i\) does not move: \[\frac{\partial a_i}{\partial Z_j}=0\quad(i\neq j)\] Every off-diagonal entry of the Jacobian is zero because the activation never mixes neurons — it is applied one entry at a time.
2
The diagonal. On its own input, \(a_i\) is the one-variable function \(\sigma\), and the box above gives its slope: \[\frac{\partial a_i}{\partial Z_i}=\sigma'(Z_i)=\sigma(Z_i)\big(1-\sigma(Z_i)\big)=a_i(1-a_i)\] Steps 1 and 2 together: a matrix with \(a_i(1-a_i)\) down the diagonal and \(0\) everywhere else — \(\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf a\odot(1-\mathbf a))\).
3
Chain it (Rule 1 in matrix form). The blame on \(Z\) is the blame on \(\mathbf a\) times this Jacobian; multiplying a row by a diagonal matrix scales each entry: \[\delta^{(Z)}_i=\sum_j \delta^{(a)}_j\,\frac{\partial a_j}{\partial Z_i}=\delta^{(a)}_i\,a_i(1-a_i)\] The sum over \(j\) is Rule 2, but only the \(j=i\) term survives (step 1) — so the "sum over routes" has one route per neuron. Written for all \(i\) at once, that is the \(\odot\) formula.
4
Check on the widget's numbers. \(\mathbf a=(0.622,\,0.953)\) gives \(\mathbf a\odot(1-\mathbf a)=(0.235,\,0.045)\); with \(\boldsymbol\delta^{(a)}=(0.6,\,-0.4)\), \[\boldsymbol\delta^{(Z)}=(0.6\cdot0.235,\ \ -0.4\cdot0.045)=(0.141,\,-0.018)\] — exactly what stage 3 below displays. ∎ Neuron 2 sits at \(a=0.953\), nearly saturated, so its factor \(0.045\) throttles almost all of its blame. That is the vanishing-gradient mechanism, in one entry of a diagonal matrix.

Claim. Every identity in the table is entry-wise differentiation, organised. The method is always the same: find every appearance of the variable, add one term per appearance.

Dot product. \(\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf a=\sum_i x_i a_i\). Differentiate with respect to one coordinate \(x_k\!:\) every term is constant except \(x_k a_k\), whose slope is \(a_k\). Collect the coordinates into a row: \[\frac{\partial(\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf a)}{\partial\mathbf x}=\mathbf a^{\top}\] And \(\mathbf a^{\top}\mathbf x\) is the same scalar, so the table's second line comes free.

The sandwich. \(\mathbf a^{\top}X\mathbf b=\sum_{i,j}a_i X_{ij}b_j\). Why it's so cheap — one sweep, everything reused

Stack \(K\) layers, \(f_i=\sigma_i(A_{i-1}f_{i-1}+b_{i-1})\), and put a loss at the end: \(L=\|\mathbf y-f_K\|^2\). Every layer's parameters \(\theta_i=\{A_i,b_i\}\) need a gradient, and the chain rule writes each one as a product that reaches from the loss back to that layer:

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_{K-1}}&=\frac{\partial L}{\partial f_K}\frac{\partial f_K}{\partial\theta_{K-1}}\\[.35em] \frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_{K-2}}&=\frac{\partial L}{\partial f_K}\frac{\partial f_K}{\partial f_{K-1}}\frac{\partial f_{K-1}}{\partial\theta_{K-2}}\\[.35em] \frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_{i}}&=\frac{\partial L}{\partial f_K}\frac{\partial f_K}{\partial f_{K-1}}\cdots\frac{\partial f_{i+2}}{\partial f_{i+1}}\frac{\partial f_{i+1}}{\partial\theta_{i}}\end{aligned}\]

Look at the shape of those formulas: each one is the previous one's product, extended by one factor. Computed naively, left to right every time, you would rebuild the same long prefix over and over. Computed once from the loss end — keeping the running product as the \(\boldsymbol\delta\) that Section 7 handed backwards — every factor in the whole triangle is touched exactly once:

Prove it · the stacked formula is the recipe of §5, unrolled

Claim. \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_i}=\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial f_K}\,\dfrac{\partial f_K}{\partial f_{K-1}}\cdots\dfrac{\partial f_{i+2}}{\partial f_{i+1}}\,\dfrac{\partial f_{i+1}}{\partial\theta_i}\) — no new chain rule, just the postal recipe on a graph that happens to be a line.

1
Draw the graph. \(f_{i+1}=\sigma_{i+1}(A_if_i+b_i)\) means node \(f_{i+1}\) has exactly two parents, \(f_i\) and \(\theta_i=\{A_i,b_i\}\), and node \(f_i\) has exactly one child, \(f_{i+1}\). The loss \(L\) is the single child of \(f_K\). A stack of layers is a chain: each activation feeds only the next. Forks exist only where a parameter joins in.
2
Recipe at an activation node. §5's rule sums over children; \(f_j\) has one, so the sum has one term: \[\bar f_K=\frac{\partial L}{\partial f_K},\qquad \bar f_j=\bar f_{j+1}\,\frac{\partial f_{j+1}}{\partial f_j}\quad(jOne child, one piece of mail. Rule 2's sum collapses to a single Rule 1 product.
3
Unroll. Apply step 2 repeatedly, from \(K\) down to \(i+1\): \[\bar f_{i+1}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial f_K}\,\frac{\partial f_K}{\partial f_{K-1}}\cdots\frac{\partial f_{i+2}}{\partial f_{i+1}}\] Each application appends one more Jacobian on the right. This running product is the \(\boldsymbol\delta^{(a)}\) that §7 handed down to each layer.
4
Recipe at the parameter node. \(\theta_i\) has one child, \(f_{i+1}\), so \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_i}=\bar\theta_i=\bar f_{i+1}\,\frac{\partial f_{i+1}}{\partial\theta_i}\] and substituting step 3 gives the claim. ∎ Every layer's gradient is the same prefix \(\bar f_{i+1}\) times one local factor — which is exactly why computing the prefixes once, from the loss end, prices the whole stack.

That asymmetry is the whole story.
2
Multiply from the loss end. \((1\times m)\cdot(m\times m)\) costs about \(m^2\) multiplications — and returns another \(1\times m\) row. 3
Multiply from the input end. 4
Same factors, same answer, wildly different bill. Keeping the scalar-loss end first means every step is a cheap row-times-matrix product. Flip that ratio and the other order — forward mode — wins instead.

Fine print worth knowing

Three ways to differentiate, only one scales. Symbolic differentiation (what you do on paper, what algebra systems do) produces exact formulas — which explode in size for deep compositions. Numerical differentiation nudges the input and divides — one pass per parameter, plus a precision trap you can see below. Automatic differentiation — this unit — is a third thing: exact like symbolic, cheap like a function call, because it differentiates the program, step by step, instead of the formula.

The precision trap. A finite difference \(\tfrac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}\) has two enemies. Big \(h\): the secant misses the tangent (Unit 6's curvature gap). Tiny \(h\): the computer subtracts two nearly equal numbers and the difference drowns in rounding error. Squeeze \(h\) yourself:

Prove it · the V-shape has a formula

Claim. The checker's error falls like \(h^2\), rises like \(1/h\), and bottoms out at a best step \(h_*\) of order \(10^{-6}\)–\(10^{-5}\) — all three facts derived, not observed.

1
Taylor both evaluations around \(x\) (Unit 6's tool, Unit 8's whole subject): \[f(x\pm h)=f(x)\pm f'(x)h+\tfrac{f''(x)}{2}h^{2}\pm\tfrac{f'''(x)}{6}h^{3}+\cdots\] Same expansion, two signs — writing them together is what makes the next line click.
2
Subtract — the even terms cancel. \(f(x+h)-f(x-h)=2f'(x)h+\tfrac{f'''(x)}{3}h^{3}+\cdots\). Divide by \(2h\): \[\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}=f'(x)+\frac{f'''(x)}{6}\,h^{2}+\cdots\] The \(h^2\) term is the left wall of the V — slope 2 on log–log axes. (The one-sided version \(\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\) keeps an \(\frac{f''}{2}h\) term — slope only 1. That is why the checker uses the centred difference.)
3
Rounding. The computer stores \(f(x\pm h)\) only to relative precision \(u\approx10^{-16}\), so the subtraction of two nearly equal numbers carries an absolute error of about \(u|f(x)|\); dividing by \(2h\) turns it into \[\text{rounding error}\approx\frac{u\,|f(x)|}{2h}\] The \(1/h\) is the right wall: shrink \(h\) and you divide the same noise by an ever smaller number.
4
Add the walls and set the slope to zero. \(E(h)\approx\frac{|f'''|}{6}h^{2}+\frac{u|f|}{2h}\). Differentiate in \(h\): \[E'(h)=\frac{|f'''|}{3}\,h-\frac{u\,|f|}{2h^{2}}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h^{3}=\frac{3\,u\,|f|}{2\,|f'''|}\] A rising wall plus a falling wall has one lowest point, where the two slopes cancel. Multiplying through by \(h^2\) isolates \(h^3\).
5
Read off the best step. \[h_{*}=\Big(\frac{3\,u\,|f|}{2\,|f'''|}\Big)^{1/3}\sim u^{1/3}\approx 5\times10^{-6}\quad\text{when }|f'''|\approx|f|\] For the monster at \(x=1\), \(|f|=1.09\) but \(|f'''|\approx153\), so \(h_*\approx1\times10^{-6}\) — the huge third derivative pushes the floor left, which is what the widget shows. ∎ And the best possible error is only \(\sim u^{2/3}\approx10^{-11}\) (about \(10^{-10}\) here) — while autodiff is exact to full precision. That is the entire case for automatic differentiation, compressed into one minimisation.

\(\sigma' \) is free. The identity \(\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\) means the backward pass needs no new function calls — the forward pass already computed \(\mathbf a\), and \(\mathbf a\odot(1-\mathbf a)\) is two cheap operations. The same trick works for \(\tanh\) (\(1-a^2\)). Frameworks cache activations on the way forward precisely so the way back is arithmetic only — that cache is the "memory cost of training".

Vanishing gradients, revisited. Each sigmoid layer multiplies the blame by \(\mathbf a\odot(1-\mathbf a)\le\tfrac14\). Fifty layers deep, the blame arriving at layer one has passed fifty such factors — Unit 6's gears compounding — which is why early deep networks would not train, and why ReLU (slope 1 where active) took over.

Reverse is for losses; forward exists too. Everything here swept blame backwards, which prices all inputs of one output — perfect for a scalar loss. There is a mirror-image forward mode that prices all outputs of one input; it wins only when outputs vastly outnumber inputs, which training never is. That is why every deep-learning framework is a reverse-mode machine.

A one-layer network computes \(\mathbf z=W\mathbf x\) and the loss \(g(\mathbf z)=\tfrac12\|\mathbf z-\mathbf t\|^2\) against a target \(\mathbf t\), with \[W=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf t=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\2\end{bmatrix}.\] (a) Compute \(\nabla_{\mathbf z}\,g\) (with its dimension) and \(J=\partial\mathbf z/\partial\mathbf x\) (with its dimension). (b) Combine them to get \(\nabla_{\mathbf x}\,g\), and give the loss value.

What this tests. One honest backward step through a linear layer: the upstream gradient is the residual, and it returns to the input through \(W^{\top}\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — forward pass. \(\mathbf z=W\mathbf x=(1{+}2,\ 0{+}1,\ -1{+}1)^{\top}=(3,1,0)^{\top}\). Residual \(\mathbf r=\mathbf z-\mathbf t=(2,1,-2)^{\top}\), and the loss is \(g=\tfrac12(2^2+1^2+(-2)^2)=\tfrac92=4.5\).
(a) Step 2 — gradient at the output. For \(g=\tfrac12\|\mathbf z-\mathbf t\|^2\), \(\ \nabla_{\mathbf z}\,g=\mathbf z-\mathbf t=\mathbf r=(2,1,-2)^{\top}\) — dimension \(3\times1\), one entry per output. The Jacobian of a linear map is the matrix itself: \(J=\partial(W\mathbf x)/\partial\mathbf x=W\), dimension \(3\times2\) (outputs × inputs).
(b) Step 3 — chain them. \[\nabla_{\mathbf x}\,g=J^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf z}\,g=W^{\top}\mathbf r=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&-1\\2&1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\-2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\cdot2+0\cdot1+(-1)(-2)\\2\cdot2+1\cdot1+1\cdot(-2)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}4\\3\end{bmatrix}\] Dimension check: \(\nabla_{\mathbf z}g\) is \(3\times1\), \(W^{\top}\) is \(2\times3\), product \(2\times1\) — the shape of \(\mathbf x\), as a gradient must be. ✓

\(\nabla_{\mathbf z}g=(2,1,-2)^{\top}\) (3×1), \(J=W\) (3×2), \(\nabla_{\mathbf x}g=(4,3)^{\top}\) (2×1), loss \(=4.5\).

Remember

"Backprop through a linear layer" = "multiply the incoming gradient by \(W^{\top}\)". You can also check by substituting first: \(g(\mathbf x)=\tfrac12\|W\mathbf x-\mathbf t\|^2\) has gradient \(W^{\top}(W\mathbf x-\mathbf t)=W^{\top}\mathbf r\) — same answer, one line.

Problem 2medium

A neuron computes \(z=wx+b,\ a=\tanh(z)\), and the loss \(L=\tfrac12(a-y)^2\). Using \(\tanh'(z)=1-\tanh^2(z)\) and the values \(w=0.5,\ x=1,\ b=0,\ y=1\): (a) do the forward pass \((z,a,L)\); (b) backpropagate to obtain \(\partial L/\partial w,\ \partial L/\partial b,\ \partial L/\partial x\).

What this tests. The full forward–backward cycle on a single neuron, and the shared blame \(\delta\) that Section 6 trains with.

Show the full solution
(a) Forward. \(z=0.5\cdot1+0=0.5\); \(\ a=\tanh(0.5)=0.4621\); \(\ L=\tfrac12(0.4621-1)^2=\tfrac12(-0.5379)^2=0.1447\).
(b) Step 1 — the shared blame. Walk \(z\to a\to L\) backwards: \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial a}=a-y=-0.5379,\qquad \frac{da}{dz}=1-a^2=1-0.2136=0.7864\] \[\delta\;=\;\frac{\partial L}{\partial z}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial a}\,\frac{da}{dz}=(-0.5379)(0.7864)=-0.4230\]
Step 2 — hand out copies. \(z=wx+b\) gives \(\partial z/\partial w=x=1\), \(\partial z/\partial b=1\), \(\partial z/\partial x=w=0.5\): \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=\delta\cdot x=-0.4230,\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}=\delta=-0.4230,\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial x}=\delta\cdot w=-0.2115\]

\(z=0.5,\ a=0.4621,\ L=0.1447\); \(\ \partial L/\partial w=-0.4230,\ \partial L/\partial b=-0.4230,\ \partial L/\partial x=-0.2115\).

Remember

\(\delta\) is computed once and reused three times — the whole economy of backprop in miniature. Both parameter gradients are negative, so a descent step raises \(w\) and \(b\), pushing \(a\) toward the target \(1\). Section 6's widget runs exactly these numbers.

Problem 3easy

A scalar function is built from two intermediates: \(f=uv\), \(\ u=x^2+y\), \(\ v=x-y\). (a) Write \(\partial f/\partial u,\ \partial f/\partial v\) and the four partials of \(u,v\) with respect to \(x,y\). (b) Using the chain rule, assemble \(\partial f/\partial x\) and \(\partial f/\partial y\), and evaluate both at \((x,y)=(1,1)\).

What this tests. Multiply along paths, add across paths — the two rules of Section 3, by hand.

Show the full solution
(a) The local derivatives. Outer: \(\partial f/\partial u=v\), \(\ \partial f/\partial v=u\). Inner: \(\partial u/\partial x=2x\), \(\ \partial u/\partial y=1\), \(\ \partial v/\partial x=1\), \(\ \partial v/\partial y=-1\).
(b) Step 1 — two paths from each input. \[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}}_{\text{via }u}+\underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}}_{\text{via }v}=v\,(2x)+u\,(1)=2x(x-y)+(x^2+y)\] \[\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=v\,(1)+u\,(-1)=(x-y)-(x^2+y)\]
Step 2 — evaluate at \((1,1)\) where \(u=2,\ v=0\): \[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Big|_{(1,1)}=0\cdot2+2\cdot1=2,\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\Big|_{(1,1)}=0\cdot1+2\cdot(-1)=-2\]
Step 3 — cross-check by expanding first. \(f=(x^2+y)(x-y)=x^3-x^2y+xy-y^2\), so \(\partial f/\partial x=3x^2-2xy+y=3-2+1=2\) ✓ and \(\partial f/\partial y=-x^2+x-2y=-1+1-2=-2\) ✓.

\(\partial f/\partial x\,(1,1)=2\), \(\ \partial f/\partial y\,(1,1)=-2\) — by paths and by expansion alike.

Problem 4hard

Define \(g(\mathbf z,\boldsymbol\nu):=\log p(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)-\log q(\mathbf z,\boldsymbol\nu)\) with \(\mathbf z:=t(\boldsymbol\epsilon,\boldsymbol\nu)\), for differentiable functions \(p,q,t\) and \(\mathbf x\in\mathbb{R}^D,\ \mathbf z\in\mathbb{R}^E,\ \boldsymbol\nu\in\mathbb{R}^F,\ \boldsymbol\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^G\). Using the chain rule, compute the total derivative \(\dfrac{d}{d\boldsymbol\nu}g(\mathbf z,\boldsymbol\nu)\).

What this tests. Direct and indirect paths of influence — the multivariate chain rule when a variable appears both explicitly and through an intermediate. (This is the gradient inside variational autoencoders.)

Show the full solution
Step 1 — draw the graph. \(\boldsymbol\nu\) reaches \(g\) two ways: directly (it sits inside \(\log q(\mathbf z,\boldsymbol\nu)\)) and indirectly (it builds \(\mathbf z=t(\boldsymbol\epsilon,\boldsymbol\nu)\), and \(\mathbf z\) feeds both \(\log p\) and \(\log q\)). Total derivative = sum over both routes.
Step 2 — the direct route. Hold \(\mathbf z\) fixed and differentiate \(g\) in the \(\boldsymbol\nu\)-slot only. The \(\log p\) term has no direct \(\boldsymbol\nu\): \[\frac{\partial g}{\partial\boldsymbol\nu}=-\frac{\partial \log q(\mathbf z,\boldsymbol\nu)}{\partial\boldsymbol\nu}\qquad(1\times F)\]
Step 3 — the indirect route, two links. First how \(g\) feels \(\mathbf z\), then how \(\mathbf z\) feels \(\boldsymbol\nu\): \[\frac{\partial g}{\partial\mathbf z}=\frac{\partial \log p(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)}{\partial\mathbf z}-\frac{\partial \log q(\mathbf z,\boldsymbol\nu)}{\partial\mathbf z}\quad(1\times E),\qquad \frac{\partial\mathbf z}{\partial\boldsymbol\nu}=\frac{\partial t(\boldsymbol\epsilon,\boldsymbol\nu)}{\partial\boldsymbol\nu}\quad(E\times F)\]
Step 4 — add the routes. \[\frac{d g}{d\boldsymbol\nu}=-\frac{\partial \log q}{\partial\boldsymbol\nu}\;+\;\left(\frac{\partial \log p}{\partial\mathbf z}-\frac{\partial \log q}{\partial\mathbf z}\right)\frac{\partial t}{\partial\boldsymbol\nu}\] Dimension check: \((1\times F)+(1\times E)(E\times F)=(1\times F)\) ✓.

\(\dfrac{dg}{d\boldsymbol\nu}=-\dfrac{\partial\log q}{\partial\boldsymbol\nu}+\Big(\dfrac{\partial\log p}{\partial\mathbf z}-\dfrac{\partial\log q}{\partial\mathbf z}\Big)\dfrac{\partial t}{\partial\boldsymbol\nu}\).

Remember

Write \(d/d\boldsymbol\nu\) (total) for "all routes", \(\partial/\partial\boldsymbol\nu\) (partial) for "this slot only, everything else frozen". The whole problem is Section 3's fork rule wearing research-paper notation.

Problem 5medium

Compute \(\dfrac{df}{d\mathbf x}\) for \(f(z)=\log(1+z)\), \(\ z=\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf x\), \(\ \mathbf x\in\mathbb{R}^D\). State the dimension of every partial derivative involved.

What this tests. A two-step graph \(\mathbf x\to z\to f\) where the middle is a scalar — plus the cookbook's dot-product identity applied to \(\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf x\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the outer link. \(\dfrac{df}{dz}=\dfrac{1}{1+z}\). Dimension \(1\times1\).
Step 2 — the inner link. \(z=\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf x=\sum_i x_i^2\), so \(\partial z/\partial x_k=2x_k\), assembled as the row \[\frac{\partial z}{\partial\mathbf x}=2\mathbf x^{\top}\qquad(1\times D)\] (Or by the cookbook: \(\mathbf x^\top B\mathbf x\) with \(B=I\) gives \(\mathbf x^{\top}(I+I)=2\mathbf x^{\top}\).)
Step 3 — multiply the links. \[\frac{df}{d\mathbf x}=\frac{df}{dz}\,\frac{\partial z}{\partial\mathbf x}=\frac{2\mathbf x^{\top}}{1+\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf x}\qquad(1\times1)(1\times D)=(1\times D)\ \checkmark\]
Step 4 — sanity check with numbers. At \(\mathbf x=(1,2)^{\top}\): \(z=5\), so the formula gives \(\tfrac{2}{6}(1,2)=(\tfrac13,\tfrac23)\) — and differentiating \(\log(1+x_1^2+x_2^2)\) directly gives \(\big(\tfrac{2x_1}{1+z},\tfrac{2x_2}{1+z}\big)=(\tfrac13,\tfrac23)\) ✓.

\(\dfrac{df}{d\mathbf x}=\dfrac{2\mathbf x^{\top}}{1+\mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf x}\), a \(1\times D\) row.

Compute \(\dfrac{d\mathbf f}{d\mathbf x}\) for \(\mathbf f(\mathbf z)=\sin(\mathbf z)\) applied entrywise, \(\ \mathbf z=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b\), with \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{E\times D},\ \mathbf x\in\mathbb{R}^{D},\ \mathbf b\in\mathbb{R}^{E}\). State every dimension.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the outer Jacobian. \(f_i=\sin(z_i)\) touches only its own \(z_i\), so off-diagonal partials vanish and \[\frac{\partial\mathbf f}{\partial\mathbf z}=\operatorname{diag}\!\big(\cos(z_1),\dots,\cos(z_E)\big)=\operatorname{diag}\!\big(\cos(\mathbf z)\big)\qquad(E\times E)\] — the same diagonal shape as Section 7's \(\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf a\odot(1-\mathbf a))\), and for the same reason: entrywise functions have diagonal Jacobians.

Verify two cookbook identities the honest way, entry by entry. (a) For \(B=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix}\), expand \(\mathbf x^{\top}B\mathbf x\) and confirm \(\partial(\mathbf x^{\top}B\mathbf x)/\partial\mathbf x=\mathbf x^{\top}(B+B^{\top})\). (b) For \(\mathbf a=(1,2)^{\top},\ \mathbf b=(3,1)^{\top}\), expand \(\mathbf a^{\top}X\mathbf b\) and confirm \(\partial(\mathbf a^{\top}X\mathbf b)/\partial X=\mathbf a\mathbf b^{\top}\).

What this tests. That the identities are nothing but organised entry-wise differentiation — once verified by hand, you may use them forever with a clear conscience.

Show the full solution
(a) Step 1 — expand the scalar. \[\mathbf x^{\top}B\mathbf x=x_1^2+2x_1x_2+3x_2x_1+4x_2^2=x_1^2+5x_1x_2+4x_2^2\]
Step 2 — differentiate like any polynomial. \(\partial/\partial x_1=2x_1+5x_2\), \(\ \partial/\partial x_2=5x_1+8x_2\), so the gradient row is \([\,2x_1+5x_2,\ 5x_1+8x_2\,]\).

Step 2 — differentiate per entry. \(\partial/\partial X_{ij}=a_ib_j\): the four coefficients \(3,1,6,2\) arranged in \(X\)'s own shape are \[\frac{\partial(\mathbf a^{\top}X\mathbf b)}{\partial X}=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\6&2\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf a\mathbf b^{\top}\ \checkmark\]

Problem 9medium

The activation family, from first principles. (a) Show that the softplus \(s(z)=\ln(1+e^{z})\) has derivative \(s'(z)=\sigma(z)\) — the sigmoid is softplus's slope. (b) Prove the bridge \(\tanh(z)=2\,\sigma(2z)-1\). (c) Using (b) and \(\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\), derive \(\tanh'=1-\tanh^{2}\) without a quotient rule.

What this tests. That the activation identities frameworks hard-code are a small connected family — one proof engine (find the appearances, chain the slopes) generates them all.

Show the full solution
(a) Chain rule through the logarithm. Outer slope \(1/(1+e^{z})\), inner slope \(e^{z}\): \[s'(z)=\frac{e^{z}}{1+e^{z}}=\frac{1}{e^{-z}+1}=\sigma(z)\ \checkmark\] (dividing top and bottom by \(e^{z}\) in the middle step). So the smooth ramp softplus steepens exactly at the sigmoid's rate — which is why \(\sigma\) is also called the logistic function.
(b) Build \(2\sigma(2z)-1\) and simplify. \[2\,\sigma(2z)-1=\frac{2}{1+e^{-2z}}-1=\frac{2-(1+e^{-2z})}{1+e^{-2z}}=\frac{1-e^{-2z}}{1+e^{-2z}}\] Multiply top and bottom by \(e^{z}\): \[\frac{e^{z}-e^{-z}}{e^{z}+e^{-z}}=\tanh(z)\ \checkmark\] In words: tanh is a sigmoid, stretched to run twice as fast and re-centred to fill \((-1,1)\).
(c) Differentiate the bridge with Rule 1. Write \(t=\tanh z\) and note from (b) that \(\sigma(2z)=\tfrac{1+t}{2}\), hence \(1-\sigma(2z)=\tfrac{1-t}{2}\). Then \[\tanh'(z)=2\cdot\sigma'(2z)\cdot 2=4\,\sigma(2z)\big(1-\sigma(2z)\big)=4\cdot\frac{1+t}{2}\cdot\frac{1-t}{2}=(1+t)(1-t)=1-t^{2}\ \checkmark\] — the factor \(2\) outside is the bridge's scaling, the factor \(2\) inside is the chain rule through \(2z\).

\(s'=\sigma\); \(\ \tanh z=2\sigma(2z)-1\); \(\ \tanh'=1-\tanh^{2}\), derived purely from \(\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\).

Remember

All three facts are one family portrait: softplus's slope is \(\sigma\), \(\tanh\) is a rescaled \(\sigma\), and both backward-pass identities (\(\sigma(1-\sigma)\) and \(1-a^2\)) are the same identity seen through the bridge. Knowing one buys you the others.

Problem 10hard

The cookbook earns its keep: ridge regression solved by pure algebra. Consider the regularised least-squares loss \[E(\mathbf s)=\|\mathbf x-A\mathbf s\|^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf s\|^{2}\] (a) Using the cookbook identities, derive \(\nabla_{\mathbf s}E\). (b) Set it to zero and show the minimiser solves \((A^{\top}A+\lambda I)\,\mathbf s=A^{\top}\mathbf x\). (c) Solve numerically for \[A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\2&1\\0&3\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad \lambda=1.\]

What this tests. That the identities you proved are tools, not trivia: two of them combine into the closed-form solution of ridge regression — the first machine-learning model this course can now train end-to-end by algebra alone.

Show the full solution
(a) Differentiate each piece with the cookbook. The first piece is the least-squares identity with \(W=I\): \[\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf s}\|\mathbf x-A\mathbf s\|^{2}=-2(\mathbf x-A\mathbf s)^{\top}A\] The second is the quadratic identity with \(B=I\) (so \(B+B^{\top}=2I\)): \[\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf s}\lambda\,\mathbf s^{\top}\mathbf s=2\lambda\,\mathbf s^{\top}\] Sum: \(\ \nabla_{\mathbf s}E=-2(\mathbf x-A\mathbf s)^{\top}A+2\lambda\,\mathbf s^{\top}\).
(b) Set the gradient to zero (and transpose the row into a column to read it as an equation in \(\mathbf s\)): \[-2A^{\top}(\mathbf x-A\mathbf s)+2\lambda\mathbf s=\mathbf 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; A^{\top}A\,\mathbf s+\lambda\mathbf s=A^{\top}\mathbf x \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \big(A^{\top}A+\lambda I\big)\mathbf s=A^{\top}\mathbf x\ \checkmark\] This is a stationary point, and it is the minimum: \(E\) is a sum of squares whose quadratic part \(\mathbf s^{\top}(A^{\top}A+\lambda I)\mathbf s\) is positive for every \(\mathbf s\neq\mathbf 0\) once \(\lambda>0\) — the bowl opens upward in every direction, and adding \(\lambda I\) even guarantees the system is invertible when \(A^{\top}A\) alone is not (Unit 5's nearly-singular warning, answered).
(c) Numbers. \[A^{\top}A=\begin{bmatrix}1{+}4{+}0&0{+}2{+}0\\0{+}2{+}0&0{+}1{+}9\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5&2\\2&10\end{bmatrix},\qquad A^{\top}A+I=\begin{bmatrix}6&2\\2&11\end{bmatrix},\qquad A^{\top}\mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}1{+}4{+}0\\0{+}2{+}0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5\\2\end{bmatrix}\] Solve \(\begin{bmatrix}6&2\\2&11\end{bmatrix}\mathbf s=\begin{bmatrix}5\\2\end{bmatrix}\): determinant \(66-4=62\), so by Cramer (or elimination) \[s_1=\frac{5\cdot11-2\cdot2}{62}=\frac{51}{62}\approx0.8226,\qquad s_2=\frac{6\cdot2-2\cdot5}{62}=\frac{2}{62}=\frac{1}{31}\approx0.0323\]

\(\nabla_{\mathbf s}E=-2(\mathbf x-A\mathbf s)^{\top}A+2\lambda\mathbf s^{\top}\); minimiser \((A^{\top}A+\lambda I)\mathbf s=A^{\top}\mathbf x\); numerically \(\mathbf s\approx(0.8226,\ 0.0323)^{\top}\).

Remember

Gradient \(\to\) zero \(\to\) linear system: for quadratic losses, "training" is one solve. Unit 9 begins exactly where this stops — with losses whose gradients cannot be set to zero in closed form, so we must walk downhill instead.

Problem 11medium

Differentiate a program with dual numbers (Section 11), seeding \(x=x_0+1\cdot\varepsilon\). (a) \(f(x)=x^{2}e^{x}\) at \(x_0=1\). (b) \(f(x)=\sqrt{x^{2}+9}\) at \(x_0=-4\) — then compare with Section 10's stand-in line. (c) In one sentence: why are these results exact, where the gradient checker's finite differences never are?

Show the full solution
(a) Run the pairs. (a) \((e,\,3e)\): \(f'(1)=3e\approx8.1548\). (b) \((5,\,-0.8)\): \(f'(-4)=-\tfrac45\). (c) The algebra rules are the calculus rules — nothing is approximated, so nothing needs a step size.

Remember

Reverse mode (this unit's hero) and dual numbers (its mirror) bracket the field: backward for one output and many inputs, forward for one input and many outputs. Both differentiate the program, both are exact — and neither ever writes a derivative formula.
