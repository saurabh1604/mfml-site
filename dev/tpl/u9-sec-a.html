Gradient Descent

Every learning algorithm you will ever meet is, underneath, a walk downhill. Somewhere there is a number that says how wrong the model is, and training means making that number smaller. You cannot see the whole landscape — there are too many dimensions and you are standing in fog — but you can feel the slope under your feet. That slope is the gradient, and the whole of this unit is one idea: take a step against it, then feel again. The interesting questions are the practical ones: how long a step, when to stop, what to do when the ground is a narrow trench, and how to walk at all when you cannot even afford to measure the slope exactly.

Downhill in the fog

Imagine this

You are on a mountainside at night, in thick fog, and you want to reach the lowest point of the valley. You cannot see ten metres ahead. But you can do one thing perfectly: you can feel which way the ground tilts under your boots. So you turn to face straight downhill, take a step, stop, feel the tilt again, turn, step. Nobody gave you a map. You never needed one. You only needed the tilt under your feet, over and over.

That is the entire method. What makes it worth a whole unit is that "the lowest point of the valley" is what every machine-learning model is looking for. A model has parameters — the numbers inside it. For any choice of those numbers there is a loss: a single number that says how badly the model is doing on the data. The loss depends on the parameters, so it is a landscape over parameter-space, and training means walking down that landscape to a low point.

Two words from the lecture before we start walking. Optimization is unconstrained when you may put the parameters anywhere — the whole mountainside is yours. It is constrained when there are fences: some parameters must stay positive, or add up to one, or lie inside a region. Fences change the game (they get their own unit), so this unit is fence-free: any point is allowed, and we only assume the landscape is smooth enough to have a slope everywhere.

And the slope has a name you know from Unit 6. At a point \(\mathbf x\), the gradient \(\nabla f(\mathbf x)\) is the vector of partial derivatives. The fact that matters for walking is this one:

The gradient is an uphill compass

\(\nabla f(\mathbf x)\) points in the direction in which \(f\) increases fastest, and its length is how fast. So the direction of steepest descent is \(-\nabla f(\mathbf x)\).

We will prove that in a moment (it is one line of Cauchy–Schwarz). For now, hold the picture: the gradient is a compass needle that points uphill, and we always walk the opposite way.

In one sentence: Training is a walk downhill on the loss landscape, done in fog, using the gradient as an uphill compass and stepping the other way.

Where the landscape comes from — fitting a line

Four receipts

A small print shop keeps four receipts: 1 box of paper cost 3.1, 2 boxes cost 4.9, 3 boxes cost 7.3, 4 boxes cost 9.1 (hundreds of rupees, say). The owner wants a rule of thumb — price ≈ a × boxes + b — so she can quote a customer for any number of boxes. Which \(a\) and which \(b\)? Whichever pair makes the rule's predictions closest to the four receipts she actually has.

This is the lecture's opening example, and it is the smallest possible machine-learning problem. The data:

\(x\) (boxes)\(y\) (price)
13.1
24.9
37.3
49.1

The model is a straight line, \(\hat y = ax+b\). The two parameters \(a\) and \(b\) are the knobs. To say how wrong a given pair of knobs is, look at each receipt, compute the miss \(y_i-\hat y_i\), square it (so misses in either direction count, and big misses count a lot), and add up:

\[L(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\big(y_i-(ax_i+b)\big)^2\]

Read \(L(a,b)\) as a landscape: pick any \((a,b)\), and \(L\) tells you the height of the ground there. The best line is the lowest point of that landscape. Walk it:

To walk you need the compass, so you need the gradient of \(L\). It is a chain-rule exercise from Unit 6, and it is worth doing slowly once because every regression gradient has this exact shape:

Prove it · the gradient of the squared-error loss

Claim. With residuals \(r_i=y_i-(ax_i+b)\), \(\ \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial a}=-2\sum_i x_i r_i\ \) and \(\ \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial b}=-2\sum_i r_i\).

1
Name the inside of each square: \(r_i=y_i-ax_i-b\). Then \(L=\sum_i r_i^2\). Giving the residual a name turns a messy expression into "a sum of squares of something", which is easy to differentiate.
2
Differentiate one square by the chain rule: \[\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\,r_i^2=2r_i\cdot\frac{\partial r_i}{\partial a}=2r_i\cdot(-x_i)=-2x_ir_i.\] Outer derivative (\(2r_i\)) times inner derivative. Inside \(r_i\), the only place \(a\) appears is \(-ax_i\), whose derivative is \(-x_i\).
3
Same for \(b\): inside \(r_i\), \(b\) appears as \(-b\), so \(\dfrac{\partial r_i}{\partial b}=-1\) and \[\frac{\partial}{\partial b}\,r_i^2=2r_i\cdot(-1)=-2r_i.\] The intercept touches every residual in the same way, so its partial is just the plain sum of residuals.
4
A derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives: \[\nabla L=\Big(-2\sum_i x_ir_i,\ \ -2\sum_i r_i\Big).\ \ \text{That is the claim.}\] ∎ Read it: if the residuals are mostly positive (the line sits below the data), both partials are negative, so the descent step \(-\gamma\nabla L\) raises \(a\) and \(b\) — the line tilts up toward the points. The compass agrees with common sense.

For this tiny problem you do not actually need to walk: set both partials to zero and you get two linear equations in \(a,b\) (the normal equations), and they solve to \(a^*=2.04,\ b^*=1\) with \(L^*=0.072\). Practice Problem 11 does exactly that. So why bother with gradient descent at all? Because the line was the easiest model anyone could think of. Make the model a neural network and \(L\) has millions of knobs and no formula for its bottom — the lecture's phrase is that the minimum cannot be found analytically in closed form. Then the fog-walk is not one option among many. It is the only option there is. That is why we practise it on a problem small enough to check.

One more thing the widget shows: the landscape is a bowl, but a lopsided one — a long narrow trench. Changing \(a\) a little changes \(L\) a lot (every \(x_i\) multiplies it), changing \(b\) a little barely matters. Keep that trench in mind; it is the reason step sizes are hard, and we will come back to it with numbers.

Pause & predict

Start at \(a=b=0\): the line is flat on the floor and every receipt sits above it, so all four residuals are positive. What does the first descent step do to \(a\)?

In one sentence: A model plus a loss turns "which parameters?" into a landscape; the gradient of a squared-error loss is \(-2\sum(\text{input})\times(\text{residual})\), and it points the line toward the data.

Where the walk ends — flat spots and the second-derivative test

The marble

Drop a marble on a curved floor and it rolls until the floor under it is level. Level floor is where it stops — but level floor is not always the bottom of a bowl. A marble balanced on top of a hill is also on level floor; the slightest breath sends it rolling. And a marble on a saddle-shaped floor is level yet unstable in one direction. So "the walk stopped" and "we found a valley" are two different claims, and telling them apart needs one more measurement: which way the floor bends.

In the fog-walk, we stop when the gradient is zero — no tilt, nowhere to go. Such a point is a stationary point. The lecture's one-variable example makes the point that it might be a minimum or a maximum:

\[l(x)=x^4+7x^3+5x^2-17x+3,\qquad l'(x)=4x^3+21x^2+10x-17.\]

The gradient is a cubic, so \(l'(x)=0\) has up to three solutions — three flat spots — at \(x\approx-4.480,\ -1.432,\ 0.662\). Which are valleys? Look at the second derivative, \(l''(x)=12x^2+42x+10\): it is positive at the first and third points (valleys, \(l=-47.07\) and \(l=-3.84\)) and negative at the middle one (a hilltop, \(l=21.25\)). Feel it for yourself:

The rule the lecture states — negative second derivative means a local maximum, otherwise a local minimum — is really a statement about the shape of the ground right next to the flat spot. Unit 8 gave us the tool to see why:

Prove it · the second-derivative test, from Taylor

Claim. If \(f'(c)=0\) and \(f''(c)>0\), then \(c\) is a local minimum; if \(f''(c)<0\), a local maximum.

1
Write Taylor's formula about \(c\) with the remainder from Unit 8, for a small step \(h\): \[\begin{aligned}f(c+h)&=f(c)+f'(c)\,h+\tfrac12 f''(\xi)\,h^2\\ &\text{for some }\xi\text{ between }c\text{ and }c+h.\end{aligned}\] Value, slope times step, half the bend times step squared — with the bend taken at a secret nearby point so the formula is exact.
2
The slope term dies because \(f'(c)=0\): \[f(c+h)-f(c)=\tfrac12 f''(\xi)\,h^2.\] At a flat spot the only thing left to decide "up or down" is the bend.
3
\(h^2\ge0\) always. If \(f''(c)>0\), then by continuity \(f''(\xi)>0\) for all small enough \(h\), so the right side is \(\ge0\): \(f(c+h)\ge f(c)\) on both sides of \(c\) — a valley. If \(f''(c)<0\) the right side is \(\le0\) — a hilltop. ∎ The sign of \(h\) never mattered: a square erases it. That is exactly why the second derivative alone decides. (If \(f''(c)=0\) the test is silent — the ground is flatter than a parabola and you must look further, as in Unit 8.)

Two things to carry from this. First, for small problems — a low-degree polynomial — you can skip the walk entirely: solve \(f'=0\), test each root, done. The lecture says so. Second, and this is the part that decides the shape of the unit: gradient descent stops at the nearest flat spot it rolls into. In the bumpy tab you can watch the walker settle into a shallow dip while a deeper valley sits a few steps away. Descent finds a local minimum — the one downhill from where you started — not the minimum. With a bowl-shaped loss (like the fitting problem) that is no worry. With a neural network it is a fact of life, and the hero animation at the top of this page has been demonstrating it since you arrived.

Pause & predict

You run gradient descent on \(l(x)=x^4+7x^3+5x^2-17x+3\) starting at \(x_0=0\). Where does it end up?

In one sentence: The walk stops where the gradient is zero; the sign of the second derivative says whether that is a valley or a hilltop, and descent only ever finds the valley downhill from its start.
