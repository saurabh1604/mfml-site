Why not a softmax over every word?

Imagine this

A teacher wants to know if you understand who chai's friends are. She could ask: "Out of all 50 000 students in this city, rank how likely each one is to be chai's friend." That takes all day.

Or she could ask five quick yes/no questions: "Is hot a friend? Is football a friend? Is kettle?…" Five answers, and she learns nearly as much.

There is a more obvious way to play word2vec's game. Given the middle word chai, ask the model to pick the neighbour out of the whole vocabulary, with a softmax from Unit 14:

\[P(\text{hot}\mid\text{chai})=\frac{e^{\mathbf u_{\text{hot}}\cdot\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}}}{\sum_{w=1}^{V}e^{\mathbf u_{w}\cdot\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}}}.\]

This is correct, and it is exactly how a full language model ends (§8). The trouble is the bottom line. To compute it you need a dot product and an \(e^{(\cdot)}\) for every word in the vocabulary. With \(V=50\,000\), that is 50 000 dot products for one training pair. Worse, the gradient of a softmax touches every one of those 50 000 neighbour lists, so every step must update all of them.

Negative sampling replaces that one giant question with \(k+1\) small yes/no questions: the real neighbour plus \(k\) random words. With \(k=5\) that is 6 dot products and 6 sigmoids per pair:

\[\frac{50\,000}{6}\approx8\,333\ \text{times less work}.\]

word2vec was trained on billions of word pairs. That saving is the difference between a year and an afternoon.

Which random words? If negatives were picked in proportion to how often words appear, "the" and "is" would be picked all the time and teach little. word2vec picks a word with probability proportional to its count raised to the power \(\tfrac34\). That gently lifts rare words: a word seen 100 times and one seen once would share the picks 99% to 1%; after the \(\tfrac34\) power it is \(100^{0.75}\approx31.6\) against 1, about 97% to 3%.

One big question, or a few small onesEvery dot is one word of the vocabulary. A full softmax must light them all for every training pair; negative sampling lights only \(k+1\).

Try: Set the vocabulary to 50 000 and \(k=5\): about 8 333 times less work. Push the vocabulary to a million: the full softmax grows, the yes/no game does not. Then press ▶ one training pair to watch both light up.

The softmax asks "which one, out of everybody?" and must look at everybody. Negative sampling asks "this one — yes or no?" a few times. The cost stops depending on the size of the vocabulary, which is what made training on billions of words possible.

A neural language model

Imagine this

Mumbai's dabbawalas pass a lunch box along a relay. One picks it up, one sorts it, one carries it, one hands it over. Nobody does the whole job, but the box always arrives.

A neural language model is a relay like that. Look up the words, glue them together, mix them in a small network, and hand over a probability for every possible next word.

In §2 we guessed the next word from a table of counts. The table could not share anything between "chai" and "coffee". In 2003, Yoshua Bengio and his team built a model that could. It works in five steps:

  1. Look up. Keep a table \(E\) with one row of \(d\) numbers for each word — an embedding table. Look up the row of each previous word.
  2. Glue. Put the rows side by side into one longer list (people say concatenate). Two previous words with \(d=2\) give a list of 4.
  3. Mix. Pass it through a hidden layer, exactly as in Unit 15: \(\mathbf h=\tanh(W\mathbf x+\mathbf b)\).
  4. Score every word. One more layer gives one score per vocabulary word, and a softmax (Unit 14) turns the scores into probabilities.
  5. Learn. Train with cross-entropy and backprop (Unit 15). The blame flows all the way back into the rows of \(E\), so the table is learned too.

The lookup is a matrix multiply. Write a word one-hot and multiply it by the table. With three words and \(d=2\):

\[\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\ 3&4\\ 5&6\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3&4\end{pmatrix}.\]

The single 1 picks out row 2. So an embedding table is nothing new: it is the first layer's weight matrix, fed with one-hot inputs. Computers skip the multiply and just fetch the row — same answer, far less work.

Now the whole relay. We trained a tiny model ourselves, for this page, on just seven sentences: "I drink hot chai .", "we drink hot chai .", "I drink hot coffee .", "we drink hot coffee .", "I play cricket .", "we play cricket .", "we play football .". It has a vocabulary of 10 words, embeddings of \(d=2\) numbers, and 8 hidden neurons. Its whole brain is 150 numbers.

Notice what it has never seen: "I play football". A count table would give it probability exactly 0. But "I" and "we" were used in the same places, so the model learned almost the same row for both. It guesses football after "I play" with probability about 0.256 — nearly what it gives after "we play" (0.258). That is sharing, and it is the whole point of word vectors.

The geometry of meaning

Imagine this

A friend gives you directions: "From the station, walk 2 blocks north and 3 blocks east." Those directions are not tied to the station. Start from the temple instead and follow the same walk — you land somewhere new, but in the same position relative to where you started.

In a good word space, the step from "man" to "woman" is a walk like that. Start the same walk from "king".

Here are four hand-made vectors with just two numbers each (we built them for this example; they are not trained):

\[\begin{aligned}\text{king}&=(3,2), & \text{man}&=(1,2),\\ \text{queen}&=(3,-2), & \text{woman}&=(1,-2).\end{aligned}\]

The step from man to woman is \(\text{woman}-\text{man}=(0,-4)\). The step from king to queen is \(\text{queen}-\text{king}=(0,-4)\). The same step. So the four points make a parallelogram, and we can use it to answer the puzzle "man is to woman as king is to ?":

\[\text{king}-\text{man}+\text{woman}=(3,2)-(1,2)+(1,-2)=(3,-2).\]

That is exactly queen. With real, trained vectors the answer is never exact; it lands near a word. So we search: which word's vector has the biggest cosine with the answer? For \((3,-2)\): queen 1, woman 0.868, king 0.385, man −0.124.

Skip the three words you started with. In trained vectors the step "woman − man" is small next to the words themselves, so the answer often lies closest to king itself. A search that allowed king would just return king — true, but useless. So the search always leaves out the three input words.

The realization

\[\begin{gathered}\mathbf q=\mathbf v_a-\mathbf v_b+\mathbf v_c,\\ \text{answer}=\arg\max_{w\notin\{a,b,c\}}\ \cos(\mathbf v_w,\mathbf q)\end{gathered}\]

In a good word space, a difference of meanings is a direction. The same arrow turns man into woman, king into queen and boy into girl; another turns a country into its capital. So "B is to C as A is to ?" becomes arithmetic: \(A-B+C\), then the nearest word by cosine, leaving out \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\).
