Where is each word? Positions

Imagine this

Tip a bag of word cards onto a table: "dog", "bites", "man". Could it say "dog bites man"? Or "man bites dog"? The bag cannot tell you. The cards are the same; only the order differs — and order is the whole news story.

Look back at the attention formula. It compares words by dot products and blends their values. Nowhere does it ask "which word came first?" So attention on its own is order-blind. Shuffle the words, and every word gets exactly the same answer as before — the answers just come out in the shuffled order.

A tiny check. Take toy vectors dog \(=(2,0)\), man \(=(0,2)\), bites \(=(1,1)\), and let each word's query, key and value be its own vector. In "dog bites man" the word dog ends up as \((1.723,\ 0.277)\). In "man bites dog" it ends up as … \((1.723,\ 0.277)\). The same. Attention cannot tell who bit whom.

The fix: add a position tag to each word's vector before attention. Give the first word \((1,0)\), the middle word \((0,0)\) and the last word \((0,1)\). Now dog becomes \((2.967,\ 0.019)\) when it comes first and \((1.576,\ 1.284)\) when it comes last. Order finally matters.

Try: Press shuffle a few times with position tags off: the words move, but each keeps exactly its arrow. Turn position tags on and shuffle again: now each word's arrow changes with its place.

Tags that work for any length: clocks. The first transformer gave each position a tag made of clock hands turning at different speeds. Picture a row of clocks. The first clock's hand turns 1 radian (about 57°) per word. The next turns 10 times slower, the next 10 times slower again. For position \(p\), each clock contributes the two numbers \(\sin(\omega p)\) and \(\cos(\omega p)\) — where its hand points. With \(d=8\) there are four clocks, with speeds \(\omega=1,\ 0.1,\ 0.01,\ 0.001\):

\[\begin{aligned}\text{pos }1:\ &(\sin1,\cos1,\ \sin0.1,\cos0.1,\ \dots)\\ &\approx(0.841,\ 0.540,\ 0.0998,\ 0.995,\\ &\qquad 0.0100,\ 1.000,\ 0.0010,\ 1.000)\end{aligned}\]

The fast clock tells near positions apart. The slow clocks tell far positions apart — like the hour hand and the minute hand together tell the time better than either alone.

Rotary positions (RoPE). Many modern models use the clock idea more directly. Instead of adding a tag, they turn the query and the key. Split each into pairs of numbers — little arrows. A word at position \(m\) turns its query arrow by \(m\theta\). A word at position \(n\) turns its key arrow by \(n\theta\). Turning is a rotation matrix, the "turn" of Unit 4.

The magic: the dot product of two arrows depends only on the angle between them. Turning both by the same extra amount changes nothing. So the score depends only on the gap \(m-n\), not on where the pair sits in the sentence.

Tiny numbers. Take \(\theta=30^\circ\) and \(\mathbf q=\mathbf k=(1,0)\). Put the query at position 3 and the key at position 1: the arrows point at \(90^\circ\) and \(30^\circ\), the angle between them is \(60^\circ\), and the score is \(\cos60^\circ=0.5\). Move them to positions 5 and 3: arrows at \(150^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\), still \(60^\circ\) apart, score 0.5. Positions 2 and 0: again 0.5.

The transformer block

Imagine this

A dabbawala's tiffin travels across Mumbai through a few stations. At each station someone may add a note or a sweet. But the tiffin itself always rides on. Nothing that was inside is thrown away — each station only adds.

A transformer is a stack of identical blocks. Each word's vector rides up through them, like the tiffin. One block has four stations:

  1. Attention. The word collects a blend from the other words (§3–§7).
  2. Add, then steady. Add what attention gave to the word's own vector: \(\mathbf x+\operatorname{Attention}(\mathbf x)\). This shortcut is the residual path. Then layer norm rescales the result to a steady size.
  3. A small network. Each word, on its own, goes through a two-layer network of Unit 15: matrix, ReLU, matrix. This is the feed-forward part. Here the word "thinks about" what it just collected.
  4. Add, then steady — again.

Why the shortcut? Because of the blame. On the way back (Unit 15), the slope of \(\mathbf x+f(\mathbf x)\) is \(1+f'\), not just \(f'\). Even if \(f'\) is tiny, the "1" carries the blame straight down, block after block. It is the LSTM's express lane from Unit 17, built into every block.

Layer norm, by hand. It makes each word's list have average 0 and spread 1 — the "standardise" of Unit 10, done inside the network, one word at a time. Take \(\mathbf x=(1,2,3,6)\):

\[\begin{aligned}\text{mean}&=\tfrac{1+2+3+6}{4}=3\\ \text{variance}&=\tfrac{(-2)^2+(-1)^2+0^2+3^2}{4}=\tfrac{14}{4}=3.5\\ \text{result}&=\tfrac{\mathbf x-3}{\sqrt{3.5}}\\ &\approx(-1.069,\ -0.535,\ 0,\ 1.604)\end{aligned}\]

Then two learned lists, a scale \(\boldsymbol\gamma\) and a shift \(\boldsymbol\beta\), stretch and move each entry, so the network can undo the steadying where it wants to.

\[\begin{aligned}\text{attention: }4d^2&=1\,048\,576\\ \text{feed-forward: }2\,d\,d_{ff}&=2\,097\,152\\ \text{weights only}&=3\,145\,728\end{aligned}\]

The realization

\[\begin{gathered}\mathbf h=\operatorname{LN}\big(\mathbf x+\operatorname{Attention}(\mathbf x)\big)\\ \mathbf y=\operatorname{LN}\big(\mathbf h+\operatorname{FFN}(\mathbf h)\big)\end{gathered}\]

A block is: look around (attention), think alone (a small network), and after each, add the result to what you had and steady it with layer norm. The adds are an express lane for the blame; layer norm keeps the numbers at a calm size, block after block.

No peeking: the causal mask

Imagine this

A quiz master reads a question one word at a time and stops to ask, "What comes next?" If you had the printed question in your hand, the game would be silly — you would just read the next word. So the rule is: you may look only at the words already read out.

A model that writes text, one word after another, plays the quiz. While it learns to predict word 3, it must not see word 3. So in the attention map every word may look only at itself and the words to its left.

The trick is tiny. Before softmax, set every score above the diagonal to \(-\infty\). Since \(e^{-\infty}=0\), those words get a share of exactly 0, and softmax spreads the whole share among the allowed words.

With the numbers of §3. Row 1 may only see word 1, so its shares are \((1,0,0)\) and its answer is \(\mathbf v_1=(2,0)\). Row 2 sees words 1 and 2 with scores \(0\) and \(1.414\):

\[\Big(\frac{1}{1+e^{1.414}},\ \frac{e^{1.414}}{1+e^{1.414}}\Big)\approx(0.196,\ 0.804)\]

and its answer is \(0.196\,(2,0)+0.804\,(0,2)\approx(0.391,\ 1.609)\). Row 3 is the last word, so it may see everything, and it is unchanged: \((0.248,0.248,0.503)\), answer \((1,1)\).

Three ways to wire attention. An encoder reads a whole input and may look both ways (no mask) — good for understanding. A decoder writes, so it uses the mask. An encoder–decoder (for translation) adds cross-attention: the decoder's words ask their queries of the encoder's words. That is the cure for the bottleneck of Unit 17: instead of one small summary note, every word being written can look back at every word of the input.

Mask the futureThe scores, the shares and the answers of the §3 example. In decoder mode the top-right cells become −∞ and their shares 0. In cross-attention a Hindi sentence being written asks its questions of an English sentence.

Try: Switch from encoder to decoder and watch row 2 re-share its weight between the two cells it may still see. Press ▶ fill row by row. Then open cross-attention and see "chai" look back at "chai", and "peeta" (drink) at "drink".

The realization

\[\begin{gathered}\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^{\mathsf T}}{\sqrt d}+M\Big)V,\\ M_{ij}=\begin{cases}0&j\le i\\ -\infty&j>i\end{cases}\end{gathered}\]

Adding \(-\infty\) above the diagonal gives the future words a share of exactly zero, so each word's answer is built only from the words before it. Because of that, one pass over a sentence trains the model on every next-word guess at once — word 1 predicting word 2, words 1–2 predicting word 3, and so on.
