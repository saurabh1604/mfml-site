Problem 1easy

A feature column holds the five values \(\{30,\,50,\,70,\,90,\,110\}\). (a) Apply min–max scaling \(x'=(x-\min)/(\max-\min)\) to all five. (b) Standardise the column (subtract the mean, divide by the population standard deviation) and check that the \(z\)-values sum to \(0\) and their squares sum to \(5\). (c) A new value \(x=130\) arrives later. Transform it under both schemes using the training statistics, and say whether the min–max result lies in \([0,1]\).

What this tests. Two of the three repairs (standardise, min–max) as plain arithmetic, and the one habit that matters at test time: reuse the training statistics, never recompute them. Plan. Read off min, max and range; then mean, deviations, variance, \(\sigma\); divide; run the two sanity sums; finally push \(130\) through both formulas with the numbers already on the page.

Show the full solution
(a) Step 1 — min–max. Here \(\min=30\), \(\max=110\), range \(=80\): \[\begin{aligned} 30&\mapsto\tfrac{0}{80}=0, & 50&\mapsto\tfrac{20}{80}=0.25,\\ 70&\mapsto\tfrac{40}{80}=0.5, & 90&\mapsto\tfrac{60}{80}=0.75,\\ 110&\mapsto\tfrac{80}{80}=1. \end{aligned}\] Evenly spaced inputs give evenly spaced outputs — min–max is a pure stretch-and-shift.
(b) Step 2 — mean and deviations. \[\begin{aligned} \mu&=\frac{30+50+70+90+110}{5}=\frac{350}{5}=70,\\ x-\mu&=(-40,\,-20,\,0,\,20,\,40). \end{aligned}\]
Step 3 — variance and standard deviation. Square the deviations and average over \(n=5\): \[\begin{aligned} \sigma^2&=\frac{1600+400+0+400+1600}{5}\\ &=\frac{4000}{5}=800,\\ \sigma&=\sqrt{800}\approx28.28. \end{aligned}\]
Step 4 — divide, then check. \(z=(x-70)/28.28\): \[\begin{aligned} z&=\Big(\tfrac{-40}{28.28},\ \tfrac{-20}{28.28},\ 0,\\ &\qquad\tfrac{20}{28.28},\ \tfrac{40}{28.28}\Big)\\ &=(-1.414,\,-0.707,\,0,\,0.707,\,1.414). \end{aligned}\] Sanity sums: \[\begin{aligned} \textstyle\sum z&=-1.414-0.707+0+0.707+1.414=0,\\ \textstyle\sum z^2&=2+0.5+0+0.5+2=5=n. \end{aligned}\] Both hold, so the column now has mean \(0\) and variance \(5/5=1\). ✓
(c) Step 5 — the unseen value, training statistics only. \[\begin{aligned} x'&=\frac{130-30}{80}=\frac{100}{80}=1.25,\\ z&=\frac{130-70}{28.28}=\frac{60}{28.28}\approx2.12. \end{aligned}\] \(1.25\) lies outside \([0,1]\): the promise "everything lands in \([0,1]\)" was only ever a promise about the training column. The \(z\)-score \(2.12\) is unusual (two standard deviations up) but perfectly well defined — \(z\)-scores are unbounded by design.

(a) \(0,\ 0.25,\ 0.5,\ 0.75,\ 1\). (b) \(\mu=70\), \(\sigma\approx28.28\), \(z=(-1.414,-0.707,0,0.707,1.414)\); \(\sum z=0\), \(\sum z^2=5\). (c) min–max \(1.25\) (outside \([0,1]\)); \(z=2.12\).

Remember

"Change the units, not the model": both repairs are invertible bookkeeping, and the bookkeeping is done once, on the training column. The slip on this type is recomputing \(\min\), \(\max\), \(\mu\), \(\sigma\) with the new point included — that silently changes every training value too. Min–max is fragile to a new extreme; standardisation degrades gracefully.

Problem 2easy

At the current iterate a loss has partial derivatives \(\partial J/\partial w_1=-640\) and \(\partial J/\partial w_2=-1.6\), and one learning rate \(\gamma=10^{-3}\) serves both knobs. (a) Compute the update to each parameter and the ratio of the two moves. (b) If gradients of this size persist, how many steps does \(w_2\) need to move by one unit, and how far does \(w_1\) travel in that time? (c) Choose \(\gamma\) instead so that \(w_1\) moves a modest \(0.01\) per step; give the new \(\gamma\), the new \(w_2\) move and the new step count for a one-unit move of \(w_2\). (d) State, in one line, what standardising the two feature columns does to the two partials.

What this tests. The hair-trigger knob in pure arithmetic — one stride length, two wildly different slopes — and the cost of protecting either knob at the other's expense. Plan. \(\Delta w_j=-\gamma\,\partial J/\partial w_j\) twice; divide \(1\) by the small move; scale \(\gamma\) down so the big move is \(0.01\) and redo the count; then name the repair as a computable claim.

Show the full solution
(a) Step 1 — the two moves. \[\begin{aligned} \Delta w_1&=-10^{-3}\,(-640)=0.64,\\ \Delta w_2&=-10^{-3}\,(-1.6)=0.0016,\\ \frac{\Delta w_1}{\Delta w_2}&=\frac{0.64}{0.0016}=400. \end{aligned}\] One stride, but a \(400:1\) imbalance in how far each knob turns.
(b) Step 2 — the slow coordinate. \[N\approx\frac{1}{0.0016}=625\ \text{steps}\] for \(w_2\) to move one unit. Meanwhile \(w_1\) moves \[625\times0.64=400\ \text{units}\] — long since past its target and bouncing.
(c) Step 3 — protect \(w_1\) instead. Require \(\gamma\times640=0.01\): \[\begin{aligned} \gamma&=\frac{0.01}{640}=1.5625\times10^{-5},\\ \Delta w_2&=1.5625\times10^{-5}\times1.6=2.5\times10^{-5},\\ N&\approx\frac{1}{2.5\times10^{-5}}=40\,000\ \text{steps}. \end{aligned}\] The hair-trigger knob is now tame; the stiff knob crawls \(64\) times more slowly than before.
(d) Step 4 — the repair. After standardising both columns, the two partials come out of the same order of magnitude (a ratio near \(1\) instead of \(400\)), so a single \(\gamma\) serves both knobs — Problem 5 does this arithmetic on real columns and watches \(576\) turn into \(1.07\).

(a) \(\Delta w_1=0.64\), \(\Delta w_2=0.0016\), ratio \(400:1\). (b) \(\approx625\) steps, during which \(w_1\) moves \(400\) units. (c) \(\gamma=1.5625\times10^{-5}\), \(\Delta w_2=2.5\times10^{-5}\), \(\approx40\,000\) steps. (d) standardising makes the partials comparable, so one \(\gamma\) fits both.

Remember

The stride \(\gamma\) is the same for every knob; it is the gradient that differs, so the actual move \(\gamma\times\)gradient is what you compare. Serve the slow knob and the fast one oscillates; serve the fast knob and the slow one takes \(40\,000\) steps. No choice of \(\gamma\) escapes — only changing the units does.

Problem 3easy

Three receipts: \(x=(1,2,3)\), \(y=(2,5,7)\), model \(\hat y=wx\) with no intercept. (a) Write the bill for being wrong \(J(w)=\sum(wx_i-y_i)^2\) and expand it to the form \(aw^2+bw+c\). (b) Find \(w^*\) by setting the slope to zero, confirm it is a minimum, and compute \(J(w^*)\) exactly. (c) Run gradient descent from \(w_0=0\) with \(\eta=0.02\) for five steps, tabulating \(w\), the slope and \(J\). (d) Give the per-step factor \(1-\eta\lambda\) and the largest \(\eta\) for which the iteration converges.

What this tests. The smallest possible training run, done twice — once by formula, once by feel — and the fact that the two agree. Plan. Expand three squares and add; differentiate; solve; evaluate \(J^*\) as a fraction with denominator \(14\); fill a five-row table; then read the curvature \(28\) for the factor and the speed limit \(2/28\).

Show the full solution
(a) Step 1 — write and expand the scorecard. \[J(w)=(w-2)^2+(2w-5)^2+(3w-7)^2.\] Each square on its own line: \[\begin{aligned} (w-2)^2&=w^2-4w+4,\\ (2w-5)^2&=4w^2-20w+25,\\ (3w-7)^2&=9w^2-42w+49. \end{aligned}\] Add columns: \[\begin{aligned} J(w)&=(1+4+9)w^2-(4+20+42)w+(4+25+49)\\ &=14w^2-66w+78. \end{aligned}\]
(b) Step 2 — slope zero, curvature positive. \[\begin{aligned} \frac{dJ}{dw}&=28w-66=0\\ w^*&=\frac{66}{28}=\frac{33}{14}\approx2.357,\\ \frac{d^2J}{dw^2}&=28>0\ \Rightarrow\ \text{a minimum.} \end{aligned}\]
Step 3 — the lowest possible bill, exactly. Put everything over \(14\): \[\begin{aligned} J(w^*)&=14\Big(\tfrac{33}{14}\Big)^2-66\cdot\tfrac{33}{14}+78\\ &=\tfrac{1089}{14}-\tfrac{2178}{14}+\tfrac{1092}{14}\\ &=\tfrac{1089-2178+1092}{14}=\tfrac{3}{14}\approx0.214. \end{aligned}\] (\(14\cdot33^2/14^2=33^2/14=1089/14\); \(66\cdot33=2178\); \(78\cdot14=1092\).)
(c) Step 4 — the parachute drop and the walk. Update \(w\leftarrow w-0.02\,(28w-66)\), one row per step:

Remember

For one knob the formula wins; the walk exists because for a billion knobs there is no formula. Keep the table — point, slope, new point, \(J\) — and check it against the closed form: the gap to \(w^*\) must shrink by exactly \(1-\eta\lambda\) each row. The slip is evaluating \(J\) at the new \(w\) but writing it in the old row.

Problem 4easy

Two bowls: the circle \(L=x^2+y^2\) and the canyon \(L=x^2+4y^2\). (a) For the circle, write both gradient-descent updates as multiplications by a constant factor and give the \(\eta\) that sends every starting point to \((0,0)\) in one step. (b) For the canyon, write the two factors, find the range of \(\eta\) for which the iteration is stable, and say which coordinate sets it. (c) Trace \(\eta=0.24\) from \((10,10)\) for three steps, with \(L\) at each point. (d) Give the Hessian and the condition number \(\kappa\), and the two per-step factors that explain the trace.

What this tests. The canyon problem in its cleanest form — same convexity, same minimiser, and yet one bowl is easy and the other bounces off the walls. Plan. Gradient, factor per coordinate; the one-step \(\eta\) is where a factor hits \(0\); stability is \(|1-\eta\lambda|<1\) for the larger \(\lambda\); then multiply through a table and read the signs of the two factors.

Show the full solution
(a) Step 1 — the circle. \(\nabla L=(2x,\,2y)\), so \[\begin{aligned} x&\leftarrow x-\eta(2x)=(1-2\eta)\,x,\\ y&\leftarrow y-\eta(2y)=(1-2\eta)\,y. \end{aligned}\] Both coordinates shrink by the same factor. Choose \(\eta=0.5\): \(1-2(0.5)=0\), so \((x,y)\to(0,0)\) in a single step from anywhere. The gradient points straight at the centre because the contours are circles.
(b) Step 2 — the canyon. \(\nabla L=(2x,\,8y)\), so \[\begin{aligned} x&\leftarrow(1-2\eta)\,x,\\ y&\leftarrow(1-8\eta)\,y. \end{aligned}\] Stability needs each factor inside \((-1,1)\): \[\begin{aligned} |1-2\eta|<1&\ \Rightarrow\ 0<\eta<1,\\ |1-8\eta|<1&\ \Rightarrow\ 0<\eta<0.25. \end{aligned}\] The \(y\)-coordinate, with \(\partial^2L/\partial y^2=8\), sets the ceiling \(\eta<0.25\) — even though \(x\) would happily accept a stride four times longer.
(c) Step 3 — trace \(\eta=0.24\). Factors: \(x\): \(1-0.48=0.52\); \(y\): \(1-1.92=-0.92\).

(d) Step 4 — Hessian, \(\kappa\), and what the trace shows. \[H=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&8\end{bmatrix},\qquad\kappa=\frac{8}{2}=4.\] In three steps \(x\) has gone \(10\to1.4\) (factor \(0.52\) each time, monotone); \(y\) has gone \(10\to-9.2\to8.5\to-7.8\), flipping sign every step and losing only \(8\%\) of its size each time (factor \(-0.92\)). Most of the motion is sideways across the canyon, not down it — \(L\) fell only from \(500\) to \(244.5\).

(c) \((5.2,-9.2)\), \((2.704,8.464)\), \((1.406,-7.787)\); \(L=500,365.6,293.9,244.5\). (d) \(H=\operatorname{diag}(2,8)\), \(\kappa=4\); factors \(0.52\) and \(-0.92\).

Remember

Everything about the canyon is in two numbers, the per-direction factors \(1-\eta\lambda_i\): the largest \(\lambda\) caps \(\eta\) at \(2/\lambda_{\max}\), a negative factor means zig-zag, and \(\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) is the difficulty score. Answer "why is the canyon slow?" with the two factors, not with prose.

Problem 5medium

House price \(y\) is modelled as \(\hat y=w_1x_1+w_2x_2\) from area \(x_1=(1000,1500,2000)\) and bedroom count \(x_2=(2,3,3)\), with \(y=(50,75,90)\) and \(J(\mathbf w)=\sum_i(w_1x_{i1}+w_2x_{i2}-y_i)^2\). (a) Compute \(\partial J/\partial w_1\) and \(\partial J/\partial w_2\) at \(\mathbf w=(0,0)\), and their ratio. (b) Standardise both columns (population \(\sigma\)) and recompute both partials at \(\mathbf w=(0,0)\). (c) Give the new ratio.

What this tests. Knobs and units: the feature scale multiplies straight into the gradient, and standardising both columns brings the two partials to the same order of magnitude. Plan. At \(\mathbf w=0\) every prediction is \(0\), so each partial is \(-2\sum y_ix_{ij}\); compute the two sums; then \(\mu,\sigma,z\) for each column; redo the two sums with \(z\) in place of \(x\); divide.

Show the full solution
(a) Step 1 — raw partials at the origin. With \(r_i=\hat y_i-y_i\), the chain rule gives \(\partial J/\partial w_j=2\sum_ir_ix_{ij}\). At \(\mathbf w=0\) all \(\hat y_i=0\), so \(r_i=-y_i\) and \(\partial J/\partial w_j=-2\sum_iy_ix_{ij}\): \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial J}{\partial w_1}&=-2\big[1000(50)+1500(75)\\ &\qquad+2000(90)\big]\\ &=-2\,[50\,000+112\,500+180\,000]\\ &=-2\,(342\,500)=-685\,000,\\ \frac{\partial J}{\partial w_2}&=-2\big[2(50)+3(75)+3(90)\big]\\ &=-2\,[100+225+270]\\ &=-2\,(595)=-1190. \end{aligned}\] Ratio: \[\frac{685\,000}{1190}\approx576.\] The bill is about \(576\) times more sensitive to \(w_1\), purely because square feet are numerically huge.
(b) Step 2 — standardise \(x_1\). \[\begin{aligned} \mu_1&=\frac{1000+1500+2000}{3}=1500,\\ x_1-\mu_1&=(-500,\,0,\,500),\\ \sigma_1&=\sqrt{\frac{250\,000+0+250\,000}{3}}\\ &=\sqrt{166\,667}\approx408.25,\\ z_1&=(-1.2247,\,0,\,1.2247). \end{aligned}\]
Step 3 — standardise \(x_2\). \[\begin{aligned} \mu_2&=\frac{2+3+3}{3}=\frac{8}{3}\approx2.667,\\ x_2-\mu_2&=(-0.667,\,0.333,\,0.333),\\ \sigma_2&=\sqrt{\frac{0.4444+0.1111+0.1111}{3}}\\ &=\sqrt{0.2222}\approx0.4714,\\ z_2&=(-1.4142,\,0.7071,\,0.7071). \end{aligned}\]
Step 4 — the partials again, with \(z\) in place of \(x\). \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial J}{\partial w_1}&=-2\big[(-1.2247)(50)+0(75)\\ &\qquad+(1.2247)(90)\big]\\ &=-2\,[-61.24+0+110.22]\\ &=-2\,(48.99)=-97.98,\\ \frac{\partial J}{\partial w_2}&=-2\big[(-1.4142)(50)+(0.7071)(75)\\ &\qquad+(0.7071)(90)\big]\\ &=-2\,[-70.71+53.03+63.64]\\ &=-2\,(45.96)=-91.92. \end{aligned}\]
(c) Step 5 — the new ratio. \[\frac{97.98}{91.92}\approx1.07.\] From \(576\) to \(1.07\): the two knobs now feel the same stride in the same way, so one \(\gamma\) drives both at similar speeds — no oscillation in \(w_1\) while \(w_2\) crawls.

(a) \(-685\,000\) and \(-1190\), ratio \(\approx576\). (b) \(z_1=(-1.2247,0,1.2247)\), \(z_2=(-1.4142,0.7071,0.7071)\); partials \(-97.98\) and \(-91.92\). (c) ratio \(\approx1.07\).

Remember

At \(\mathbf w=0\) the gradient is \(-2\sum y_ix_{\cdot j}\), so a column's scale multiplies straight into its partial. Quote the before/after ratio — \(576\to1.07\) — as the whole result. The slip is using the sample \(\sigma\) (dividing by \(n-1\)) when the population \(\sigma\) was asked for; the \(z\)'s then change by \(\sqrt{2/3}\).

Problem 6medium

Consider \(L(x,y)=9x^2+y^2\). (a) Write the gradient-descent updates as multiplications by constant factors and find the exact range of \(\eta\) for convergence, naming the coordinate that sets it. (b) With \(\eta=0.1\), trace three steps from \((1,3)\); say which coordinate oscillates and give the threshold of \(\eta\) above which it does. (c) Give the Hessian and \(\kappa\); give the \(\eta\) that finishes in one step on the equal-curvature bowl \(9x^2+9y^2\), and the two values of \(\eta\) that one-step convergence would need simultaneously on \(L\).

What this tests. The canyon again with the stiff direction along \(x\) this time — checking you read the factors from the curvatures and not from habit. Plan. \(\nabla L=(18x,2y)\); factors \(1-18\eta\), \(1-2\eta\); stability from the larger curvature; multiply through three rows; then \(\kappa=18/2\) and the one-step condition \(1-\eta\lambda=0\).

Show the full solution
(a) Step 1 — decoupled updates. \(\nabla L=(18x,\,2y)\), so \[\begin{aligned} x_{k+1}&=(1-18\eta)\,x_k,\\ y_{k+1}&=(1-2\eta)\,y_k. \end{aligned}\] Stability needs both factors inside \((-1,1)\): \[\begin{aligned} |1-18\eta|<1&\ \Rightarrow\ \eta<\tfrac{2}{18}=\tfrac{1}{9},\\ |1-2\eta|<1&\ \Rightarrow\ \eta<1. \end{aligned}\] The stiff direction is \(x\) (curvature \(18\)), so the range is \[0<\eta<\tfrac19\approx0.111.\]
(b) Step 2 — trace \(\eta=0.1\). Factors: \(x\): \(1-1.8=-0.8\); \(y\): \(1-0.2=0.8\).

Remember

Three thresholds per direction: \(\eta<1/\lambda\) smooth, \(\eta=1/\lambda\) one-step, \(1/\lambda<\eta<2/\lambda\) zig-zag, beyond that blow-up. The largest \(\lambda\) owns the speed limit, the smallest owns the crawl, and \(\kappa\) is their ratio. The slip is putting the stiff factor on \(y\) out of habit — read it from the coefficient.

Problem 7medium

For the least-squares bill \(J(\mathbf w)=\sum_{i=1}^n(\mathbf w^{\top}X_i-y_i)^2\): (a) derive \(\nabla J(\mathbf w)=2\sum_i(\mathbf w^{\top}X_i-y_i)\,X_i\). (b) For \(X_1=(2,1)\), \(y_1=4\), \(X_2=(1,3)\), \(y_2=5\), compute \(J\) and \(\nabla J\) at \(\mathbf w_0=(0,0)\), take one step with \(\gamma=0.02\), and compute the new residuals and \(J_1\); give the fraction of the loss removed.

What this tests. The engine behind every gradient in this unit — residual times feature vector, summed — derived once and then run once. Plan. Name the residual \(r_i\), differentiate the square by the chain rule, note \(\nabla r_i=X_i\); then residuals at the origin, two scaled vectors added, one update, two new residuals, compare.

Show the full solution
(a) Step 1 — the chain rule. Write \(J=\sum_ir_i^2\) with \(r_i=\mathbf w^{\top}X_i-y_i\). Since \(\mathbf w^{\top}X_i\) is linear in \(\mathbf w\), its gradient is the vector \(X_i\), and \(y_i\) is a constant: \[\nabla_{\mathbf w}r_i=X_i.\] Differentiate each square: \[\begin{aligned} \nabla J&=\sum_i2r_i\,\nabla_{\mathbf w}r_i\\ &=2\sum_i(\mathbf w^{\top}X_i-y_i)\,X_i. \end{aligned}\] Reading: each row pulls \(\mathbf w\) along its own feature vector, in proportion to its current error.

Column 1: \(\min=100\), range \(800\); column 2: \(\min=0.3\), range \(0.6\): \[\begin{aligned} x_1'&=\Big(\tfrac{0}{800},\tfrac{400}{800},\tfrac{800}{800}\Big)=(0,\,0.5,\,1),\\ x_2'&=\Big(\tfrac{0.6}{0.6},\tfrac{0}{0.6},\tfrac{0.3}{0.6}\Big)=(1,\,0,\,0.5). \end{aligned}\]
Step 4 — the new Hessian. \[\begin{aligned} \textstyle\sum x_1'^2&=0+0.25+1=1.25,\\ \textstyle\sum x_1'x_2'&=0+0+0.5=0.5,\\ \textstyle\sum x_2'^2&=1+0+0.25=1.25. \end{aligned}\] \[H'=2\begin{bmatrix}1.25&0.5\\0.5&1.25\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2.5&1\\1&2.5\end{bmatrix}.\] Equal diagonal, so \(\lambda=a\pm b\): \[\begin{aligned} \lambda&=2.5\pm1=3.5,\ 1.5,\\ \gamma'_{\max}&=\frac{2}{3.5}\approx0.571,\\ \kappa'&=\frac{3.5}{1.5}\approx2.33. \end{aligned}\]
(c) Step 5 — the improvement. \[\frac{\gamma'_{\max}}{\gamma_{\max}}=\frac{0.571}{9.3\times10^{-7}}\approx6\times10^5.\] The stride grew by roughly six orders of magnitude, and \(\kappa\) fell from \(\sim10^6\) to \(2.33\) — the quantitative content of "scale your features before descending".

(a) \(H=\begin{bmatrix}2.14\times10^6&1560\\1560&2.52\end{bmatrix}\); \(\lambda_{\max}\approx2.14\times10^6\), \(\lambda_{\min}\approx1.38\); \(\gamma_{\max}\approx9.3\times10^{-7}\); \(\kappa\approx1.6\times10^6\). (b) \(x_1'=(0,0.5,1)\), \(x_2'=(1,0,0.5)\); \(H'=\begin{bmatrix}2.5&1\\1&2.5\end{bmatrix}\); \(\lambda=3.5,1.5\); \(\gamma'_{\max}\approx0.571\); \(\kappa'\approx2.33\). (c) \(\approx6\times10^5\).

Remember

Feature scales enter \(H\) squared, so a \(10^3\) scale gap is a \(10^6\) curvature gap and a \(10^{-6}\) speed limit. Scaling is not cosmetic; it is a computable \(10^5\)–\(10^6\times\) speed-up. For a nearly diagonal \(2\times2\), \(\lambda_{\max}\approx\) the big diagonal entry and \(\lambda_{\min}=\det/\lambda_{\max}\) — never read \(\lambda_{\min}\) off the small diagonal entry (\(2.52\), not \(1.38\)).
