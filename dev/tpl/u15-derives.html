Prove it · sigmoid's slope is never more than ¼

Claim. For \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\), the slope is \(\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)\,(1-\sigma(z))\), and it is at most \(\tfrac14\), reached only at \(z=0\).

1
Write \(\sigma=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}\) and use the chain rule: \[\begin{aligned}\sigma'(z)&=\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2}\\ &=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}.\end{aligned}\] The derivative of \(u^{-1}\) is \(-u^{-2}u'\), and \(u'=-e^{-z}\).
2
The second factor is \(1-\sigma\), because \(\dfrac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}=1-\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\). So \(\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\). A handy form: the slope comes straight from the value you already stored on the way forward.
3
Put \(s=\sigma\in(0,1)\). Then \(s(1-s)=\tfrac14-(s-\tfrac12)^2\le\tfrac14\), with equality only at \(s=\tfrac12\), that is \(z=0\). ∎ Example: at \(z=2\), \(\sigma\approx0.881\) and the slope is \(0.881\times0.119\approx0.105\).
Prove it · a straight step after a straight step is one straight step

Claim. If \(f(\mathbf x)=W_1\mathbf x+\mathbf b_1\) and \(g(\mathbf u)=W_2\mathbf u+\mathbf b_2\), then \(g(f(\mathbf x))=W\mathbf x+\mathbf b\) with \(W=W_2W_1\) and \(\mathbf b=W_2\mathbf b_1+\mathbf b_2\).

1
Substitute \(f\) into \(g\): \[\begin{aligned}&g(f(\mathbf x))\\ &=W_2(W_1\mathbf x+\mathbf b_1)+\mathbf b_2.\end{aligned}\] The output of the first layer is the input of the second.
2
Multiplying by a matrix spreads over a sum (it is a linear machine, Unit 1): \[\begin{aligned}&W_2(W_1\mathbf x+\mathbf b_1)+\mathbf b_2\\ &=W_2W_1\mathbf x+W_2\mathbf b_1+\mathbf b_2\\ &=(W_2W_1)\mathbf x\\ &\quad+(W_2\mathbf b_1+\mathbf b_2).\end{aligned}\] ∎ With the numbers of §3: \(W_2W_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 2&5\end{pmatrix}\) and \(W_2\mathbf b_1+\mathbf b_2=(1,3)\).
Prove it · a hundred straight layers are still one

Claim. Any stack of \(n\) layers without activations, \(\mathbf x\mapsto W_n(\cdots(W_1\mathbf x+\mathbf b_1)\cdots)+\mathbf b_n\), equals one layer \(\mathbf x\mapsto M\mathbf x+\mathbf c\).

1
For \(n=1\) it is already one layer. Suppose the first \(n-1\) layers equal \(M'\mathbf x+\mathbf c'\). This is a proof by walking up one layer at a time (induction).
2
Add layer \(n\) on top and use the previous proof: \[\begin{aligned}&W_n(M'\mathbf x+\mathbf c')+\mathbf b_n\\ &=(W_nM')\,\mathbf x\\ &\quad+(W_n\mathbf c'+\mathbf b_n).\end{aligned}\] So \(M=W_n\cdots W_2W_1\) and the shifts collect into one vector \(\mathbf c\). ∎ The consequence: without bends, every boundary the network can draw is a straight line (a flat plane, in more dimensions) — exactly what one layer could draw.
Prove it · three ReLUs make a hat

Claim. \(h(x)=\operatorname{ReLU}(x)-2\operatorname{ReLU}(x-1)+\operatorname{ReLU}(x-2)\) equals \(0\) for \(x\le0\), \(x\) on \([0,1]\), \(2-x\) on \([1,2]\), and \(0\) for \(x\ge2\).

1
Each ReLU is \(0\) to the left of its crease and the straight line \(x-c\) to the right. So just count which creases are behind you. The creases sit at 0, 1 and 2.
2
Go piece by piece: \[\begin{aligned}x\le0:&\quad 0\\ 0\le x\le1:&\quad x\\ 1\le x\le2:&\quad 2-x\\ x\ge2:&\quad 0\end{aligned}\] ∎ For example, on \([1,2]\): \(x-2(x-1)=2-x\); and for \(x\ge2\): \(x-2(x-1)+(x-2)=0\). The weights \(1,-2,1\) are the changes of slope at each crease: slope goes \(0\to1\), then \(1\to-1\) (a change of \(-2\)), then \(-1\to0\).
Prove it · k tent folds make 2ᵏ pieces

Claim. Let \(t(x)=1-|2x-1|\) on \([0,1]\) (one tent, made from two ReLUs: \(t(x)=2\operatorname{ReLU}(x)-4\operatorname{ReLU}(x-\tfrac12)\)). Then \(t\) applied \(k\) times, \(t^{(k)}\), has exactly \(2^k\) straight pieces, and each piece runs all the way from 0 to 1 or from 1 to 0.

1
For \(k=1\): \(t\) rises from 0 to 1 on \([0,\tfrac12]\) and falls back on \([\tfrac12,1]\). Two pieces, each covering all of \([0,1]\). Check the ReLU form: for \(x\le\tfrac12\) it is \(2x\); for \(x\ge\tfrac12\) it is \(2x-4(x-\tfrac12)=2-2x\).
2
Suppose \(t^{(k-1)}\) has \(2^{k-1}\) pieces, each sweeping across all of \([0,1]\). Applying \(t\) on top of one such sweep folds it once: the sweep passes \(\tfrac12\) exactly once, so it becomes 2 pieces, each again sweeping all of \([0,1]\). A tent turns any full sweep into an up-and-down.
3
So every piece doubles: \(t^{(k)}\) has \(2\cdot2^{k-1}=2^k\) pieces. ∎ Cost: \(2k\) ReLUs in \(k\) layers. A single layer makes at most one new crease per neuron, so it needs about \(2^k\) neurons for the same zig-zag. Depth is exponentially cheaper here.
Prove it · the ½ is there to cancel the 2

Claim. For \(L=\tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2\), the output gradient is \(\partial L/\partial\hat y=\hat y-y\). And minimising \(L\) is maximising a Gaussian likelihood.

1
The chain rule on a square: \(\dfrac{d}{d\hat y}\,\tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2=\tfrac12\cdot2(\hat y-y)\cdot1=\hat y-y.\) Without the ½ every gradient in the network would simply be twice as big — the same direction, a different step size.
2
If the truth is \(y=\hat y+\) Gaussian noise of spread \(\sigma\), the likelihood of one example is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}e^{-(y-\hat y)^2/2\sigma^2}\), so \[\begin{aligned}&-\log(\text{likelihood})\\ &=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\cdot\tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2+\text{const}.\end{aligned}\] ∎ So squared error is the negative log-likelihood of Gaussian noise, exactly as Unit 14 showed.
Prove it · softmax + cross-entropy gives q − y

Claim. With \(q_i=e^{z_i}/\sum_je^{z_j}\) and true class \(c\), the loss \(L=-\log q_c\) has \(\partial L/\partial z_i=q_i-y_i\), where \(y\) is the one-hot label.

1
Open the log: \[\begin{aligned}L&=-\log\frac{e^{z_c}}{\sum_je^{z_j}}\\ &=-z_c+\log\sum_je^{z_j}.\end{aligned}\] The log of a fraction is the top minus the bottom.
2
Differentiate by \(z_i\). The first term gives \(-1\) if \(i=c\) and \(0\) otherwise — that is \(-y_i\). The second gives \(e^{z_i}/\sum_je^{z_j}=q_i\): \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i}=q_i-y_i.\] ∎ Scores \((2,1,0)\), true class 1: \(q-y=(-0.335,\,0.245,\,0.090)\). The entries add to 0 because both \(q\) and \(y\) add to 1. Unit 14 has the full story.
Prove it · Rule A, the switch

Claim. Across \(\mathbf a=\operatorname{ReLU}(\mathbf z)\) (entry by entry), \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z}=\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf a}\odot\operatorname{ReLU}'(\mathbf z)\).

1
\(a_j=\operatorname{ReLU}(z_j)\) depends on \(z_j\) and on nothing else. So \(z_j\) reaches the loss only through \(a_j\) — one path, one term in the chain rule (Unit 7): \[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j}&=\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_j}\cdot\frac{\partial a_j}{\partial z_j}\\ &=\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_j}\cdot\operatorname{ReLU}'(z_j).\end{aligned}\] No sum, because \(z_j\) does not feed any other \(a_k\).
2
Do it for every \(j\) at once: multiply the two lists entry by entry. That is what \(\odot\) means. ∎ \(\operatorname{ReLU}'(z)\) is 1 for \(z>0\) and 0 for \(z<0\): a switch. (At exactly \(z=0\) we pick 0, a common convention.)
Prove it · Rule B, parts (i) and (ii): the weights and the shift

Claim. Across \(\mathbf z=W\mathbf a+\mathbf b\): \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial W_{jk}}=\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z_j}\,a_k\), so \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial W}=\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z}\,\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}\); and \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf b}=\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z}\).

1
Row \(j\) of the arrow says \(z_j=\sum_kW_{jk}a_k+b_j\). The number \(W_{jk}\) appears in \(z_j\) only, multiplied by \(a_k\). So \(\partial z_j/\partial W_{jk}=a_k\), and one chain-rule term: \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{jk}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j}\,a_k.\] "A weight's blame = the blame at its output × what flowed in through it."
2
Entry \((j,k)\) is (entry \(j\) of a column) × (entry \(k\) of a row). A grid built like that is an outer product: \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z}\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}\). Shapes: \((n\times1)(1\times m)=n\times m\), the shape of \(W\).
3
\(b_j\) also sits only in \(z_j\), with coefficient 1: \(\partial L/\partial b_j=\partial L/\partial z_j\). ∎ Salary example, layer 3: \((-10)(6,7)=(-60,-70)\), and \(\partial L/\partial b^{(3)}=-10\).
Prove it · Rule B, part (iii): blame for the layer below is Wᵀ times blame

Claim. \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf a}=W^{\mathsf T}\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z}\).

1
Now \(a_k\) sits inside every \(z_j\) (each row uses every input). So \(a_k\) reaches the loss along many paths, and the chain rule adds them up: \[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_k}&=\sum_j\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j}\,\frac{\partial z_j}{\partial a_k}\\ &=\sum_j\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j}\,W_{jk}.\end{aligned}\] Many paths means a sum — this is the "add the blame from every road" rule of Unit 7.
2
The sum runs down column \(k\) of \(W\). Column \(k\) of \(W\) is row \(k\) of \(W^{\mathsf T}\). So the whole list is \(W^{\mathsf T}\,\partial L/\partial\mathbf z\). ∎ Salary example, layer 2: \(W^{(2)\mathsf T}(-40,-20)=(-60,\,10,\,-20)\). Forward you multiply by \(W\); backward by \(W^{\mathsf T}\) — the same wires, walked the other way.
Prove it · the whole backward pass, as four formulas with shapes

Claim. For a network \(\mathbf z^{(l)}=W^{(l)}\mathbf a^{(l-1)}+\mathbf b^{(l)}\), \(\mathbf a^{(l)}=\operatorname{ReLU}(\mathbf z^{(l)})\), with \(L=\tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2\), the gradients of all parameters come from one backward sweep:

1
Start: \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\hat y}=\hat y-y\) (a \(1\times1\)). The seed. For a softmax head it is \(\mathbf q-\mathbf y\) instead, with the same meaning.
2
Then, for \(l=L,L-1,\dots,1\): \[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z^{(l)}}&=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf a^{(l)}}\\ &\quad\odot\operatorname{ReLU}'(\mathbf z^{(l)})\\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(l)}}&=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z^{(l)}}\,\mathbf a^{(l-1)\mathsf T}\\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf b^{(l)}}&=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z^{(l)}}\\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf a^{(l-1)}}&=W^{(l)\mathsf T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z^{(l)}}\end{aligned}\] Shapes, line by line: \(n_l\times1\), \(n_l\times n_{l-1}\), \(n_l\times1\), \(n_{l-1}\times1\) (\(n_l\) = neurons in layer \(l\)). Rule A, then Rule B's three results. The top of the output layer uses \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf a^{(L)}=\partial L/\partial\hat y\).
3
Each line needs only things already known: the stored \(\mathbf z^{(l)}\) and \(\mathbf a^{(l-1)}\) from the forward pass, \(W^{(l)}\), and the blame just computed one layer up. So one backward sweep gives every gradient. ∎ This is why the forward pass must keep its notes (the cache): the backward pass reads them in reverse order.
Prove it · the shapes place every transpose

Claim. "The gradient of anything has the shape of that thing" leaves only one way to write each formula.

1
\(\partial L/\partial W^{(2)}\) must be \(2\times3\), like \(W^{(2)}\). The two ingredients are \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf z^{(2)}\) (\(2\times1\)) and \(\mathbf a^{(1)}\) (\(3\times1\)). The only product of these that is \(2\times3\) is \((2\times1)(1\times3)\): \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(2)}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf z^{(2)}}\,\mathbf a^{(1)\mathsf T}.\] \(\mathbf a^{(1)}\,\partial L/\partial\mathbf z^{(2)\mathsf T}\) would be \(3\times2\) — the wrong shape.
2
\(\partial L/\partial\mathbf a^{(1)}\) must be \(3\times1\). From \(W^{(2)}\) (\(2\times3\)) and \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf z^{(2)}\) (\(2\times1\)) the only \(3\times1\) product is \(W^{(2)\mathsf T}\,\partial L/\partial\mathbf z^{(2)}\): \((3\times2)(2\times1)\). ∎ A shape check is not a proof of the formula — the proofs in §7 are — but it catches almost every slip.
Prove it · why a central difference is so accurate

Claim. \(\dfrac{L(\theta+h)-L(\theta-h)}{2h}=L'(\theta)+O(h^2)\), while the one-sided \(\dfrac{L(\theta+h)-L(\theta)}{h}=L'(\theta)+O(h)\).

1
Taylor (Unit 8) on both sides: \[\begin{aligned}L(\theta\pm h)&=L\pm hL'\\ &\quad+\tfrac{h^2}{2}L''\\ &\quad\pm\tfrac{h^3}{6}L'''+\cdots\end{aligned}\] Everything evaluated at \(\theta\).
2
Subtract: the even terms cancel, so \(L(\theta+h)-L(\theta-h)=2hL'+\tfrac{h^3}{3}L'''+\cdots\). Divide by \(2h\): the error is \(\tfrac{h^2}{6}L'''\), tiny when \(h\) is small. ∎ For the salary network with \(h=0.001\) on \(W^{(1)}_{11}\): \((48.2162-51.8162)/0.002=-1800\), matching backprop. When a check disagrees, the backward pass has a bug.
Prove it · big inputs make big first-layer gradients

Claim. Scaling input \(x_k\) by a factor \(s\) (and \(W^{(1)}_{jk}\) by \(1/s\), so the network computes the same thing) scales \(\partial L/\partial W^{(1)}_{jk}\) by \(s\) — and the safe step size for that weight by about \(1/s^2\).

1
Rule B: \(\partial L/\partial W^{(1)}_{jk}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial z^{(1)}_j}\,x_k\). The first factor does not change (the network's outputs are the same), so the gradient is proportional to \(x_k\). Age = 30 is why \(\partial L/\partial W^{(1)}_{11}=-60\times30=-1800\).
2
A step \(\Delta W_{jk}=-\eta\,\partial L/\partial W_{jk}\) changes \(z_j\) by \(\Delta W_{jk}\,x_k=-\eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j}x_k^2\). So the effect of one step grows like \(x_k^2\), and a step size that is safe must shrink like \(1/x_k^2\). ∎ That is the canyon of Unit 10: one very steep direction forces a tiny \(\eta\) on all of them. The cure is to scale the inputs (Unit 10, §5).
Prove it · how fast blame can fade through sigmoid layers

Claim. In a chain of \(k\) one-neuron sigmoid layers \(a_l=\sigma(w_la_{l-1}+b_l)\), \(\left|\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial a_0}\right|\le\left|\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial a_k}\right|\prod_{l=1}^k\frac{|w_l|}{4}\). With \(|w_l|\le1\) the blame shrinks at least by \(4^{-k}\).

1
Crossing layer \(l\) backwards multiplies the blame by the local slope: Rule A gives \(\sigma'(z_l)\le\tfrac14\), Rule B gives \(w_l\). So \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_{l-1}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_l}\,\sigma'(z_l)\,w_l.\] The §2 drawer proved \(\sigma'\le\tfrac14\).
2
Multiply the \(k\) crossings together and bound each slope by \(\tfrac14\). ∎ \(0.25^5\approx0.00098\) and \(0.25^{10}\approx9.5\times10^{-7}\). With ReLU the slope is exactly 1 on awake neurons, so nothing shrinks there; with weights bigger than 4 the product can grow instead — exploding blame.
