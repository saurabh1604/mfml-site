### Act II — Guessing the next word by counting
**s2 · A sentence is a chain of guesses.** New framing. Chain rule of probability: P(w₁…wₙ) = P(w₁)·P(w₂|w₁)·P(w₃|w₁w₂)… — "a sentence is a string of guesses, each allowed to use everything before it." Too much history to count → **Markov assumption**: keep only the last N−1 words ("a guesser with a short memory"; bigram N = 2, trigram N = 3). Counting = maximum likelihood (Unit 14's coin: heads/tosses). Corpus with sentence marks:
`<s> I drink chai </s>` · `<s> I drink coffee </s>` · `<s> I drink chai </s>` · `<s> I play cricket </s>`.
P(I|<s>) = 4/4, P(drink|I) = 3/4, P(chai|drink) = 2/3, P(</s>|chai) = 2/2 ⇒ P(<s> I drink chai </s>) = 1·¾·⅔·1 = **1/2**. Generating text = rolling a loaded die from the current word's row (Shannon's game). Why the `</s>` matters: it lets the model decide when a sentence ends, and makes probabilities of all sentences add to 1. Trap: a bigram model has no idea what came two words back ("the train to Delhi from platform four is ___" — it sees only "is"). Widget: **w-bigram** (keep; update to `<s>`/`</s>`; show the chain of guesses as a highlighted path through the table with the product building up; add a "memory" selector bigram/trigram that highlights which previous words the guesser may use; keep "write a sentence").

**s3 · Never seen is not impossible: smoothing.** Anchor: a new chaiwala opens in town — you have never bought from him, but that does not make it impossible. Add-one ("one free ticket to every pair"): P(chai|drink) = (2+1)/(3+7) = **0.3**, P(cricket|drink) = 1/10 = **0.1** (V = 7 next-word types: I, drink, play, chai, coffee, cricket, </s>). **Trap:** with a real vocabulary add-one steals almost everything from what you saw: with V = 50 000, P(chai|drink) = 3/50 003 ≈ **0.00006** — the two real sightings are drowned. Fixes: add-k with small k; **interpolation** — ask three advisers (trigram, bigram, unigram) and blend with weights that add to 1; **backoff** — if the specialist has never seen it, ask the generalist. Worked interpolation (λ = 0.8 bigram + 0.2 unigram; 16 tokens after the `<s>` marks: I 4, drink 3, play 1, chai 2, coffee 1, cricket 1, </s> 4): P(chai|drink) = 0.8·⅔ + 0.2·2/16 = **0.5583**, P(cricket|drink) = 0.8·0 + 0.2·1/16 = **0.0125**. Widget (new, 2-D): **w-smooth** — the "drink" row as bars under MLE / add-one / add-k (k slider 0.01–1) / interpolation (λ slider); a V slider (7 → 50 000) showing add-one flattening the row; a "mass moved" meter (how much probability was taken from seen words and given to unseen ones); rows always sum to 1 (test it).

### Act III — Meaning from the company a word keeps
**s5 · You shall know a word by the company it keeps.** Keep "kadak" and Firth. New insight: **window size decides the kind of similarity** — small windows (±1–2) find words that can *replace* each other in a sentence (chai ↔ coffee); large windows (a whole paragraph) find words about the same *topic* (chai ↔ kettle ↔ cup). **s6 · Not all company counts: TF-IDF and PMI.** Anchor: two people seen together at the market — if both go there every day, meeting means nothing; if they rarely go but always meet, it means a lot. **TF-IDF** (term–document): weight = tf × log₁₀(N/df). Three documents: D1 = the×3, chai×2, hot×1; D2 = the×2, cricket×3; D3 = the, chai, cricket. idf: the **0**, chai **0.176**, cricket **0.176**, hot **0.477**. D1: chai 2·0.176 = **0.352**, hot **0.477**, the **0**; D2: cricket **0.528**; D3: chai **0.176**, cricket **0.176**. ("A word in every document says nothing about any one.") **PMI** (word–word): PMI(w, c) = log₂ [P(w, c) / (P(w)·P(c))] — "how many times more often do they meet than strangers would by chance?" Counts (N = 40): chai: hot 8, the 10, match 2; cricket: hot 2, the 10, match 8. PMI(chai, hot) = log₂(0.2/(0.5·0.25)) = log₂1.6 = **0.678**; PMI(chai, the) = log₂(0.25/0.25) = **0**; PMI(chai, match) = log₂(0.05/0.125) = **−1.322** → PPMI = max(PMI, 0) = **0**. Note: raw counts say "the" is chai's biggest neighbour (10); PMI says it tells us nothing. Trap: PMI over-rewards very rare pairs (a pair seen once can look hugely "surprising") — that is why people smooth the context counts (the 0.75 power reappears in s12). Widget (new): **w-pmi** — a count table you can edit → expected-if-strangers table → ratio → log → PPMI heat map; "the" column fading to 0; a TF-IDF tab with the three documents.

### Act IV — Stop counting, start predicting: word2vec
**s8 · From counting to predicting: the word2vec idea.** Anchor: *don't write down the gossip — train a guesser, then read its mind.* The count table is huge and has to be rebuilt when new text arrives; word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013) slides a window over the text and plays a guessing game at every position. Two games: **fill in the blank** (CBOW: neighbours → middle word) and **guess the neighbours** (skip-gram: middle word → each neighbour). Both use the same small network: one-hot word → **input table** W_in (V × d) → a hidden list of d numbers → **output table** W_out (d × V) → softmax over the whole vocabulary. After training, **throw the game away and keep the input table** — its rows are the word vectors. Counting examples: a 6-word sentence with window C = 2 gives CBOW **6** examples (one per position) but skip-gram **18** (centre, neighbour) pairs (2+3+4+4+3+2). Widget: `<!--@W2V window-->` (by 16-w2v): the window slides; each position emits one CBOW example or up to 2C skip-gram pairs; counters. Also draw (static SVG) the shared network picture with shapes V → d → V.

**s9 · CBOW: the committee fills in the blank.** Use the exemplar in `R22-REWORK.md §2` as the basis (expand it into the full rhythm). Toy model (V = 5: we, drink, chai, daily, cricket; d = 2). Input vectors: we (0,1), drink (1,0), chai (1,1), daily (0,1), cricket (−1,0). Output vectors: we (0,0), drink (1,0), chai (1,1), daily (0,1), cricket (−1,−1). Sentence *we drink chai daily*, window 1, centre chai, context {drink, daily}.
h = (0.5, 0.5); scores (0, 0.5, 1, 0.5, −1); softmax **(0.1354, 0.2233, 0.3682, 0.2233, 0.0498)**; loss −ln 0.3682 = **0.9993**; error e = p − y = (0.1354, 0.2233, −0.6318, 0.2233, 0.0498); output update u_w ← u_w − η e_w h; blame for h: EH = Σ e_w u_w = **(−0.4584, −0.4584)**, shared equally: each context vector ← v − η·EH/2. With η = 1: new u_chai = (1.3159, 1.3159), new v_drink = (1.2292, 0.2292), new v_daily = (0.2292, 1.2292); new p(chai) = **0.5942**, loss **0.5205**.
Name explained ("bag" = order inside the window ignored; "continuous" = dense vectors). Checks (conceptual): swap the two neighbours → no change (it's a bag); with window 2 (four neighbours) each neighbour gets a quarter of EH instead of half. Widget: `<!--@W2V cbow-->`.

**s10 · Skip-gram: one word guesses its neighbours.** Anchor: *the new student in class guesses who sits around him* — or: one word throws a guess at each of its neighbours. Same toy model, centre chai, targets drink and daily. h = v_chai = (1, 1) (no averaging — the centre's own vector); scores (0, 1, 2, 1, −2); softmax **(0.0716, 0.1947, 0.5293, 0.1947, 0.0097)** — the *same* distribution is used for every neighbour slot; loss = −ln 0.1947 − ln 0.1947 = **3.2725**; summed error E = 2p − y_drink − y_daily = **(0.1433, −0.6106, 1.0585, −0.6106, 0.0194)**; EH = Σ E_w u_w = **(0.4286, 0.4286)**; with η = 1: new v_chai = **(0.5714, 0.5714)** (chai stops pointing at itself), u_drink = (1.6106, 0.6106), u_daily = (0.6106, 1.6106), u_chai = (−0.0585, −0.0585); new p = (0.0922, **0.3862**, 0.1015, **0.3862**, 0.0339), loss **1.9026**. Two insights to make memorable: (1) one softmax must spread over *all* of a word's usual neighbours, so skip-gram literally learns a word's company as a probability distribution; (2) the centre word's vector receives the **sum** of all its neighbours' errors — a rare word gets strong, undiluted updates every time it appears. Widget: `<!--@W2V skipgram-->`.

**s11 · CBOW or skip-gram? Race them.** Compare the variants (owner's rule). Table: examples per position (1 vs up to 2C), what is averaged (contexts vs nothing), speed (CBOW faster), strengths (CBOW: smoother, slightly better for frequent words; skip-gram: better for rare words and small data — Mikolov et al. 2013), the popular default (skip-gram with negative sampling). Why rare words fare better in skip-gram: in CBOW a rare word is one voice in a committee and its update is divided by the committee size; in skip-gram it is the centre of 2C predictions and gets every update in full. **s12 · Making it cheap: negative sampling, hierarchical softmax, subsampling.** Anchor: a teacher checking who chai's friends are — rank all 50 000 students (softmax), ask five yes/no questions (negative sampling), or play twenty questions down a tree (hierarchical softmax). Keep the negative-sampling worked round (loss **0.948 → 0.505**) and its widget **w-sgstep**, and the unigram^¾ rule (100 vs 1 sightings → 100^0.75 ≈ 31.6 vs 1 → **96.9% vs 3.1%** instead of 99% vs 1%). Keep **w-cost** (fix label overlaps; 50 000 vs 6 → ≈ 8 333× less work). New **hierarchical softmax**: words are leaves of a binary tree; P(word) = product of left/right sigmoid decisions along its path. Four-leaf tree, h = (1, 1), node vectors θ₁ = (0.5, 0.5), θ₂ = (−0.5, 0), θ₃ = (1, −2): P(left at root) = σ(1) = 0.7311; leaves LL **0.2760**, LR **0.4551**, RL **0.0723**, RR **0.1966** — sum exactly 1 without adding anything up; V = 50 000 needs only ⌈log₂ 50 000⌉ = **16** decisions; frequent words get short paths (Huffman). New **subsampling**: each occurrence of a word with frequency f is kept with probability √(t/f), t = 10⁻⁵ (word2vec paper): f = 4×10⁻⁵ → keep **0.5**; "the" with f = 0.05 → keep **0.0141** (1.4%); rare words (f ≤ t) always kept. Why: frequent words teach little and waste time; dropping them also widens the effective window. Widgets: **w-sgstep** (keep), **w-cost** extended into "three ways to make it cheap" (tabs: softmax vs negative sampling / hierarchical-softmax tree with a path lighting up / subsampling stream where frequent words fade).

**s13 · Why it works: counting and predicting meet.** Anchor: two strangers who shop at the same stores end up carrying the same bags — words that keep the same company receive the same pulls, so their vectors converge. Show it (rebuild **w-w2v** into a clear demo: two target words that never appear together but share contexts, their vectors converging while an unrelated word drifts away; declutter the globe — label only the highlighted words, others as dots with hover). The surprise result (Levy & Goldberg, 2014): at its best, skip-gram with negative sampling makes u·v equal PMI(w, c) − ln k — so word2vec is secretly factorising the PMI table of s6; the counting road and the predicting road arrive at the same city. Example: PMI(chai, hot) = ln 1.6 ≈ **0.470** (natural log) → with k = 5 the best u·v = 0.470 − ln 5 ≈ **−1.139**. **GloVe** (Pennington, Socher & Manning, 2014) makes this explicit: meaning lives in *ratios* of co-occurrence probabilities. Toy table (1 000 context tokens each): chai: hot 40, cold 2, drink 60, cricket 4; lassi: hot 2, cold 40, drink 60, cricket 4 → P(k|chai)/P(k|lassi) = hot **20**, cold **0.05**, drink **1**, cricket **1** (ln 20 ≈ **2.996**): words that tell chai and lassi apart give ratios far from 1; words that don't give 1. GloVe fits wᵢ·w̃ⱼ + bᵢ + b̃ⱼ ≈ ln Xᵢⱼ with weight f(x) = (x/100)^¾ below 100 (f(40) ≈ **0.503**, f(2) ≈ **0.053**) — so rare, noisy counts matter less. (The GloVe paper found the same pattern with ice/steam and solid/gas on 6 billion words — mention it in words.) Widget (new): **w-ratio** — pick two target words and a probe word; bars of P(probe|each) and the ratio on a log scale; the difference vector (chai − lassi) drawn next to the probe directions.
