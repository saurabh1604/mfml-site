4

Why we divide by the square root of d

Imagine this

A judge scores a singing contest out of 10. The scores are close, so the prize money is shared fairly. Now a second judge scores the same singers out of 1000. One singer gets 30 points more than the next — and with scores that big, softmax hands that singer all the money.

Nothing about the singers changed. Only the size of the numbers did. The fix is simple: bring the scores back to a sensible size before you share out the money.

A dot product adds up \(d\) little products, one for each entry. More entries means a bigger pile. If the entries of \(\mathbf q\) and \(\mathbf k\) are random numbers of typical size 1, each little product is about size 1 too, and they add up like steps of a drunkard's walk. The pile's typical size — its spread, the standard deviation of Unit 14 — grows like \(\sqrt d\):

\[\begin{aligned}d=2&:\ \sqrt2\approx1.41\\ d=64&:\ \sqrt{64}=8\\ d=512&:\ \sqrt{512}\approx22.6\end{aligned}\]

Why is that bad? Softmax only cares about gaps between scores. Take two scores 8 and 0, a normal gap when \(d=64\). Softmax gives

\[\Big(\frac{e^8}{e^8+1},\ \frac{1}{e^8+1}\Big)\approx(0.99966,\ 0.00034).\]

That is nearly one-hot: the word listens to one other word and ignores the rest. Worse, the row is stuck. The slope of a share \(p\) is \(p(1-p)\approx0.00034\), almost zero, so training gets almost no blame through it (the fading blame of Unit 15).

Now divide by \(\sqrt{64}=8\) first. The scores become 1 and 0, and softmax gives \((0.731,\ 0.269)\). The slope is \(0.731\times0.269\approx0.197\) — nearly 600 times bigger. Dividing by \(\sqrt d\) brings the spread back to about 1, whatever \(d\) is.

Try: Keep divide by √d off and drag \(d\) from 1 to 512: the cloud of scores spreads wider and the shares on the right turn into one tall bar. Then switch divide by √d on: the cloud stays the same width for every \(d\), and the shares stay soft.

The realization

\[\begin{gathered}\operatorname{spread}(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k)=\sqrt d\\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{use }\frac{\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k}{\sqrt d}\end{gathered}\]

Long vectors make big dot products, and big scores make softmax pick one winner and freeze. Dividing every score by \(\sqrt d\) keeps the scores at a spread of about 1, so the shares stay soft and blame keeps flowing. It is the same spirit as putting inputs on one scale in Unit 10.

In one sentence: A dot product of two random length-\(d\) vectors has spread \(\sqrt d\), which makes softmax spiky and frozen, so attention divides every score by \(\sqrt d\) to bring the spread back to about 1.

The attention map: who looks at whom

Imagine this

Read this: "The batsman hit the ball because it was loose." What was loose? You did not stop to think. You knew "it" meant the ball, not the batsman. Somewhere in your head, the word "it" reached back and grabbed "ball".

That grab is exactly what attention does. And we can draw it.

Give every word of the sentence its query, key and value, and work out every row of the attention matrix. With 9 words you get a \(9\times9\) table. Row "it" says how much "it" listens to each word. Draw the table as a grid of squares, brighter where the share is bigger: that is an attention map.

The lab below does exactly the maths of §3, for any short sentence you type. Its word vectors are toy ones, made by hand — not taken from a trained model. Each word is a list of 12 numbers. Eight of them are labelled features you can read: thing, someone, action, "it"-word, "he/she"-word, describing word, place, small linking word. The other four say where the word stands (§7). The matrices \(W_Q, W_K, W_V\) are hand-set too, so you can see why each share comes out as it does. But the arithmetic — dot products, \(\sqrt d\), softmax, the blend — is exactly the real one.

In the lab, head 1 is set up so that an "it"-word asks "which word is a thing?", and every thing answers "I am a thing". So row "it" gives "ball" a share of about 0.766, and each of the other eight words about 0.029.

After attention, a word's vector depends on its context.

The realization

\[\mathbf x_i\ \longrightarrow\ \mathbf x_i+\sum_j A_{ij}\,\mathbf v_j\]

The attention map is a table of who listens to whom. Each row adds up to 1. After attention each word's vector is its old self plus a blend of what the words it listened to were carrying. So "it" in "the ball … it was loose" ends up close to "ball". A fixed word vector becomes a vector that knows its sentence.

In one sentence: The attention map shows, row by row, how much each word listens to every other word, and adding the blended values to each word turns fixed word vectors into vectors that depend on the sentence around them.

6

Many heads: many questions at once

Imagine this

A cricket commentary box has several experts. One watches the batsman's feet. One watches the field placing. One keeps the score. They all watch the same ball, but each asks a different question, and together they tell the full story.

One attention can ask only one kind of question at a time. "Which thing does 'it' mean?" is one question. "What word came just before me?" is another. So a transformer runs several attentions side by side. Each one is called a head.

Here is the trick that keeps it cheap. Do not give each head the full length \(d\). Cut it into \(h\) equal pieces. Each head gets its own small \(W_Q, W_K, W_V\) that make queries, keys and values of length

\[d_k=\frac{d}{h}.\]

Tiny numbers. A common size is \(d=512\) with \(h=8\) heads, so each head works with lists of length \(512/8=64\). Each head builds its own attention map. The eight answers, each of length 64, are glued side by side back into one list of length \(8\times64=512\). A last matrix \(W_O\) (size \(512\times512\)) mixes them.

The cost is the same as one big head. One big head needs three \(512\times512\) matrices. Eight small heads need \(8\times3\) matrices of size \(512\times64\), which is exactly the same number of weights. Add \(W_O\) and either way attention has \(4d^2=4\times512^2=1\,048\,576\) weights.

Each head looks at the words through its own pair of glasses: its matrices keep only some directions of the word vectors and throw away the rest. That is the projection of Unit 12. Different glasses, different neighbours.

The realization

\[\begin{gathered}\operatorname{head}_i=\operatorname{Attention}(Q_i,K_i,V_i)\\ Q_i=XW_Q^{(i)},\ K_i=XW_K^{(i)},\\ V_i=XW_V^{(i)}\\ \operatorname{MultiHead}\\ =\big[\operatorname{head}_1,\dots,\operatorname{head}_h\big]\,W_O\end{gathered}\]

Many heads are many small attentions, each looking at the words through its own projection — its own subspace. Cut \(d\) into \(h\) pieces of size \(d/h\), let each head build its own map, glue the answers back and mix them with \(W_O\). You get \(h\) different questions for the price of one.
