4

The rule — step against the gradient

Back in the fog

Feel the tilt. Turn to face straight downhill. Take a step whose length is proportional to how steep it is — a big step on a steep slope, a shy one when the ground is nearly level. Stop, feel, repeat. Written as a formula, the hiker's whole strategy is one line.

Gradient descent

From the current point \(\mathbf x_i\), move to

\[\mathbf x_{i+1}=\mathbf x_i-\gamma_i\,\big(\nabla f(\mathbf x_i)\big)^{\!\top}\]

where \(\gamma_i>0\) is the step size (in machine learning, the learning rate). For a suitable step size, \(f(\mathbf x_0)\ge f(\mathbf x_1)\ge f(\mathbf x_2)\ge\cdots\) and the sequence settles into a local minimum.

The little transpose is only bookkeeping: in the lecture's convention the gradient is a row vector and the point is a column, so one of them is flipped to make the subtraction legal. The content is two claims that deserve proof. First, that \(-\nabla f\) really is the steepest way down. Second, that a short step that way really does lower \(f\).

Prove it · the gradient points the steepest way up

Claim. Among all unit directions \(\mathbf u\), the rate of change of \(f\) along \(\mathbf u\) is largest when \(\mathbf u\) points along \(\nabla f\), and that largest rate is \(\|\nabla f\|\).

1
From Unit 6, the rate of change of \(f\) along a unit direction \(\mathbf u\) (the directional derivative) is a dot product: \[D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u.\] Walk a tiny distance \(t\) along \(\mathbf u\); \(f\) changes by about \(t\,\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u\).
2
Cauchy–Schwarz (Unit 3) bounds any dot product by the product of lengths: \[\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u\le\|\nabla f\|\,\|\mathbf u\|=\|\nabla f\|\cdot1.\] A dot product is \(\|\mathbf a\|\|\mathbf b\|\cos\theta\), and \(\cos\theta\le1\).
3
Equality holds exactly when \(\cos\theta=1\), i.e. \(\mathbf u\) points the same way as \(\nabla f\): \(\mathbf u=\nabla f/\|\nabla f\|\). Then \(D_{\mathbf u}f=\|\nabla f\|\), the largest possible. Turning \(\mathbf u\) the opposite way gives \(\cos\theta=-1\) and the most negative rate, \(-\|\nabla f\|\): the steepest descent. ∎ So the uphill compass is not a metaphor. The gradient direction is the unique winner of "which way is steepest", and its length is the steepness.
Prove it · a small step against the gradient lowers f

Claim. If \(\mathbf g=\nabla f(\mathbf x)\neq\mathbf 0\), then for all small enough \(\gamma>0\), \(\ f(\mathbf x-\gamma\mathbf g)

1
Unroll the step into one variable, the line trick from Unit 8: \(h(\gamma)=f(\mathbf x-\gamma\mathbf g)\). Its derivative at \(\gamma=0\) is, by the chain rule, \[h'(0)=\nabla f(\mathbf x)\cdot(-\mathbf g)=-\mathbf g\cdot\mathbf g=-\|\mathbf g\|^2<0.\] Moving along \(-\mathbf g\) means the directional derivative is the gradient dotted with \(-\mathbf g\). A nonzero vector dotted with itself is strictly positive, so with the minus sign the slope of \(h\) at the start is strictly negative.
2
Taylor for \(h\) at \(0\) with the remainder: \[\begin{aligned}h(\gamma)&=h(0)+h'(0)\,\gamma+\tfrac12h''(\xi)\,\gamma^2\\ &=f(\mathbf x)-\gamma\|\mathbf g\|^2+\tfrac12h''(\xi)\,\gamma^2.\end{aligned}\] The value, a drop proportional to \(\gamma\), and a correction proportional to \(\gamma^2\).
3
For small \(\gamma\) the \(\gamma\) term beats the \(\gamma^2\) term: if \(|h''|\le M\) nearby, then \[\begin{aligned}f(\mathbf x-\gamma\mathbf g)&\le f(\mathbf x)-\gamma\|\mathbf g\|^2+\tfrac12M\gamma^2\\ &=f(\mathbf x)-\gamma\Big(\|\mathbf g\|^2-\tfrac12M\gamma\Big),\end{aligned}\] and the bracket is positive as soon as \(\gamma<2\|\mathbf g\|^2/M\). So \(f\) goes down. ∎ Notice what the proof also warns: the guarantee is only for \(\gamma\) small enough, and "small enough" depends on the bend \(M\). Too big a step and the \(\gamma^2\) term wins — the step overshoots and \(f\) can go up. Section 5 turns that warning into an exact number.

Now the lecture's two-variable example, which will be our test valley for the rest of the unit. Take

\[f(\mathbf x)=\tfrac12\mathbf x^{\top}A\mathbf x+\mathbf b^{\top}\mathbf x,\qquad A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&20\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf b=\begin{bmatrix}-5\\-3\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf x_0=\begin{bmatrix}-3\\-1\end{bmatrix}.\]

The lecture quotes the gradient from Unit 6 as the row \(\mathbf x^{\top}A+\mathbf b^{\top}\) — as a column, \(\nabla f=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b\). Since we lean on it for the rest of the unit, here it is in three lines:

Prove it · the gradient of a quadratic bowl is Ax + b

Claim. For symmetric \(A\), \(f(\mathbf x)=\tfrac12\mathbf x^{\top}A\mathbf x+\mathbf b^{\top}\mathbf x\) has \(\nabla f=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b\), and its only flat point is \(\mathbf x^*=-A^{-1}\mathbf b\).

1
Write the two pieces out in coordinates: \[\tfrac12\mathbf x^{\top}A\mathbf x=\tfrac12\sum_{j}\sum_{k}A_{jk}x_jx_k,\qquad \mathbf b^{\top}\mathbf x=\sum_jb_jx_j.\] A quadratic form is a double sum of "entry times two coordinates"; the linear part is a plain dot product.
2
Differentiate with respect to one coordinate \(x_i\). In the double sum, \(x_i\) appears in the terms with \(j=i\) (giving \(\sum_kA_{ik}x_k\)) and in the terms with \(k=i\) (giving \(\sum_jA_{ji}x_j\)); by symmetry these two are equal, so \[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\Big(\tfrac12\mathbf x^{\top}A\mathbf x\Big)&=\tfrac12\Big(\sum_kA_{ik}x_k+\sum_jA_{ji}x_j\Big)\\ &=\sum_kA_{ik}x_k=(A\mathbf x)_i,\\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\big(\mathbf b^{\top}\mathbf x\big)&=b_i.\end{aligned}\] The \(\tfrac12\) in front was put there precisely to cancel the 2 that symmetry produces — that is why the lecture writes the bowl as \(\tfrac12\mathbf x^{\top}A\mathbf x\).
3
Stack the coordinates: \(\nabla f=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b\). Setting it to zero, \(A\mathbf x=-\mathbf b\), so \(\mathbf x^*=-A^{-1}\mathbf b\). For the lecture's numbers, \(A^{-1}=\tfrac1{39}\begin{bmatrix}20&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\) and \(-\mathbf b=(5,3)\), giving \[\begin{aligned}\mathbf x^*&=\tfrac1{39}\begin{bmatrix}100-3\\-5+6\end{bmatrix}=\Big(\tfrac{97}{39},\tfrac1{39}\Big)\\ &\approx(2.487,\,0.026),\qquad f^*\approx-6.256.\end{aligned}\] ∎ So for this valley we know exactly where the walk should end, and can watch it get there — or fail to. Run it:

Two things you cannot miss in the picture. The valley is a trench: the \(20\) in \(A\) makes \(f\) rise steeply as \(y\) moves, while the \(2\) makes it rise gently along \(x\). And the walk zig-zags: it bounces from wall to wall of the trench while creeping along its floor. Section 5 explains both with one number.

In one sentence: \(\mathbf x_{i+1}=\mathbf x_i-\gamma\nabla f(\mathbf x_i)\) — the gradient is provably the steepest way up, a small step the other way provably lowers \(f\), and on a trench-shaped valley the walk zig-zags.
5

Goldilocks — how long a step?

Stairs in the dark

You are going down a staircase with the lights off. Shuffle an inch at a time and you will get there — next week. Leap three steps at a time and you will overshoot a landing, stumble, maybe fall back up a flight. There is a stride that is just right, and it depends on the staircase: steep, narrow stairs demand short strides. Gradient descent has exactly this problem, and for a bowl we can say precisely what "just right" means.

The lecture states the two failure modes: a step size that is too small makes descent painfully slow; one that is too large overshoots, fails to converge, or diverges outright. Let us make that exact on the simplest possible valley, \(f(x)=\tfrac12\lambda x^2\) — a parabola whose steepness is set by the single number \(\lambda>0\). Its gradient is \(f'(x)=\lambda x\), so the update is

\[x_{k+1}=x_k-\gamma\lambda x_k=(1-\gamma\lambda)\,x_k.\]

Every step multiplies the position by the same factor \(1-\gamma\lambda\). That one number decides everything:

Prove it · the speed limit γ < 2/λ

Claim. On \(f=\tfrac12\lambda x^2\), gradient descent converges to \(0\) from any start if and only if \(0<\gamma<2/\lambda\); it lands exactly at \(0\) in one step when \(\gamma=1/\lambda\).

1
Apply the update \(k\) times. The factor is the same each time, so \[x_k=(1-\gamma\lambda)^k\,x_0.\] A geometric sequence: the position after \(k\) steps is the start times the factor to the power \(k\).
2
A number to the power \(k\) goes to \(0\) exactly when its size is below \(1\): \[\begin{aligned}|1-\gamma\lambda|<1&\iff-1<1-\gamma\lambda<1\\ &\iff0<\gamma\lambda<2\\ &\iff0<\gamma<\frac2\lambda.\end{aligned}\] Subtract 1 from all three parts, multiply by \(-1\) (which flips the inequalities), divide by \(\lambda>0\).
3
Read the four regimes off the factor. \(0<1-\gamma\lambda<1\) (\(\gamma<1/\lambda\)): shrinks, same side each time — smooth descent, slow when the factor is near \(1\). \(1-\gamma\lambda=0\) (\(\gamma=1/\lambda\)): \(x_1=0\), done in one step. \(-1<1-\gamma\lambda<0\) (\(1/\lambda<\gamma<2/\lambda\)): shrinks but flips sign — overshoots the bottom every step, still converging. \(|1-\gamma\lambda|\ge1\) (\(\gamma\ge2/\lambda\)): never shrinks; at exactly \(2/\lambda\) it bounces \(\pm x_0\) forever, beyond that each step is bigger than the last. ∎ Steeper valley (bigger \(\lambda\)) means a lower speed limit \(2/\lambda\). That is the dark staircase: steep stairs, short strides.

Now the trench. A bowl in two variables, \(f=\tfrac12(\lambda_1x^2+\lambda_2y^2)\), is two parabolas at once — one steep (\(\lambda_1\)), one gentle (\(\lambda_2\)) — and because the gradient splits, \((\lambda_1x,\lambda_2y)\), the update splits too:

Prove it · each direction has its own factor — the zig-zag explained

Claim. On \(f=\tfrac12(\lambda_1x^2+\lambda_2y^2)\) with \(\lambda_1>\lambda_2>0\), a fixed step \(\gamma\) must satisfy \(\gamma<2/\lambda_1\), and then the \(y\)-direction converges with factor \(1-\gamma\lambda_2\), which is close to \(1\) when \(\lambda_1/\lambda_2\) is large.

1
The gradient is \((\lambda_1x,\ \lambda_2y)\), so the two coordinates update independently: \[x_{k+1}=(1-\gamma\lambda_1)x_k,\qquad y_{k+1}=(1-\gamma\lambda_2)y_k.\] The bowl has no cross-term, so each coordinate lives on its own parabola and gets its own factor.
2
Both factors must have size below \(1\). The stricter demand comes from the larger \(\lambda\): \[\gamma<\frac2{\lambda_1}\quad(\text{the steep direction sets the limit}).\] If the stiff direction diverges, the whole walk diverges — one bad coordinate is enough.
3
With \(\gamma\) held under that limit, the gentle direction's factor is \[1-\gamma\lambda_2>1-\frac{2\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}.\] For the lecture's valley \(\lambda_1\approx20.06,\ \lambda_2\approx1.94\), so this factor is above \(0.81\) no matter what: at best the \(y\)-error shrinks by a fifth per step while the \(x\)-error, near the limit, flips sign with a factor near \(-1\) — bouncing across the trench while creeping along it. ∎ The ratio \(\lambda_1/\lambda_2\) is the condition number. Big ratio, long trench, slow zig-zag walk. For a general bowl \(\tfrac12\mathbf x^{\top}A\mathbf x\) the same argument runs in the eigenbasis of \(A\) (Unit 4): the eigenvalues of the Hessian are the \(\lambda\)'s.

So what does a practitioner do without knowing \(\lambda_1\)? The lecture's first answer is a simple adaptive rule: after each step, look at the loss. If it went up, you were too bold — undo the step and halve the step size. If it went down, you may be a little bolder next time. Because a step is only kept when it lowers \(f\), the sequence \(f(\mathbf x_0)\ge f(\mathbf x_1)\ge\cdots\) is guaranteed — monotonic convergence. Section 8 turns this into the bold-driver algorithm and into scheduled decay. Section 6 does something cleverer first: it lets the landscape itself choose the stride.

6

Let the landscape choose — exact line search

Sliding the foot

Instead of guessing a stride, do this on the dark staircase: point your foot downhill and slide it forward, feeling the floor, until it stops going down and starts coming up. Put your weight there. You have found the lowest point along that line. Then feel the tilt again — it will point somewhere new — and slide again.

That is line search. Fix the point \(\mathbf x_i\) and the direction \(-\nabla f(\mathbf x_i)\); the only thing left to choose is how far, \(\gamma\). Along the ray the landscape becomes a function of one variable,

\[h(\gamma)=f\big(\mathbf x_i-\gamma\,\nabla f(\mathbf x_i)^{\top}\big),\]

and "how far" is simply "where is \(h\) smallest". The lecture works it out for \(f(x,y)=x^2+3y^2\) from \((2,2)\). Every line of that calculation is worth seeing:

Prove it · the lecture's line search on x² + 3y²

Claim. For \(f=x^2+3y^2\) the best step from \((x,y)\) is \(\gamma^*=\dfrac{x^2+9y^2}{2x^2+54y^2}\); from \((2,2)\) it is \(5/28\), landing at \((9/7,\,-1/7)\).

1
The gradient is \(\nabla f=(2x,\ 6y)\). The trial point a distance \(\gamma\) down the ray is \[(x-2\gamma x,\ \ y-6\gamma y)=\big((1-2\gamma)x,\ (1-6\gamma)y\big).\] Each coordinate keeps its own factor, exactly as in Section 5 — but now \(\gamma\) is a variable we will choose.
2
Put the trial point into \(f\): \[h(\gamma)=(1-2\gamma)^2x^2+3(1-6\gamma)^2y^2.\] A quadratic in \(\gamma\) with a positive leading coefficient — a parabola in \(\gamma\) with a single bottom.
3
Differentiate with respect to \(\gamma\) (chain rule: inner derivatives \(-2\) and \(-6\)) and set to zero: \[h'(\gamma)=-4(1-2\gamma)x^2-36(1-6\gamma)y^2=0.\] \(2(1-2\gamma)\cdot(-2)=-4(1-2\gamma)\) and \(3\cdot2(1-6\gamma)\cdot(-6)=-36(1-6\gamma)\).
4
Expand, collect the \(\gamma\) terms, solve: \[\begin{aligned}-4x^2+8\gamma x^2-36y^2+216\gamma y^2&=0\\ \gamma\,(8x^2+216y^2)&=4x^2+36y^2\\ \gamma^*&=\frac{4x^2+36y^2}{8x^2+216y^2}\\ &=\frac{x^2+9y^2}{2x^2+54y^2}.\end{aligned}\] Divide top and bottom by \(4\). It is a minimum because \(h''=8x^2+216y^2>0\).
5
At \((2,2)\): \(\gamma^*=\dfrac{4+36}{8+216}=\dfrac{40}{224}=\dfrac5{28}\). The new point is \[\begin{aligned}&\Big((1-\tfrac{10}{28})\cdot2,\ (1-\tfrac{30}{28})\cdot2\Big)\\ &=\Big(\tfrac{18}{28}\cdot2,\ -\tfrac{2}{28}\cdot2\Big)=\Big(\tfrac97,\ -\tfrac17\Big),\end{aligned}\] and \(f\) drops from \(16\) to \(\tfrac{81}{49}+\tfrac3{49}=\tfrac{12}7\approx1.71\). ∎ One landscape-chosen step removed 89% of the height. Notice \(y\) overshot past \(0\) to \(-1/7\): even the best step along the gradient overshoots in the stiff direction. The next step's \(\gamma^*\) is \(5/12\), landing at \((3/14,3/14)\).

For any quadratic bowl the same three moves give a closed formula — the one you will be asked for again and again:

Prove it · the general line-search step for a quadratic

Claim. For \(f=\tfrac12\mathbf x^{\top}A\mathbf x+\mathbf b^{\top}\mathbf x\) with \(A\) symmetric positive-definite and \(\mathbf g=\nabla f(\mathbf x)=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b\), the best step along \(-\mathbf g\) is \(\gamma^*=\dfrac{\mathbf g^{\top}\mathbf g}{\mathbf g^{\top}A\mathbf g}\).

1
\(h(\gamma)=f(\mathbf x-\gamma\mathbf g)\). By the chain rule, \(h'(\gamma)=\nabla f(\mathbf x-\gamma\mathbf g)\cdot(-\mathbf g)\). Derivative of \(f\) along a moving point = gradient at that point dotted with the point's velocity, which here is \(-\mathbf g\).
2
The gradient at the trial point is \(A(\mathbf x-\gamma\mathbf g)+\mathbf b=(A\mathbf x+\mathbf b)-\gamma A\mathbf g=\mathbf g-\gamma A\mathbf g\). So \[h'(\gamma)=-\mathbf g^{\top}(\mathbf g-\gamma A\mathbf g)=-\mathbf g^{\top}\mathbf g+\gamma\,\mathbf g^{\top}A\mathbf g.\] The gradient of a quadratic is linear in the point, so it splits cleanly into "the old gradient" minus "\(\gamma\) times \(A\mathbf g\)".
3
Set \(h'(\gamma)=0\): \[\gamma^*=\frac{\mathbf g^{\top}\mathbf g}{\mathbf g^{\top}A\mathbf g},\] a minimum because \(h''=\mathbf g^{\top}A\mathbf g>0\) for positive-definite \(A\). ∎ Check it against the lecture: for \(x^2+3y^2\), \(A=\mathrm{diag}(2,6)\), \(\mathbf g=(2x,6y)\), so \(\mathbf g^{\top}\mathbf g=4x^2+36y^2\) and \(\mathbf g^{\top}A\mathbf g=8x^2+216y^2\) — the same fraction as before. Practice Problems 6, 7 and 9 use this formula.

Now the beautiful fact, the one the lecture proves with Taylor's series: after an exact line-search step, the new gradient is perpendicular to the direction you just walked. Watch the arrow swing to a right angle in the widget, then read why:

Prove it · after an exact step, the new gradient is perpendicular to the old direction

Claim. If \(\alpha_t\) minimises \(J(\mathbf w_t+\alpha\mathbf g_t)\) over \(\alpha\), then \(\mathbf g_t^{\top}\nabla J(\mathbf w_{t+1})=0\), where \(\mathbf w_{t+1}=\mathbf w_t+\alpha_t\mathbf g_t\).

1
Suppose not: \(\mathbf g_t^{\top}\nabla J(\mathbf w_{t+1})\ne0\). Move a tiny extra \(\pm\delta\) along the same direction and use first-order Taylor at \(\mathbf w_{t+1}\): \[J(\mathbf w_{t+1}\pm\delta\mathbf g_t)\approx J(\mathbf w_{t+1})\pm\delta\,\mathbf g_t^{\top}\nabla J(\mathbf w_{t+1}).\] Value plus (step) dot (gradient). This is exactly the line trick from Unit 8, one derivative deep.
2
The correction \(\pm\delta\,\mathbf g_t^{\top}\nabla J\) is nonzero, and we get to pick its sign: choose \(+\delta\) if the dot product is negative, \(-\delta\) if it is positive. Either way, \[J(\mathbf w_{t+1}\pm\delta\mathbf g_t)A nonzero slope along the line means one of the two ways along the line goes down.
3
But that is a point on the same ray — at \(\alpha=\alpha_t\pm\delta\) — with a lower value of \(J\), contradicting that \(\alpha_t\) was the minimiser. So the dot product must be zero: \(\mathbf g_t\perp\nabla J(\mathbf w_{t+1})\). ∎ In one-variable language: \(h'(\alpha_t)=\mathbf g_t^{\top}\nabla J(\mathbf w_{t+1})\), and the minimiser of a smooth function has zero derivative. Geometrically: the ray stops exactly where it is tangent to a contour, and the gradient is always perpendicular to contours. Consequence: steepest descent with exact line search turns a right angle at every corner — a zig-zag of perfect squares, which on a long trench is still a lot of corners.

Line search is honest but expensive: every step demands a one-variable minimisation of its own. The lecture is candid that it is rarely used in vanilla gradient descent for that reason; Newton's method (Units 10–11) needs an exact one, and cheap inexact versions — the Armijo rule of the next section — are what ordinary descent actually uses. The compensation is fewer steps: on our stretched bowl, exact search removed 89% of the height in one move where a safe fixed step removed far less.
