Finding the bottom of h — squeezing a bracket

The radio dial

An old radio has a dial, and somewhere on it the station comes in clearest. You cannot see the signal strength — you can only turn the knob and listen. Turn it left of the sweet spot and the hiss grows; turn it right and the hiss grows too. So you play a game: pick a spot, nudge the knob a hair to the right, and ask "did it get better or worse?" Better means the sweet spot is further right; worse means it is to the left. Each answer throws away half the dial. Ten answers and you are within a thousandth of the station.

Line search hands us exactly this problem: minimise the one-variable function \(h(\alpha)=J(\mathbf w_t+\alpha\mathbf g_t)\), where we can evaluate \(h\) at any \(\alpha\) we like but have no formula for its bottom (for a quadratic we do — Section 6 — but a real loss is not a quadratic). The lecture leans on one property that makes the game fair: near a descent direction, \(h\) is usually unimodal — it goes down, reaches one bottom, and goes up. One valley, no side dips. Especially true when \(h\) is built from a quadratic or convex stand-in for the real loss.

The first step is to find a range \([0,\alpha_{\max}]\) that contains the bottom: try \(\alpha\) at geometrically growing values (\(0.1,\,0.2,\,0.4,\,0.8,\ldots\)) until \(h\) starts rising again. Then squeeze:

Three ways to squeeze, from the lecture.

Binary search. At the midpoint \(m\) of \([a,b]\), evaluate \(h(m)\) and \(h(m+\epsilon)\) for a tiny \(\epsilon\) (say \(10^{-6}\)) — that is "nudge the knob a hair". If \(h\) is increasing at \(m\), the bottom is to the left: keep \([a,\,m+\epsilon]\). Otherwise keep \([m,\,b]\). Every round halves the bracket.

Golden-section search. Use two interior points \(awhere to put \(m_1,m_2\): at the golden fractions \(0.382\) and \(0.618\) of the bracket, because then, after you cut, one of the old interior points is already sitting at a golden fraction of the new bracket — you get the next round's second point for free. One new evaluation per round.

The Armijo rule gives up on exactness. Start with a bold \(\alpha\); accept it if \(h(\alpha)\) is sufficiently below \(h(0)\) — below the line \(h(0)+c\,\alpha\,h'(0)\) with a small \(c\) like \(0.1\) — otherwise halve \(\alpha\) and try again. It does not find the bottom; it finds a step that made honest progress, in a handful of evaluations, and it never needed unimodality at all. That is why the lecture calls it broader in applicability than the two exact methods, and why it is the one vanilla gradient descent actually uses.

Prove it · how many squeezes, and why the golden ratio

Claim. Binary search reaches a bracket of width \(\le w\) from \([a,b]\) in \(k=\lceil\log_2\big((b-a)/w\big)\rceil\) rounds; golden-section search shrinks the bracket by \(\varphi=(\sqrt5-1)/2\approx0.618\) per round and re-uses one point each time.

1
Binary: each round halves the width, so after \(k\) rounds the width is \((b-a)/2^k\). Demanding \((b-a)/2^k\le w\): \[2^k\ge\frac{b-a}{w}\iff k\ge\log_2\frac{b-a}{w}.\] For \([0,4]\) and \(w=0.01\): \(\log_2 400\approx8.64\), so \(k=9\) (width \(4/512\approx0.0078\)). Take the logarithm base 2 of both sides; round up because \(k\) counts whole rounds. This is Practice Problem 12.
2
Golden: put \(m_1=b-\varphi(b-a)\) and \(m_2=a+\varphi(b-a)\). Suppose we keep \([a,m_2]\), whose width is \(\varphi(b-a)\). Where does the old \(m_1\) sit inside it, as a fraction from the left? \[\frac{m_1-a}{m_2-a}=\frac{(1-\varphi)(b-a)}{\varphi(b-a)}=\frac{1-\varphi}{\varphi}.\] We want this to equal \(1-\varphi\), the left golden fraction, so that \(m_1\) can serve as the new bracket's left interior point without a fresh evaluation.
3
Demand \(\dfrac{1-\varphi}{\varphi}=1-\varphi\), i.e. \(1-\varphi=\varphi-\varphi^2\), i.e. \(\varphi^2+\varphi-1=0\), whose positive root is \[\varphi=\frac{-1+\sqrt5}{2}\approx0.618.\] ∎ So the golden ratio is not decoration — it is the unique fraction that lets each round re-use a point. Width after \(k\) rounds: \((b-a)\varphi^k\); for \([0,4]\) and \(w=0.01\), \(k=\lceil\ln(0.0025)/\ln0.618\rceil=13\) rounds, but only 13 new evaluations against binary search's 18 (two per round). Fewer evaluations per unit of squeeze — that is the whole point when each \(h\) costs a pass over the data.

When is all this worth it? The lecture's answer: exact line search provably converges and needs fewer steps, which can outweigh the cost of each step — but each step is expensive, so plain gradient descent rarely bothers, and uses Armijo-style inexact steps instead. Some methods, Newton's among them, require the exact version.

In one sentence: To minimise the one-variable \(h(\alpha)\) without a formula, bracket the bottom and squeeze — halving (binary), golden cuts that re-use a point, or Armijo's cheap "good enough" rule that needs no unimodality.

Bold early, careful late — learning-rate schedules and the bold driver

Parking the car

Nobody parks a car at one speed. On the open road you drive fast because the target is far; as the wall of the garage comes near you slow to a crawl, because a fast car near a wall overshoots and a crawl on the open road takes all day. A learning rate is a speed, and the same logic applies: start large, finish small.

The lecture poses the dilemma plainly. A large constant learning rate gets close to the answer in a few steps and then oscillates around it forever — Section 5's negative factor, bouncing across the trench. A small constant rate converges but takes very long. The fix is a rate that changes with time: \(\mathbf w_{n+1}=\mathbf w_n-\alpha_t\nabla J\), where \(t\) counts epochs — full passes over the training data — so the rate stays fixed within one pass and drops between passes. Three shapes are common:

\[\text{exponential decay: }\alpha_t=\alpha_0e^{-kt},\qquad \text{inverse decay: }\alpha_t=\frac{\alpha_0}{1+kt},\qquad \text{step decay: divide }\alpha\text{ by a constant every few epochs.}\]

In each, \(k\) sets how fast the rate falls. Compare them, then watch the bold driver choose its own speed:

Prove it · the two half-way marks

Claim. Inverse decay halves the learning rate exactly at \(t=1/k\); exponential decay multiplies it by \(1/e\approx0.37\) at the same \(t\).

1
Inverse: set \(\dfrac{\alpha_0}{1+kt}=\dfrac{\alpha_0}{2}\). Cross-multiplying, \[1+kt=2\iff t=\frac1k.\] For \(k=0.05\) that is epoch \(20\): \(0.8/(1+1)=0.4\). Practice Problem 3.
2
Exponential at the same \(t\): \(\alpha_0e^{-k\cdot(1/k)}=\alpha_0e^{-1}\approx0.37\,\alpha_0\). Already below half — and at \(t=2/k\) it is \(e^{-2}\approx0.14\) against inverse decay's \(1/3\). ∎ Exponential decay is geometric (a fixed fraction lost per epoch), inverse decay is harmonic (it loses less and less). Which is right depends on how far the walk still has to go: a rate that dies too fast leaves the walker stranded short of the bottom, one that dies too slowly keeps it jittering. Section 10 shows the second danger with noisy gradients, where decay is not a convenience but a requirement.

The bold-driver algorithm replaces a schedule with a reflex. After every step, compare the loss with the step before. If it improved, press the accelerator a little: multiply the rate by about \(1.05\). If it got worse, you overshot: undo the step, halve the rate, and try again from where you were. Kept steps only ever lower the loss, so — like the adaptive rule in Section 5 — descent is monotone, and the rate drifts up until it finds the speed limit and is knocked back down. In the widget it hunts around \(2/\lambda_1\), exactly the number Section 5 predicted.

The lecture adds one honest caveat that points at Section 10. When the loss is estimated from a sample of the data rather than computed exactly, it is noisy — it may go "up" by chance, not because the step was bad. Then the driver panics and halves for no reason. The remedy is patience: judge the loss every \(m\) steps, not every step.

Pause & predict

Pause & predict

In one sentence: A learning rate should be a speed that falls as the wall approaches — by schedule (exponential, inverse, step) or by reflex (the bold driver: up 5% on success, halve and undo on failure).

Is my compass even right? — the finite-difference audit

The known weight

Before a market inspector trusts a shopkeeper's scale, she puts a known one-kilogram weight on it. If the needle says 1.0, fine. If it says 1.3, every sale that day was wrong and nobody noticed. A gradient computed by code — by hand-written derivatives or by backpropagation — is that scale. It will confidently steer a million-parameter walk, and if it is wrong by a sign or a factor, the walk goes confidently to the wrong place. So we put a known weight on it.

The known weight is the definition of a derivative itself. For a loss \(J(w_1,\ldots,w_d)\), nudge one parameter by a small \(\Delta\) and see how much \(J\) moves:

\[\frac{\partial J}{\partial w_i}\approx\frac{J(w_1,\ldots,w_i+\Delta,\ldots,w_d)-J(w_1,\ldots,w_i,\ldots,w_d)}{\Delta}.\]

This is the forward difference. It costs one extra evaluation of \(J\) per parameter, so for a big model you audit a few sampled parameters, not all — which is precisely what the lecture prescribes. A slightly smarter version nudges both ways, the central difference \(\big(J(w_i+\Delta)-J(w_i-\Delta)\big)/2\Delta\), and it is far more accurate for the same \(\Delta\). Unit 8 tells us exactly how much more:

Prove it · forward error ∝ Δ, central error ∝ Δ²

Claim. For a smooth one-variable \(J\), the forward difference errs by about \(\tfrac12J''\Delta\) and the central difference by about \(\tfrac16J'''\Delta^2\).

1
Taylor at \(w\), one step forward and one step back: \[\begin{aligned}J(w+\Delta)&=J+J'\Delta+\tfrac12J''\Delta^2+\tfrac16J'''\Delta^3+\cdots\\ J(w-\Delta)&=J-J'\Delta+\tfrac12J''\Delta^2-\tfrac16J'''\Delta^3+\cdots\end{aligned}\] Same series, with \(\Delta\) replaced by \(-\Delta\): odd powers flip sign, even powers do not.
2
Forward: subtract \(J\) and divide by \(\Delta\): \[\begin{aligned}\frac{J(w+\Delta)-J(w)}{\Delta}&=J'+\tfrac12J''\Delta+\cdots\\ \text{error}&\approx\tfrac12J''\,\Delta.\end{aligned}\] The leading leftover is the bend term; it shrinks only as fast as \(\Delta\).
3
Central: subtract the backward series from the forward one — every even-power term cancels — and divide by \(2\Delta\): \[\begin{aligned}\frac{J(w+\Delta)-J(w-\Delta)}{2\Delta}&=\frac{2J'\Delta+\tfrac13J'''\Delta^3+\cdots}{2\Delta}\\ &=J'+\tfrac16J'''\Delta^2+\cdots\end{aligned}\] ∎ The bend term is gone; the error is \(\Delta^2\)-sized. Halve \(\Delta\) and the forward error halves but the central error quarters. On the widget's example, \(J=w_1^3+w_1w_2^2\) at \((2,1)\) with \(\Delta=0.1\): forward \(13.61\) (error \(0.61\approx\tfrac12\cdot12\cdot0.1\)), central \(13.01\) (error \(0.01=\tfrac16\cdot6\cdot0.01\)). Practice Problem 8.

Why not make \(\Delta\) absurdly tiny, then? Because a computer stores \(J\) to about sixteen digits, and \(J(w+\Delta)-J(w)\) is the difference of two nearly equal numbers: the true digits cancel and only rounding noise survives, which then gets divided by a tiny \(\Delta\) and amplified. Slide \(\Delta\) below about \(10^{-6}\) in the widget and watch the error curves turn back up. The sweet spot is a moderately small \(\Delta\) — and the audit is a spot check, not a way to compute gradients for real.

Pause & predict

With \(\Delta=0.1\) the central-difference estimate of a partial derivative is off by \(0.01\). You shrink \(\Delta\) to \(0.01\). Roughly what is the new error?

In one sentence: Audit a coded gradient by nudging a few parameters; the forward difference errs like \(\Delta\), the central one like \(\Delta^2\), and too tiny a \(\Delta\) drowns in round-off.
