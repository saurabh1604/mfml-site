Tasting the soup — stochastic gradient descent

One spoon, not the pot

A cook checking whether the soup needs salt does not drink the whole pot. One spoonful, stirred from anywhere, tells her enough — not exactly, but enough to decide "a little more salt". She adds a pinch, stirs, tastes another spoon. Fifty small, slightly wrong decisions get the soup right faster than one exact measurement that requires drinking the pot. Training on a million examples is the pot. A minibatch is the spoon.

In a real learning problem the loss is a sum over the data points: with \(N\) examples and the loss \(L_n(\boldsymbol\theta)\) of the \(n\)-th,

\[L(\boldsymbol\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}L_n(\boldsymbol\theta),\qquad \nabla L=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla L_n.\]

The gradient descent we have used so far is batch descent: every step computes the whole sum — \(N\) gradients, one per example — before moving. For \(N\) in the millions that is drinking the pot on every step. The lecture's alternative: take a sample \(S\) of the indices, build the sample's own loss

\[J(S)=\sum_{i\in S}\big(\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf X_i-y_i\big)^2\qquad(\text{the lecture's least-squares example}),\]

and step against its gradient instead. With \(|S|\) examples in the sample this is minibatch stochastic gradient descent; in the extreme case \(|S|=1\) — one example, chosen at random — it is stochastic gradient descent proper. The key claim is that the sample's gradient is an excellent approximation of the true gradient's direction. Watch how good, and how noisy:

Why does a spoonful work? The lecture's answer is one word — unbiased — and it is worth seeing exactly what that word buys and what it does not:

Prove it · the minibatch gradient is unbiased

Claim. If \(S\) is a random sample of \(m\) indices out of \(N\) (each index equally likely to be included), then the scaled sample gradient \(\dfrac Nm\sum_{i\in S}\nabla L_i\) has expected value exactly \(\nabla L\).

1
Write the sample sum with an indicator for each example: \(\mathbb 1_n=1\) if \(n\in S\), else \(0\). Then \[\sum_{i\in S}\nabla L_i=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb 1_n\,\nabla L_n.\] Summing over "the chosen ones" is the same as summing over everyone with a switch that is on only for the chosen.
2
Take expectations. The gradients \(\nabla L_n\) are fixed numbers; only the switches are random, and each is on with probability \(m/N\): \[\begin{aligned}\mathbb E\Big[\sum_{n}\mathbb 1_n\nabla L_n\Big]&=\sum_n\mathbb E[\mathbb 1_n]\,\nabla L_n\\ &=\sum_n\frac mN\,\nabla L_n=\frac mN\,\nabla L.\end{aligned}\] Expectation of a sum is the sum of expectations; the expectation of a 0/1 switch is the probability it is on. Every example is in the sample with the same chance, \(m/N\).
3
Multiply by \(N/m\): \(\ \mathbb E\big[\tfrac Nm\sum_{i\in S}\nabla L_i\big]=\nabla L\). ∎ "Unbiased" means: on average, over many spoonfuls, the estimate is exactly the true gradient. It does not mean any single spoonful is right — a single one can point well off. The scale factor \(N/m\) is absorbed into the learning rate in practice, which is why the lecture's update simply uses \(\nabla J(S)\).
Prove it · bigger spoon, less noise — variance falls like 1/|S|

Claim. If the per-example gradients are drawn independently with variance \(\sigma^2\) (per component), the average of \(m\) of them has variance \(\sigma^2/m\).

1
Let \(g_1,\ldots,g_m\) be the sampled gradients (one component) and \(\bar g=\tfrac1m\sum_i g_i\) their average. Pulling the constant out of a variance squares it: \[\operatorname{Var}(\bar g)=\frac1{m^2}\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_ig_i\Big).\] \(\operatorname{Var}(cX)=c^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\).
2
For independent draws the variance of a sum is the sum of variances: \[\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_ig_i\Big)=\sum_i\sigma^2=m\sigma^2.\] Independence kills the cross-terms; each draw contributes \(\sigma^2\).
3
So \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar g)=\dfrac{m\sigma^2}{m^2}=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{m}\), and the typical size of the noise (the standard deviation) is \(\sigma/\sqrt m\). ∎ Four times the batch, half the noise. That is the lecture's last line — large minibatches give lower variance — and the trade it names: a bigger spoon is a better estimate but a dearer step. In the widget, \(|S|=4\) out of \(40\) buys ten steps for the price of one exact one, with half the noise of a single example.

One consequence deserves to be said out loud, because it changes how you set the learning rate. With exact gradients, a fixed small step converges: the walker stops when the gradient does. With a noisy gradient it never stops — near the bottom the true gradient is nearly zero, but the spoonful keeps saying "a bit more salt", "a bit less", and the walker jitters in a cloud. How big a cloud? On the simplest valley we can compute it exactly:

Prove it · a constant step leaves a jitter cloud proportional to γ

Claim. On \(f=\tfrac12\lambda x^2\) with a noisy gradient \(\lambda x_k+\varepsilon_k\) (noise of mean \(0\), variance \(\sigma^2\), independent step to step) and a constant \(\gamma<2/\lambda\), the iterates settle into a cloud around \(0\) of variance \(\dfrac{\gamma\sigma^2}{\lambda(2-\gamma\lambda)}\approx\dfrac{\gamma\sigma^2}{2\lambda}\).

1
The noisy update is \[x_{k+1}=x_k-\gamma(\lambda x_k+\varepsilon_k)=(1-\gamma\lambda)\,x_k-\gamma\varepsilon_k.\] Section 5's factor, plus a random kick of size \(\gamma\varepsilon_k\) every step.
2
Square and take expectations. The cross-term vanishes because \(\varepsilon_k\) has mean \(0\) and is independent of \(x_k\): \[\mathbb E[x_{k+1}^2]=(1-\gamma\lambda)^2\,\mathbb E[x_k^2]+\gamma^2\sigma^2.\] \((a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2\); the middle term's expectation is \(2(1-\gamma\lambda)\gamma\,\mathbb E[x_k]\mathbb E[\varepsilon_k]=0\).
3
In the long run the spread stops changing: \(\mathbb E[x_{k+1}^2]=\mathbb E[x_k^2]=V\). Then \[\begin{aligned}V&=(1-\gamma\lambda)^2V+\gamma^2\sigma^2\\ V\big(1-(1-\gamma\lambda)^2\big)&=\gamma^2\sigma^2\\ V&=\frac{\gamma^2\sigma^2}{\gamma\lambda(2-\gamma\lambda)}=\frac{\gamma\sigma^2}{\lambda(2-\gamma\lambda)}.\end{aligned}\] ∎ \(1-(1-\gamma\lambda)^2=\gamma\lambda(2-\gamma\lambda)\) (difference of squares). For small \(\gamma\) this is \(\approx\gamma\sigma^2/2\lambda\): the cloud's variance is proportional to \(\gamma\). Halve the step and you halve the cloud; only \(\gamma\to0\) shrinks it to a point — which is exactly what a decaying schedule does.

The lecture states the cure and the theorem: if the learning rate decreases at a suitable rate — fast enough that the jitter dies, slowly enough that the total distance walked is unbounded (the classic conditions are \(\sum_t\gamma_t=\infty\) and \(\sum_t\gamma_t^2<\infty\), which \(\gamma_t=\gamma_0/(1+kt)\) satisfies) — then under mild assumptions stochastic gradient descent converges to a local minimum almost surely. Section 8's decay schedules were not a luxury after all; with noise, they are what makes the walk end.

And one benefit the lecture only hints at with "a little noise": on a bumpy landscape with many shallow dips (Section 3), a noisy step can hop out of a shallow one that exact descent would be stuck in forever. The fog was never the enemy.

Pause & predict

You have \(N=1{,}000{,}000\) examples. One step of batch descent costs a million per-example gradients. With minibatches of \(|S|=100\), how many steps do you get for the same cost — and are they as good?

Pause & predict

You run stochastic gradient descent with \(|S|=1\) and a constant learning rate. The loss falls fast and then stops improving, wobbling around a value slightly above the minimum. What is going on, and what fixes it?

In one sentence: A minibatch gradient is unbiased and its noise shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{|S|}\), so many cheap noisy steps beat one exact one — and a decaying learning rate is what finally lets the noisy walk stop.

The one thing to remember

Feel the slope, step against it, and let the steepest direction set your stride. The gradient is the only map you get in the fog; a step of \(\gamma<2/\lambda_{\max}\) is guaranteed to go down; a narrow trench makes that step tiny and the walk a zig-zag; and when the data is too big to touch, a random spoonful of it points the right way on average. Everything the next units add — momentum, Newton, constraints — is a way of walking the same fog more cleverly.

Practice arena — twelve problems, solved in full

Twelve problems, easy to hard, covering the whole walk: classifying flat spots, tracing gradient-descent iterations by hand, learning-rate schedules, the line-search formula derived and applied, gradient checking, a coupled quadratic, training a straight line by descent, and a bracketing trace. Every number below is machine-verified.

Two habits do most of the work: trace iterations in a table — point, gradient, new point, value — so nothing is lost in the bookkeeping; and for any step-size question, find the largest eigenvalue first, because \(2/\lambda_{\max}\) is the limit and \(1-\gamma\lambda\) is the whole story of the approach.
