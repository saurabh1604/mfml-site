The problem Unit 17 left behind

Imagine this

A class of eight students plays "pass the message". The first student whispers to the second, the second to the third, and so on. By the time it reaches the eighth student, the message is faint and mixed up. And it took eight turns.

Now change the rule. Every student may stand up and ask any other student a question directly, and everyone asks at the same time. One turn. Nothing gets lost on the way.

The first game is how the machines of Unit 17 read. A recurrent network reads one word, updates its running note, reads the next word, updates the note again. It has two problems.

  1. It is slow. Word 8 must wait for word 7, which waits for word 6. A computer with a thousand workers can still use only one of them at a time.
  2. It forgets. The first word's voice is passed along seven times to reach word 8. If each hand-over keeps about half of it, what arrives is \(0.5^7\approx0.0078\) — less than 1%. And in an encoder–decoder, the whole sentence must squeeze through one small note: the bottleneck.

The second game is attention. Every word looks at every other word directly. The path from word 1 to word 8 is one step long, not seven. And all the looking happens at once, so all thousand workers can help.

The price is the number of questions. With 8 words, each of the 8 asks all 8 (itself too): \(8\times8=64\) questions. We will come back to that price in §10.

The realization

\[\begin{gathered}\text{relay: }n-1\text{ hops,}\\ \text{one after another}\\ \text{attention: }1\text{ hop,}\\ \text{all }n\times n\text{ at once}\end{gathered}\]

Reading one word at a time makes the path between far-apart words long, so their link fades, and it makes the work wait in a queue. Attention joins every pair of words directly and does all the joining at the same time. It pays for this with \(n^2\) questions.

Attention is a soft lookup

Imagine this

You walk up to a tea stall and say, "Something hot and milky, please."

A strict shopkeeper looks for the one item that matches best and hands you only that. A kind shopkeeper pours you a small cup of each drink, with more of the ones that match your wish better, and mixes them. The first is a lookup. The second is attention.

A dictionary is a strict shopkeeper. You bring a query (what you ask for). Each entry has a key (its label) and a value (what it gives back). If your query matches a key exactly, you get that one value. Otherwise, nothing.

Attention is the kind shopkeeper. It never gives just one value. It gives a blend of all the values, and the recipe of the blend comes from how well each key matches the query.

A tiny example. The stall has two drinks. Chai costs ₹10, coffee costs ₹20. Your wish matches chai with a score of 2 and coffee with a score of 0. Turn the scores into shares with softmax (Unit 14):

\[\begin{aligned}\text{chai: }&\frac{e^{2}}{e^{2}+e^{0}}=\frac{7.389}{8.389}\approx0.881\\ \text{coffee: }&\frac{e^{0}}{e^{2}+e^{0}}=\frac{1}{8.389}\approx0.119\end{aligned}\]

The two shares add up to 1. Your mixed cup is 88% chai and 12% coffee, so its price is the same blend of the prices: \(0.881\times10+0.119\times20\approx11.19\) — ₹11.19. A strict lookup would have said ₹10, full stop.

Where does a score come from? From the dot product of Unit 3: write the query and each key as lists of numbers, multiply them entry by entry and add up. Big when they point the same way, zero when they have nothing in common, negative when they disagree.

The realization

\[\begin{gathered}\text{output}=\sum_i w_i\,\mathbf v_i,\\ w_i=\frac{e^{\,\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k_i}}{\sum_j e^{\,\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k_j}}\end{gathered}\]

Attention is a lookup that never says "no match". Every key gets a score by a dot product with the query. Softmax turns the scores into shares that add up to 1. The answer is the blend of all the values with those shares. Make the scores bigger and bigger, and the biggest share swallows the rest: the soft lookup becomes a strict one.

3

Queries, keys and values, worked by hand

Imagine this

At a school fair each student wears two badges. One badge says what I am looking for. The other says what I can help with. And each student carries a small bag with what I will hand over if you come to me.

You walk around, compare your "looking for" badge with everyone's "can help with" badge, and collect a little from each bag — more from the students who match you best.

Those three things have names. The "looking for" badge is the query \(\mathbf q\). The "can help with" badge is the key \(\mathbf k\). The bag is the value \(\mathbf v\). In this unit queries are orange, keys are blue, values are green and attention weights are gold.

Three words, worked by hand. Each word has a key and a value, each a list of two numbers (so the size is \(d=2\)):

\[\begin{aligned}&\mathbf k_1=(1,0),\ \ \mathbf k_2=(0,1),\ \ \mathbf k_3=(1,1)\\ &\mathbf v_1=(2,0),\ \ \mathbf v_2=(0,2),\ \ \mathbf v_3=(1,1)\end{aligned}\]

A word arrives with the query \(\mathbf q=(2,0)\). Four small moves:

  1. Score each key with a dot product, then divide by \(\sqrt d=\sqrt2\approx1.414\) (§4 says why): \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k_1=2\), \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k_2=0\), \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k_3=2\). Divided: \((1.414,\ 0,\ 1.414)\).
  2. Turn scores into shares with softmax: \(e^{1.414}\approx4.113\) and \(e^{0}=1\), total \(9.227\). Shares \(\approx(0.446,\ 0.108,\ 0.446)\).
  3. Blend the values with those shares: \(0.446\,(2,0)+0.108\,(0,2)+0.446\,(1,1)\).
  4. Read the answer: \(\approx(1.337,\ 0.663)\).

The query points along the first direction, so it matched keys 1 and 3 equally well and key 2 badly. The answer leans towards \(\mathbf v_1\) and \(\mathbf v_3\).

Where do q, k and v come from? From the words themselves. Each word starts as one list of numbers (its vector, Unit 16). Three learned matrices turn that one list into three:

\[\mathbf q=W_Q^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x,\qquad\mathbf k=W_K^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x,\qquad\mathbf v=W_V^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x.\]

So the same word asks one thing (its query), advertises another (its key) and hands over a third (its value). Training chooses the three matrices.

All three words at once. In a sentence every word asks its own question. Stack the queries as the rows of a matrix \(Q\), the keys as the rows of \(K\) and the values as the rows of \(V\). Take the three queries \((2,0)\), \((0,2)\) and \((1,1)\). Then all the lookups happen in one line:

\[\operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^{\mathsf T}}{\sqrt d}\Big)\,V.\]

Read it from the inside out. \(QK^{\mathsf T}\) is a table of every query dotted with every key. Dividing by \(\sqrt d\) calms the numbers. Softmax works along each row, so each row becomes shares that add up to 1. That table of shares is the attention matrix:

\[A\approx\begin{pmatrix}0.446&0.108&0.446\\ 0.108&0.446&0.446\\ 0.248&0.248&0.503\end{pmatrix}.\]

Multiplying by \(V\) blends the values, one row per word. The answers are \((1.337,0.663)\), \((0.663,1.337)\) and \((1,1)\). The third query \((1,1)\) likes key 3 most, and its answer is exactly \(\mathbf v_3\) — the other two values balance each other.

The realization

\[\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^{\mathsf T}}{\sqrt d}\Big)V\]

Every word turns itself into a question, a label and a gift. Each question is scored against every label by a dot product; softmax makes the scores into shares; each word collects the gifts in those shares. One matrix line does it for all the words at once. This is Unit 13's kernel vote: similarity by a dot product, then a weighted sum.
