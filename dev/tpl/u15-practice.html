13

Practice arena — fourteen problems, solved in full

Fourteen problems, easy to hard: shapes and parameter counts, a forward pass by hand, collapsing two straight layers, building a shape out of ReLUs, a softmax head, both backward rules on their own, then a complete backward pass through a new network, one update, a gradient check, fading blame, dead neurons, wide against deep, and a training run to diagnose. Every number here was checked by machine.

Three habits do most of the work. Write the shapes first: every gradient has the shape of the thing it belongs to. Keep a cache: write every \(\mathbf z\) and \(\mathbf a\) of the forward pass in a table before you start going back. And check each ReLU's switch before you use Rule A — a negative \(z\) means a zero.

Problem 1easyshapes

A network has shape 3 → 4 → 4 → 2. (a) Give the shape of every weight matrix and bias vector. (b) How many numbers does the network learn in total?

What this tests. The shape rule: \(W^{(l)}\) is (neurons in layer \(l\)) × (neurons in layer \(l-1\)). Plan. Walk the arrows one at a time; count weights and biases separately, then add.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — layer 1 (3 → 4). Rows = 4 neurons here, columns = 3 before: \(W^{(1)}\) is \(4\times3\) (12 numbers), \(\mathbf b^{(1)}\) is \(4\times1\) (4).
Step 2 — layer 2 (4 → 4). \(W^{(2)}\) is \(4\times4\) (16), \(\mathbf b^{(2)}\) is \(4\times1\) (4).
Step 3 — layer 3 (4 → 2). \(W^{(3)}\) is \(2\times4\) (8), \(\mathbf b^{(3)}\) is \(2\times1\) (2).
Step 4 — add up (b). \(12+4+16+4+8+2=46\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(W^{(1)}:4\times3\), \(\mathbf b^{(1)}:4\times1\), \(W^{(2)}:4\times4\), \(\mathbf b^{(2)}:4\times1\), \(W^{(3)}:2\times4\), \(\mathbf b^{(3)}:2\times1\). (b) 46 numbers.

Remember

Rows belong to where the wires go, columns to where they come from. Each layer adds (out × in) weights plus (out) biases.

Problem 2easyforward pass

A 2 → 2 → 1 network has a ReLU hidden layer and a plain (no bend) output: \[W^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\ 2&1\end{pmatrix},\ \mathbf b^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\ -3\end{pmatrix},\qquad W^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}3&-2\end{pmatrix},\ b^{(2)}=4.\] Find \(\mathbf z^{(1)}\), \(\mathbf a^{(1)}\) and \(\hat y\) for (a) \(\mathbf x=(2,1)\) and (b) \(\mathbf x=(1,3)\).

What this tests. Row-times-column, the shift, and the ReLU switch. Plan. One row per hidden neuron; check each sign before bending.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a), the mix. \[\begin{aligned}\text{row 1: }&(1)(2)+(-1)(1)+0=1\\ \text{row 2: }&(2)(2)+(1)(1)-3=2\end{aligned}\] So \(\mathbf z^{(1)}=(1,2)\).
Step 2 — (a), bend and output. Both positive, so \(\mathbf a^{(1)}=(1,2)\). Then \(\hat y=(3)(1)+(-2)(2)+4=3-4+4=3\).
Step 3 — (b), the mix. Row 1: \(1-3+0=-2\). Row 2: \(2+3-3=2\). So \(\mathbf z^{(1)}=(-2,2)\).
Step 4 — (b), bend and output. The first neuron is asleep: \(\mathbf a^{(1)}=(0,2)\). Then \(\hat y=(3)(0)+(-2)(2)+4=0\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(\mathbf z^{(1)}=(1,2)\), \(\mathbf a^{(1)}=(1,2)\), \(\hat y=3\). (b) \(\mathbf z^{(1)}=(-2,2)\), \(\mathbf a^{(1)}=(0,2)\), \(\hat y=0\).

Remember

Which neurons are awake depends on the input. The same network uses different "active wires" for different people.

Problem 3easycollapse

Two layers with no activation: \(W_1=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\ 1&1\end{pmatrix}\), \(\mathbf b_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\ -1\end{pmatrix}\), \(W_2=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 0&3\end{pmatrix}\), \(\mathbf b_2=\begin{pmatrix}0\\ 2\end{pmatrix}\). (a) Write the single layer \(M\mathbf x+\mathbf c\) that does the same job. (b) Check both versions at \(\mathbf x=(1,2)\).

What this tests. \(W_2(W_1\mathbf x+\mathbf b_1)+\mathbf b_2=(W_2W_1)\mathbf x+(W_2\mathbf b_1+\mathbf b_2)\). Plan. One matrix product, one matrix–vector product, one check.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the matrix. \(M=W_2W_1=\begin{pmatrix}2+1&0+1\\ 0+3&0+3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\ 3&3\end{pmatrix}\).
Step 2 — the shift. \(\mathbf c=W_2\mathbf b_1+\mathbf b_2=(1-1,\ 0-3)+(0,2)=(0,-1)\).
Step 3 — two layers at (1, 2). Layer 1: \((2+0+1,\ 1+2-1)=(3,2)\). Layer 2: \((3+2+0,\ 0+6+2)=(5,8)\).
Step 4 — one layer at (1, 2). \((3+2+0,\ 3+6-1)=(5,8)\). The same.

answers at a glance: (a) \(M=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\ 3&3\end{pmatrix}\), \(\mathbf c=(0,-1)\). (b) both give \((5,8)\).

Remember

Without a bend, depth adds nothing: every stack of straight layers is one matrix and one shift.

Problem 4mediumbuilding shapes

Build this function from ReLUs of the form \(c\cdot\operatorname{ReLU}(x-k)\): it is 0 for \(x\le1\), rises in a straight line to 3 at \(x=2\), falls in a straight line to 0 at \(x=5\), and stays 0 after that. Give the creases and their weights, and check the value at \(x=3\).

What this tests. The hat idea: each crease's weight is the change of slope there. Plan. Find the slope of each straight piece, then the jumps in slope.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — slopes of the pieces. Before 1: slope 0. From 1 to 2: rise 3 over 1, slope 3. From 2 to 5: fall 3 over 3, slope −1. After 5: slope 0.
Step 2 — changes of slope. At \(x=1\): \(0\to3\), change \(+3\). At \(x=2\): \(3\to-1\), change \(-4\). At \(x=5\): \(-1\to0\), change \(+1\).
Step 3 — the function. \[f(x)=3\operatorname{ReLU}(x-1)-4\operatorname{ReLU}(x-2)+\operatorname{ReLU}(x-5).\]
Step 4 — checks. \(f(2)=3\). \(f(3)=3(2)-4(1)+0=2\) (on the falling piece: \(3-1=2\) ✓). \(f(6)=15-16+1=0\).

answers at a glance: creases at 1, 2, 5 with weights \(+3,-4,+1\); \(f(3)=2\).

Remember

A ReLU's weight is how much the slope turns at its crease. Read slopes off the picture, take differences, done.

Problem 5easyclassification head

A three-class head outputs scores \(\mathbf z=(1,3,0)\). The true class is 2. Find (a) the softmax \(\mathbf q\), (b) the cross-entropy loss, (c) \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf z\).

What this tests. Softmax, \(-\ln q_{\text{true}}\), and prediction minus truth. Plan. Exponentiate, add, divide; then subtract the one-hot label.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — exponentials. \(e^1\approx2.718\), \(e^3\approx20.086\), \(e^0=1\). Total \(\approx23.804\).
Step 2 — softmax (a). \(\mathbf q\approx(2.718,\,20.086,\,1)/23.804=(0.114,\,0.844,\,0.042)\).
Step 3 — loss (b). \(L=-\ln0.844\approx0.170\).
Step 4 — blame (c). \(\mathbf y=(0,1,0)\), so \(\mathbf q-\mathbf y\approx(0.114,\,-0.156,\,0.042)\). The entries add to 0.

answers at a glance: (a) \(\mathbf q\approx(0.114,0.844,0.042)\). (b) \(L\approx0.170\). (c) \((0.114,-0.156,0.042)\).

Remember

At a softmax head the blame is \(\mathbf q-\mathbf y\): the true class is told "go up" by how much probability it is missing; the others "go down" by how much they took.

Problem 6easyRule A

Blame \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf a=(4,-2,6,-1)\) arrives at a ReLU layer whose stored pre-activations are \(\mathbf z=(3,-1,0.5,-4)\). Find \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf z\) and say which neurons pass no blame.

What this tests. Rule A as a switch. Plan. Slopes from the signs of \(\mathbf z\), then multiply entry by entry.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the switches. \(\operatorname{ReLU}'(\mathbf z)=(1,0,1,0)\): neurons 2 and 4 were asleep.
Step 2 — multiply. \((4,-2,6,-1)\odot(1,0,1,0)=(4,0,6,0)\).

answers at a glance: \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf z=(4,0,6,0)\); neurons 2 and 4 pass nothing.

Remember

Rule A looks at the sign of the stored \(z\), never at the sign of the blame.

Problem 7mediumRule B

A linear layer has \(W=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0\\ 1&3&-2\end{pmatrix}\), stored input \(\mathbf a=(1,2,3)\), and incoming blame \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf z=(0.5,-1)\). Find \(\partial L/\partial W\), \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf b\) and \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf a\).

What this tests. All three results of Rule B, and where the transpose goes. Plan. Shapes first: \(\partial L/\partial W\) is \(2\times3\), \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf a\) is \(3\times1\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the weights (outer product). \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}=\begin{pmatrix}0.5\\ -1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&1&1.5\\ -1&-2&-3\end{pmatrix}.\]
Step 2 — the shifts. \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf b=(0.5,-1)\).
Step 3 — the layer below (\(W^{\mathsf T}\)). \[\begin{aligned}\text{row 1: }&(2)(0.5)+(1)(-1)=0\\ \text{row 2: }&(-1)(0.5)+(3)(-1)=-3.5\\ \text{row 3: }&(0)(0.5)+(-2)(-1)=2\end{aligned}\]

answers at a glance: \(\partial L/\partial W=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&1&1.5\\ -1&-2&-3\end{pmatrix}\), \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf b=(0.5,-1)\), \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf a=(0,-3.5,2)\).

Remember

Weights: (blame column)(input row). Layer below: \(W^{\mathsf T}\)·blame — each input collects the blame of every neuron it fed.

Problem 8hardfull backward pass

Take Problem 2's network at \(\mathbf x=(2,1)\), with target \(y=5\) and loss \(L=\tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2\). Find the loss and all six gradients: \(\partial L/\partial W^{(2)}\), \(\partial L/\partial b^{(2)}\), \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf a^{(1)}\), \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf z^{(1)}\), \(\partial L/\partial W^{(1)}\), \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf b^{(1)}\).

What this tests. The whole backward sweep on a fresh network. Plan. Cache first; the output has no bend, so the seed goes straight into Rule B.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the cache. From Problem 2: \(\mathbf a^{(0)}=(2,1)\), \(\mathbf z^{(1)}=(1,2)\), \(\mathbf a^{(1)}=(1,2)\), \(\hat y=3\). Loss \(L=\tfrac12(3-5)^2=2\).
Step 2 — the seed. \(\partial L/\partial\hat y=3-5=-2\). No bend at the output, so this is also \(\partial L/\partial z^{(2)}\).
Step 3 — Rule B at layer 2. \(\partial L/\partial W^{(2)}=(-2)(1,2)=(-2,-4)\), \(\partial L/\partial b^{(2)}=-2\), \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf a^{(1)}=(3,-2)^{\mathsf T}(-2)=(-6,4)\).
Step 4 — Rule A at layer 1. \(\mathbf z^{(1)}=(1,2)\): both awake, so \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf z^{(1)}=(-6,4)\).
Step 5 — Rule B at layer 1. \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(1)}}=\begin{pmatrix}-6\\ 4\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-12&-6\\ 8&4\end{pmatrix},\qquad\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf b^{(1)}}=\begin{pmatrix}-6\\ 4\end{pmatrix}.\]

answers at a glance: \(L=2\); \(\partial L/\partial W^{(2)}=(-2,-4)\), \(\partial L/\partial b^{(2)}=-2\), \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf a^{(1)}=(-6,4)\), \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf z^{(1)}=(-6,4)\), \(\partial L/\partial W^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}-12&-6\\ 8&4\end{pmatrix}\), \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf b^{(1)}=(-6,4)\).

Remember

Seed, then alternate: Rule B, Rule A, Rule B… Every step reads one thing from the cache.

Problem 9mediumone update

Continue Problem 8. Take one gradient-descent step with \(\eta=0.01\). Write the new weights and shifts, run the forward pass again at \(\mathbf x=(2,1)\), and give the new \(\hat y\) and loss.

What this tests. \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\partial L/\partial\theta\) for every number, then a fresh forward pass. Plan. Each change is \(-0.01\times\)gradient.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the new numbers. \[\begin{aligned}W^{(1)}&=\begin{pmatrix}1.12&-0.94\\ 1.92&0.96\end{pmatrix},\ \mathbf b^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0.06\\ -3.04\end{pmatrix},\\ W^{(2)}&=\begin{pmatrix}3.02&-1.96\end{pmatrix},\ b^{(2)}=4.02.\end{aligned}\] For example \(1-0.01(-12)=1.12\), and \(-3-0.01(4)=-3.04\).
Step 2 — forward again. Row 1: \(2.24-0.94+0.06=1.36\). Row 2: \(3.84+0.96-3.04=1.76\). Both awake.
Step 3 — output and loss. \(\hat y=(3.02)(1.36)+(-1.96)(1.76)+4.02=4.1072-3.4496+4.02=4.6776\). \(L=\tfrac12(4.6776-5)^2\approx0.052\).

answers at a glance: \(\hat y=4.6776\), loss \(2\to0.052\).

Remember

One forward pass, one backward pass, one small step — and the loss here fell by a factor of almost 40.

Problem 10mediumgradient check

In Problem 8's setting, check \(\partial L/\partial W^{(2)}_1\) (the weight 3) by finite differences with \(h=0.1\). (a) Compute \(L(3.1)\) and \(L(2.9)\) and the central difference. (b) Compute the one-sided difference \((L(3.1)-L(3))/0.1\). Compare both with backprop.

What this tests. Doing a gradient check by hand, and why the central version is better. Plan. Only \(\hat y\) changes: \(\hat y=w\cdot1-2\cdot2+4=w\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the loss as a function of this weight. With \(\mathbf a^{(1)}=(1,2)\) fixed, \(\hat y=w-4+4=w\), so \(L(w)=\tfrac12(w-5)^2\).
Step 2 — central (a). \(L(3.1)=\tfrac12(1.9)^2=1.805\), \(L(2.9)=\tfrac12(2.1)^2=2.205\). Central difference \((1.805-2.205)/0.2=-2\). Backprop said \(-2\): exact agreement.
Step 3 — one-sided (b). \(L(3)=2\). \((1.805-2)/0.1=-1.95\): off by 0.05, which is \(h/2\) times the curvature 1.

answers at a glance: (a) \(1.805,\ 2.205,\ -2\) (matches backprop). (b) \(-1.95\).

Remember

Use the central difference \((L(\theta+h)-L(\theta-h))/2h\): its error shrinks like \(h^2\), the one-sided one only like \(h\).

Problem 11easyfading blame

A chain of 6 one-neuron sigmoid layers. (a) If every \(|w|\le2\), what is the most the blame can keep, as a fraction, from top to bottom? (b) And if every \(|w|\le1\)? (c) What is the factor through 6 awake ReLU layers with every \(w=1\)?

What this tests. The blame is a product of (slope × weight) per layer. Plan. Sigmoid's slope ≤ 0.25; ReLU's slope = 1 when awake.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). Each layer: at most \(0.25\times2=0.5\). Six layers: \(0.5^6=1/64\approx0.0156\).
Step 2 — (b). Each layer at most \(0.25\): \(0.25^6=1/4096\approx0.000244\).
Step 3 — (c). Each layer exactly \(1\times1=1\), so the factor is 1: nothing fades.

answers at a glance: (a) \(1/64\approx0.0156\). (b) \(1/4096\approx0.000244\). (c) 1.

Remember

Blame through \(k\) layers is a product of \(k\) factors. Keep each factor near 1.

Problem 12mediumdead neurons

A ReLU layer of 3 neurons sees a batch of 4 inputs \(\mathbf x_1=(1,0)\), \(\mathbf x_2=(2,1)\), \(\mathbf x_3=(0,1)\), \(\mathbf x_4=(1,1)\). Its pre-activations (rows = neurons, columns = inputs) and the incoming blames \(\partial L/\partial a\) are \[Z=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0.5&3\\ -3&-0.2&-1&-4\\ -1&-2&1&-0.5\end{pmatrix},\quad G=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&1\\ 0.5&-0.5&2&1\\ 0.4&-0.2&0.6&1\end{pmatrix}.\] (a) Which neuron is dead on this batch? (b) Find each neuron's bias gradient, summed over the batch. (c) Find each neuron's weight gradients, summed over the batch.

What this tests. Rule A per input, then Rule B summed over a batch. Plan. Mask \(G\) by the signs of \(Z\); sum rows for the biases; for the weights add (masked blame) × input.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the switches. Neuron 1 is awake on inputs 1, 3, 4. Neuron 2 is asleep on every input — dead. Neuron 3 is awake only on input 3.
Step 2 — masked blame. Neuron 1: \((1,0,1,1)\). Neuron 2: \((0,0,0,0)\). Neuron 3: \((0,0,0.6,0)\).
Step 3 — biases (b). Sum each row: \(3,\ 0,\ 0.6\).
Step 4 — weights (c). Neuron 1: \(1\cdot(1,0)+1\cdot(0,1)+1\cdot(1,1)=(2,2)\). Neuron 2: \((0,0)\). Neuron 3: \(0.6\cdot(0,1)=(0,0.6)\).

answers at a glance: (a) neuron 2. (b) \((3,\ 0,\ 0.6)\). (c) \((2,2)\), \((0,0)\), \((0,0.6)\).

Remember

A neuron asleep on the whole batch gets exactly zero gradient, so plain gradient descent can never wake it. That is a dead ReLU.

Problem 13mediumwide or deep

Two ReLU networks with one input and one output. A: one hidden layer of 16 neurons (1 → 16 → 1). B: five hidden layers of 2 neurons (1 → 2 → 2 → 2 → 2 → 2 → 1). (a) Count each network's numbers. (b) At most how many straight pieces can A make? (c) Using the tent fold, how many can B make?

What this tests. Parameter counting, and "width adds creases, depth folds the folds". Plan. Count per layer; A has one crease per neuron; B doubles per layer.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — count A. \(16\) weights + \(16\) biases in, \(16\) weights + \(1\) bias out: \(49\).
Step 2 — count B. First layer \(2+2=4\). Four \(2\to2\) layers, \(4+2=6\) each: \(24\). Output \(2+1=3\). Total \(31\).
Step 3 — pieces of A. 16 creases cut the line into at most \(17\) straight pieces.
Step 4 — pieces of B. Each layer of 2 ReLUs makes one tent, \(t(x)=2\operatorname{ReLU}(x)-4\operatorname{ReLU}(x-\tfrac12)\), and each tent doubles the pieces: \(2^5=32\).

answers at a glance: (a) A: 49, B: 31. (b) 17. (c) 32.

Remember

Width adds pieces one crease at a time. Depth multiplies them. That is one reason deep networks are worth their trouble.

Problem 14harddiagnose the run

The salary network of this unit was trained three times from the same start with three step sizes. The recorded losses:
\(\eta\)step 0step 1step 2step 3step 10
\(10^{-5}\)5018.897.072.620.0024
\(10^{-4}\)50428.811078.76841.740.0157
\(2\times10^{-3}\)50624412125012501250
(a) Which run would you trust, and why? (b) What is \(\hat y\) in the third run from step 2 on? (c) Why can the third run never recover? (d) What is the real fix?

What this tests. Reading a loss curve like a doctor (Unit 10), and connecting it to dead ReLUs. Plan. Look for monotone falls, jumps, and a loss that freezes at a suspicious value.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(\eta=10^{-5}\): the loss falls every step, by a factor of about 2.7 each time. The \(10^{-4}\) run jumps up to 429 and then 1079 — it overshoots and bounces; it happens to settle by step 10, but you would not bet on it. Trust \(10^{-5}\).
Step 2 — (b). A loss of exactly 1250 means \(\tfrac12(\hat y-50)^2=1250\), so \(\hat y=0\) or \(\hat y=100\). If it were 100, the blame \(\hat y-y=+50\) would move the weights and the loss would change. It is frozen, so nothing is moving: \(\hat y=0\).
Step 3 — (c). The giant first step (loss 624 412, \(\hat y\approx1168\)) pushed the first-layer weights so far that every hidden-1 neuron now has a negative \(z\): all asleep. Rule A blocks all blame, every gradient before them is 0, and \(\hat y\) stays 0. The network is dead.
Step 4 — (d). Scale the inputs (age/100, experience/10). Then the first-layer gradients are no longer 30 times inflated, and a much larger \(\eta\) (like \(5\times10^{-4}\)) trains smoothly.

answers at a glance: (a) \(\eta=10^{-5}\) (steady fall). (b) \(\hat y=0\). (c) every first-layer neuron is asleep, so all gradients are 0. (d) scale the inputs.

Remember

A loss frozen at a round number is a clue. \(\tfrac12y^2\) means the network is outputting 0 — often because its ReLUs have died.

The network gets its first real job: reading. Each word becomes a list of numbers — a vector — and a softmax over the whole vocabulary predicts the next word. The blame at that softmax is prediction minus truth again. And meaning turns out to be a direction: the arrow from "man" to "woman" points the same way as the arrow from "king" to "queen".
