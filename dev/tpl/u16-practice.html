Practice arena — sixteen problems, solved in full

Sixteen problems, easy to hard, one or more for every idea of the unit: chains of guesses, smoothing, perplexity, TF-IDF, PMI, cosines, the SVD squeeze, counting training examples, a CBOW pass, a skip-gram pass, a negative-sampling step, a path down a tree, subsampling, GloVe ratios, BPE merges and an analogy. Some run forwards, some backwards (find the setting that gives a result), and one asks you to judge two recorded models. Every number was checked by machine.

Three habits do most of the work. Write the counts or vectors in a small table first. Turn probabilities into surprises before you average them. And for "similar", divide by the lengths: use the cosine, not the raw dot product.

Problem 1easya chain of guesses

The corpus is "we play cricket", "we play football", "we watch cricket", and a bigram model adds \(\langle s\rangle\) and \(\langle/s\rangle\) to every sentence. (a) Give \(P(\text{we}\mid\langle s\rangle)\), \(P(\text{play}\mid\text{we})\), \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{play})\) and \(P(\langle/s\rangle\mid\text{cricket})\). (b) What chance does the model give \(\langle s\rangle\) we play cricket \(\langle/s\rangle\)? (c) And \(\langle s\rangle\) we watch football \(\langle/s\rangle\)? (d) List every sentence the model can produce with a chance above 0, and add up their chances.

What this tests. The chain rule with a one-word memory, count ÷ total, and why the end marker makes the chances of all sentences add up to 1. Plan. Make the table of next-word counts for every history, then multiply along each sentence.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the counts. After \(\langle s\rangle\): we 3. After we: play 2, watch 1. After play: cricket 1, football 1. After watch: cricket 1. After cricket: \(\langle/s\rangle\) 2. After football: \(\langle/s\rangle\) 1.
Step 2 — (a). \(P(\text{we}\mid\langle s\rangle)=3/3=1\), \(P(\text{play}\mid\text{we})=2/3\), \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{play})=1/2\), \(P(\langle/s\rangle\mid\text{cricket})=2/2=1\).
Step 3 — (b). Multiply along the chain: \(1\cdot\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12\cdot1=\tfrac13\).
Step 4 — (c). "watch" was only ever followed by cricket, so \(P(\text{football}\mid\text{watch})=0\), and one zero makes the whole sentence 0.
Step 5 — (d). From "we" the chain can go to play or watch; from play to cricket or football; from watch only to cricket; then it must stop. Three sentences: we play cricket (\(\tfrac13\)), we play football (\(1\cdot\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12\cdot1=\tfrac13\)), we watch cricket (\(1\cdot\tfrac13\cdot1\cdot1=\tfrac13\)). Total: 1.

answers at a glance: (a) 1, \(2/3\), \(1/2\), 1. (b) \(1/3\). (c) 0. (d) three sentences, \(1/3\) each, total 1.

Remember

With the end marker, a bigram model shares out exactly 1 among all the sentences it can write.

Problem 2mediumsmoothing

Same corpus as Problem 1. The possible next words are we, play, watch, cricket, football and \(\langle/s\rangle\) (\(V=6\)). (a) With add-one smoothing, give \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{play})\) and \(P(\text{watch}\mid\text{play})\). (b) The unigram adviser counts the 12 tokens that follow the \(\langle s\rangle\) markers. With interpolation \(0.7\,P_{\text{bigram}}+0.3\,P_{\text{unigram}}\), give the same two chances. (c) Which weight \(\lambda\) on the bigram would make \(P(\text{watch}\mid\text{play})\) exactly 0.05? (d) Show that the interpolated row for "play" adds up to 1 whatever \(\lambda\) is.

What this tests. Add-one, interpolation, and running interpolation backwards. Plan. Write the "play" row (cricket 1, football 1, total 2) and the unigram counts first.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). Add 1 to each count and \(V=6\) to the total: \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{play})=\tfrac{1+1}{2+6}=0.25\), \(P(\text{watch}\mid\text{play})=\tfrac{0+1}{2+6}=0.125\).
Step 2 — the unigram counts. we 3, play 2, watch 1, cricket 2, football 1, \(\langle/s\rangle\) 3: 12 tokens.
Step 3 — (b). \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{play})=0.7\cdot\tfrac12+0.3\cdot\tfrac2{12}=0.35+0.05=0.4\). \(P(\text{watch}\mid\text{play})=0.7\cdot0+0.3\cdot\tfrac1{12}=0.025\).
Step 4 — (c). Only the unigram part helps watch: \((1-\lambda)\cdot\tfrac1{12}=0.05\), so \(1-\lambda=0.6\) and \(\lambda=0.4\).
Step 5 — (d). The bigram row adds to 1 and the unigram row adds to 1, so the blend adds to \(\lambda\cdot1+(1-\lambda)\cdot1=1\).

answers at a glance: (a) 0.25 and 0.125. (b) 0.4 and 0.025. (c) \(\lambda=0.4\). (d) \(\lambda+(1-\lambda)=1\).

Remember

Interpolation gives an unseen pair the unigram's share, scaled by \(1-\lambda\): more trust in the general adviser, more chance for the unseen.

Problem 3mediumperplexity, both ways

(a) A model gave five real next words the probabilities \(0.25,\ 0.5,\ 0.125,\ 0.5,\ 1\). Find the average surprise in bits and the perplexity. (b) What is the perplexity of a model that guesses evenly over 8 words? (c) On a 3-word text a model gave the first two true words 0.5 and 0.25. What must it give the third for the perplexity to be exactly 4? (d) Two models were each scored twice: on their own training text and on new text they had never seen. A scored 45 on its training text and 210 on the new text; B scored 80 and 95. Which would you use, and how many bits per word better is it on the new text?

What this tests. Perplexity as \(2^{\text{average surprise}}=(p_1\cdots p_N)^{-1/N}\), forwards, backwards, and as a judge (Unit 10's overfitting). Plan. Work in bits: \(\log_2(1/p)\) for each word.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). Surprises: 2, 1, 3, 1, 0 bits. Average \(7/5=1.4\) bits. Perplexity \(2^{1.4}\approx2.639\).
Step 2 — (b). Every word gets \(1/8\): perplexity 8, a fair die with 8 faces.
Step 3 — (c). \((0.5\cdot0.25\cdot p)^{-1/3}=4\) means \(0.125\,p=4^{-3}=\tfrac1{64}\), so \(p=\tfrac18=0.125\). Check: surprises 1, 2, 3; average 2; \(2^2=4\).
Step 4 — (d). Judge on new text only: B (95 < 210). In bits: \(\log_2 210\approx7.714\) and \(\log_2 95\approx6.570\), so B is \(\log_2(210/95)\approx1.144\) bits per word better. A's big jump from 45 to 210 is the sign of memorising.

answers at a glance: (a) 1.4 bits; \(2^{1.4}\approx2.639\). (b) 8. (c) \(p=0.125\). (d) B; about 1.144 bits per word.

Remember

A model that always gives the truth probability \(p\) has perplexity \(1/p\); and only the score on text the model never saw counts.

Problem 4easyTF-IDF

Four documents. D1: the ×4, chai ×3, milk ×1. D2: the ×3, cricket ×2, bat ×1. D3: the ×2, chai ×1, cricket ×1. D4: the ×1, milk ×1, kadak ×2. (a) Give the document frequency and \(\text{idf}=\log_{10}(N/\text{df})\) of the, chai, milk, cricket, bat and kadak. (b) Give the TF-IDF weights of the, chai and milk in D1. (c) Which word is the best keyword for D4? (d) A new word has idf \(\approx0.125\). In how many of the 4 documents does it appear?

What this tests. TF-IDF, and reading idf backwards. Plan. Count documents, not copies, for df; then multiply by the counts.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). the: in all 4, idf \(\log_{10}1=0\). chai, milk, cricket: in 2 each, idf \(\log_{10}2\approx0.301\). bat, kadak: in 1 each, idf \(\log_{10}4\approx0.602\).
Step 2 — (b). In D1: the \(4\times0=0\), chai \(3\times0.301\approx0.903\), milk \(1\times0.301\approx0.301\).
Step 3 — (c). In D4: the 0, milk \(1\times0.301=0.301\), kadak \(2\times0.602\approx1.204\). Kadak is the best keyword.
Step 4 — (d). \(\log_{10}(4/\text{df})=0.125\) gives \(4/\text{df}\approx1.334\), so \(\text{df}=3\) (indeed \(\log_{10}(4/3)\approx0.125\)).

answers at a glance: (a) df 4, 2, 2, 2, 1, 1; idf 0, 0.301, 0.301, 0.301, 0.602, 0.602. (b) 0, 0.903, 0.301. (c) kadak (1.204). (d) 3 documents.

Remember

A word everywhere has idf 0; a word in one document out of \(N\) has the biggest idf, \(\log_{10}N\).

Problem 5mediumPMI

Counted pairs (\(N=60\)): tea meets hot 15, the 12, over 3 times; bat meets hot 3, the 12, over 15 times. (a) Give the row and column totals and the "if strangers" count, row total × column total ÷ \(N\), for tea with each word. (b) Give \(\operatorname{PMI}(\text{tea},c)\) for c = hot, the, over. (c) Give the PPMI of each. (d) Keeping the "if strangers" count for tea–hot fixed, how many tea–hot meetings would make its PMI exactly 1?

What this tests. PMI as the log of count ÷ chance. Plan. Totals first, then one ratio per box.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). Rows: tea 30, bat 30. Columns: hot 18, the 24, over 18. If strangers: tea–hot \(30\cdot18/60=9\), tea–the \(30\cdot24/60=12\), tea–over \(9\).
Step 2 — (b). \(\log_2(15/9)=\log_2(5/3)\approx0.737\); \(\log_2(12/12)=0\); \(\log_2(3/9)=\log_2(1/3)\approx-1.585\).
Step 3 — (c). Keep the positive part: 0.737, 0 and 0.
Step 4 — (d). PMI 1 means a ratio of \(2^1=2\): \(2\times9=18\) meetings.

answers at a glance: (a) rows 30, 30; columns 18, 24, 18; strangers 9, 12, 9. (b) 0.737, 0, −1.585. (c) 0.737, 0, 0. (d) 18.

Remember

PMI counts meetings against chance: 0 means "no more than strangers", and "the" usually scores 0 with everyone.

Problem 6easycosine of counts

Count rows over the neighbours (drink, hot, play): tea \(=(3,4,0)\), coffee \(=(6,8,0)\), cricket \(=(0,3,4)\). (a) Compute cos(tea, coffee) and cos(tea, cricket). (b) Compute the straight-line distances tea–coffee and tea–cricket. (c) Which measure clearly says tea is closer to coffee, and why?

What this tests. Cosine against distance on count rows. Plan. Lengths first: \(\lVert(3,4,0)\rVert=5\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — lengths. tea 5, coffee 10, cricket 5.
Step 2 — (a). tea·coffee \(=18+32=50\), so cos \(=50/(5\cdot10)=1\). tea·cricket \(=0+12+0=12\), so cos \(=12/25=0.48\).
Step 3 — (b). tea − coffee \(=(-3,-4,0)\): distance 5. tea − cricket \(=(3,1,-4)\): distance \(\sqrt{26}\approx5.099\).
Step 4 — (c). The cosine (1 against 0.48). Distance barely prefers coffee (5 against 5.099), because coffee is just "tea, counted twice as often", and distance is fooled by that size.

answers at a glance: (a) 1 and 0.48. (b) 5 and \(\sqrt{26}\approx5.099\). (c) the cosine, decisively.

Remember

Counts grow with how common a word is; the cosine divides that away.

Problem 7mediumthe SVD squeeze

(a) Find the singular values of \(A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\ 1&3\end{pmatrix}\) and check that \(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2\) equals the sum of the squares of the entries. (b) A company table has singular values \(6,\ 4,\ 2,\ 1\). What share of the energy do \(k=1\) and \(k=2\) keep? (c) What is the smallest \(k\) that keeps at least 90%? (d) What is the rank-2 rebuild error?

What this tests. Energy \(=\sum\sigma_i^2\), and the error of keeping \(k\). Plan. For a symmetric matrix with positive eigenvalues, the singular values are the eigenvalues.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(A\) is symmetric; its eigenvalues solve \((3-\lambda)^2=1\): \(\lambda=4,2\). Both positive, so \(\sigma=(4,2)\). Check: \(16+4=20=9+1+1+9\).
Step 2 — (b). Total \(36+16+4+1=57\). \(k=1\): \(36/57\approx63.2\%\). \(k=2\): \(52/57\approx91.2\%\).
Step 3 — (c). \(k=1\) is below 90% and \(k=2\) is above, so \(k=2\).
Step 4 — (d). \(\sqrt{\sigma_3^2+\sigma_4^2}=\sqrt{4+1}=\sqrt5\approx2.236\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(\sigma=(4,2)\); \(16+4=20\). (b) 63.2% and 91.2%. (c) \(k=2\). (d) \(\sqrt5\approx2.236\).

Remember

Square the singular values before you add them — energy lives in \(\sigma^2\).

Problem 8mediumcounting examples

(a) A 6-word sentence, window \(C=2\): how many examples does CBOW make, and how many pairs does skip-gram make? (b) Find a formula for the number of skip-gram pairs from a sentence of \(n\) words (\(n\ge C+1\)). (c) Apply it to \(n=10\), \(C=3\). (d) With \(C=2\), a sentence gave 34 skip-gram pairs. How many words did it have? (e) In a very long text, about how many times more guesses does skip-gram make than CBOW?

What this tests. The cost difference between the two games, as a general formula. Plan. Count the neighbours of each position, left and right separately.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). CBOW: one example per word, 6. Skip-gram: \(2+3+4+4+3+2=18\).
Step 2 — (b). The left neighbours add up to \(0+1+\dots+C\) for the first \(C+1\) positions and \(C\) for each of the other \(n-1-C\): \(\tfrac12C(C+1)+C(n-1-C)=Cn-\tfrac12C(C+1)\). The right side gives the same, so the total is \(2Cn-C(C+1)\).
Step 3 — (c). \(2\cdot3\cdot10-3\cdot4=60-12=48\) pairs (CBOW: 10).
Step 4 — (d). \(4n-6=34\), so \(n=10\).
Step 5 — (e). For large \(n\), \((2Cn-C(C+1))/n\to2C\).

answers at a glance: (a) 6 and 18. (b) \(2Cn-C(C+1)\). (c) 48 (CBOW 10). (d) 10 words. (e) about \(2C\).

Remember

Skip-gram makes about \(2C\) guesses for every one CBOW makes — that is the whole price of home tuition.

Problem 9hardone CBOW round

A vocabulary of four words with \(d=2\). Input vectors: tea \((1,0)\), hot \((1,1)\), cup \((0,1)\), bat \((-1,0)\). Output vectors: tea \((1,1)\), hot \((0,1)\), cup \((1,0)\), bat \((-1,-1)\). The sentence is "hot tea cup", window 1, and the blank is tea. (a) Find \(\mathbf h\). (b) Find the four scores and the softmax. (c) Find the loss. (d) Find \(\mathbf e=\mathbf p-\mathbf y\) and the blame on \(\mathbf h\), \(\sum_w e_w\mathbf u_w\). (e) With \(\eta=1\), how far does each context vector move?

What this tests. The whole CBOW round: average, score, softmax, prediction − truth, and the equal share of the blame. Plan. Keep four decimals; do the output side before the input side.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(\mathbf h=\big((1,1)+(0,1)\big)/2=(0.5,\ 1)\).
Step 2 — (b). Scores \(\mathbf u_w\cdot\mathbf h\): tea 1.5, hot 1, cup 0.5, bat −1.5. \(e^{s}\): 4.4817, 2.7183, 1.6487, 0.2231, total 9.0718. Softmax: 0.4940, 0.2996, 0.1817, 0.0246.
Step 3 — (c). \(L=-\ln0.4940\approx0.7052\).
Step 4 — (d). \(\mathbf e=(-0.5060,\ 0.2996,\ 0.1817,\ 0.0246)\). Blame on \(\mathbf h\): \(-0.5060(1,1)+0.2996(0,1)+0.1817(1,0)+0.0246(-1,-1)=(-0.3488,\ -0.2309)\).
Step 5 — (e). Each of the two context vectors moves by \(-\eta\cdot\tfrac12(-0.3488,-0.2309)=(0.1744,\ 0.1155)\): toward tea's output vector. (If you also update the output vectors and play the blank again, tea's chance rises to 0.7742.)

answers at a glance: (a) \((0.5,1)\). (b) scores 1.5, 1, 0.5, −1.5; softmax 0.4940, 0.2996, 0.1817, 0.0246. (c) 0.7052. (d) \(\mathbf e=(-0.5060,0.2996,0.1817,0.0246)\); blame \((-0.3488,-0.2309)\). (e) \((0.1744,\ 0.1155)\) each.

Remember

The committee shares the blame equally: with \(2C\) members, each gets \(\tfrac1{2C}\) of it.

Problem 10hardone skip-gram round

The same four words and vectors as Problem 9. Now tea is the centre word and must guess its two neighbours, hot and cup. (a) Find \(\mathbf h\) and the softmax. (b) Find the loss. (c) Find the summed error \(\mathbf E=2\mathbf p-\mathbf y_{\text{hot}}-\mathbf y_{\text{cup}}\) and the blame on the centre word. (d) With \(\eta=1\), what is tea's new input vector? (e) What is the lowest loss skip-gram could ever reach at this position?

What this tests. One softmax checked against two neighbours, errors that add up, and the floor of \(2\ln2\). Plan. The centre's own input vector is \(\mathbf h\) — no averaging.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(\mathbf h=\mathbf v_{\text{tea}}=(1,0)\). Scores: tea 1, hot 0, cup 1, bat −1. Softmax: 0.3995, 0.1470, 0.3995, 0.0541.
Step 2 — (b). \(L=-\ln0.1470-\ln0.3995\approx1.9176+0.9176=2.8352\).
Step 3 — (c). \(\mathbf E=(0.7990,\ -0.7061,\ -0.2010,\ 0.1081)\). Blame on the centre: \(0.7990(1,1)-0.7061(0,1)-0.2010(1,0)+0.1081(-1,-1)=(0.4898,\ -0.0152)\).
Step 4 — (d). \(\mathbf v_{\text{tea}}\leftarrow(1,0)-(0.4898,-0.0152)=(0.5102,\ 0.0152)\). The whole blame goes to the centre word.
Step 5 — (e). One list must share its chances between hot and cup: the best is 0.5 each, a loss of \(2\ln2\approx1.386\).

answers at a glance: (a) \((1,0)\); 0.3995, 0.1470, 0.3995, 0.0541. (b) 2.8352. (c) \(\mathbf E=(0.7990,-0.7061,-0.2010,0.1081)\); blame \((0.4898,-0.0152)\). (d) \((0.5102,\ 0.0152)\). (e) \(2\ln2\approx1.386\).

Remember

Skip-gram's centre word takes the full sum of its neighbours' errors — nothing is divided.

Problem 11hardone negative-sampling step

\(\mathbf v=(0,1)\), real neighbour \(\mathbf u_+=(1,1)\), one negative \(\mathbf u_-=(1,-1)\), step size \(\eta=0.5\). (a) Find \(g_+=1-\sigma(\mathbf u_+\cdot\mathbf v)\) and \(g_-=\sigma(\mathbf u_-\cdot\mathbf v)\). (b) Do one step of the pull-and-push rules for all three vectors. (c) Give the new scores \(\mathbf u_\pm\cdot\mathbf v\) and the loss before and after.

What this tests. The rules \(\mathbf u_+\mathrel{+}=\eta g_+\mathbf v\), \(\mathbf u_-\mathrel{-}=\eta g_-\mathbf v\), \(\mathbf v\mathrel{+}=\eta(g_+\mathbf u_+-g_-\mathbf u_-)\), all using the OLD vectors. Plan. Compute the two "how wrong" numbers once, then update.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(\mathbf u_+\cdot\mathbf v=1\): \(g_+=1-\sigma(1)\approx0.2689\). \(\mathbf u_-\cdot\mathbf v=-1\): \(g_-=\sigma(-1)\approx0.2689\).
Step 2 — the neighbours. \(\mathbf u_+\leftarrow(1,1)+0.5(0.2689)(0,1)=(1,\ 1.1345)\). \(\mathbf u_-\leftarrow(1,-1)-0.5(0.2689)(0,1)=(1,\ -1.1345)\).
Step 3 — the centre word. \(g_+\mathbf u_+-g_-\mathbf u_-=0.2689\big((1,1)-(1,-1)\big)=(0,\ 0.5379)\), so \(\mathbf v\leftarrow(0,1)+0.5(0,0.5379)=(0,\ 1.2689)\).
Step 4 — (c). New scores: \(1.1345\times1.2689\approx1.4396\) and \(-1.4396\). Loss before: \(-2\ln\sigma(1)\approx0.6265\). After: \(-2\ln\sigma(1.4396)\approx0.4254\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(g_+=g_-\approx0.2689\). (b) \(\mathbf u_+=(1,1.1345)\), \(\mathbf u_-=(1,-1.1345)\), \(\mathbf v=(0,1.2689)\). (c) scores \(\pm1.4396\); loss 0.6265 → 0.4254.

Remember

Use the old vectors on the right-hand side of all three updates — compute first, then overwrite.

Problem 12mediumdown the tree

A hierarchical softmax with four leaves (LL, LR, RL, RR) and \(\mathbf h=(2,-1)\). The root has \(\boldsymbol\theta=(1,1)\), the left fork \((0,1)\), the right fork \((0.5,1)\); each fork sends \(\sigma(\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf h)\) to the left. (a) Find the three fork scores \(\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf h\). (b) Find the chance of each leaf. (c) Check that they add up to 1. (d) A balanced tree over 30 000 words: how many decisions per guess, and how many times less work than a full softmax?

What this tests. A leaf's chance as the product of its left/right decisions, and the \(\log_2V\) cost. Plan. Remember \(\sigma(-z)=1-\sigma(z)\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). Root: \(2-1=1\). Left fork: \(0\cdot2+1\cdot(-1)=-1\). Right fork: \(1-1=0\).
Step 2 — (b). Root left \(\sigma(1)=0.7311\), right 0.2689. LL \(=0.7311\cdot\sigma(-1)=0.7311\cdot0.2689\approx0.1966\). LR \(=0.7311\cdot0.7311\approx0.5344\). RL \(=0.2689\cdot\sigma(0)=0.1345\). RR \(=0.2689\cdot0.5=0.1345\).
Step 3 — (c). \(0.1966+0.5344+0.1345+0.1345=1\) (exactly, before rounding).
Step 4 — (d). \(2^{14}=16\,384<30\,000\le2^{15}=32\,768\), so 15 decisions; \(30\,000/15=2\,000\) times less work.

answers at a glance: (a) 1, −1, 0. (b) 0.1966, 0.5344, 0.1345, 0.1345. (c) 1. (d) 15 decisions; 2 000 times less.

Remember

Every fork splits what it receives into two parts that add up, so the leaves always add up to 1.

Problem 13mediumsubsampling

Subsampling keeps each copy of a word with frequency \(f\) with chance \(\sqrt{t/f}\) (and always if \(f\le t\)), with \(t=10^{-5}\). (a) Give the keep chances for \(f=10^{-3}\), \(f=4\times10^{-4}\) and \(f=2.5\times10^{-6}\). (b) Of 1 000 copies of the \(10^{-3}\) word, how many are kept on average? (c) In another model a word with \(f=0.01\) is kept 5% of the time. What \(t\) was used? (d) A word with \(f_1=10^{-3}\) is 100 times as common as one with \(f_2=10^{-5}\). How many times as common is it after subsampling?

What this tests. The keep rule forwards and backwards, and the square-root flattening. Plan. A kept share is \(f\sqrt{t/f}=\sqrt{tf}\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(\sqrt{10^{-5}/10^{-3}}=\sqrt{0.01}=0.1\). \(\sqrt{10^{-5}/(4\times10^{-4})}=\sqrt{0.025}\approx0.158\). \(2.5\times10^{-6}\le t\): always kept, 1.
Step 2 — (b). \(1000\times0.1=100\).
Step 3 — (c). \(\sqrt{t/0.01}=0.05\) gives \(t/0.01=0.0025\), so \(t=2.5\times10^{-5}\).
Step 4 — (d). Kept shares: \(\sqrt{t f_1}=\sqrt{10^{-8}}=10^{-4}\); the second word has \(f_2=t\), so it keeps all of its \(10^{-5}\). The ratio is \(10^{-4}/10^{-5}=10\) — down from 100.

answers at a glance: (a) 0.1, about 0.158, 1. (b) 100. (c) \(t=2.5\times10^{-5}\). (d) 10 times.

Remember

Subsampling turns a word's share \(f\) into about \(\sqrt{tf}\): common words shrink the most, rare words are untouched.

Problem 14mediumGloVe ratios

Around "ice" and around "steam", 1 000 context words were counted each. The probe solid appears 38 and 2 times, gas 1 and 19, water 60 and 60, fashion 2 and 2. (a) Give \(P(k\mid\text{ice})/P(k\mid\text{steam})\) for each probe. (b) Give the natural log of each ratio — what GloVe wants \((\mathbf w_{\text{ice}}-\mathbf w_{\text{steam}})\cdot\tilde{\mathbf w}_k\) to be. (c) Which probe vectors should end up at right angles to \(\mathbf w_{\text{ice}}-\mathbf w_{\text{steam}}\)? (d) Give GloVe's weights \(f(x)=(x/100)^{3/4}\) (1 above 100) for \(x=38\), 2, 60 and 150.

What this tests. Meaning in ratios, and why rare counts get small weights. Plan. Both targets have 1 000 contexts, so the ratio of probabilities is the ratio of counts.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). solid \(38/2=19\), gas \(1/19\approx0.0526\), water \(60/60=1\), fashion \(2/2=1\).
Step 2 — (b). \(\ln19\approx2.944\), \(\ln(1/19)\approx-2.944\), \(\ln1=0\), \(\ln1=0\).
Step 3 — (c). Water and fashion: their target dot product is 0. Water is shared by both, fashion by neither — either way it cannot tell ice from steam.
Step 4 — (d). \(0.38^{0.75}\approx0.484\), \(0.02^{0.75}\approx0.0532\), \(0.6^{0.75}\approx0.682\), and 1 for 150.

answers at a glance: (a) 19, 0.0526, 1, 1. (b) 2.944, −2.944, 0, 0. (c) water and fashion. (d) 0.484, 0.0532, 0.682, 1.

Remember

A probe that tells two words apart gives a ratio far from 1; one that doesn't gives 1, and its arrow is at right angles to their difference.

Problem 15hardBPE by hand

A corpus: low ×5, lower ×2, newest ×6, widest ×3. Run byte-pair encoding from single letters; when two pairs tie, merge the one met first reading the corpus in order. (a) Count the neighbouring pairs and find the first merge. (b) Find the next three merges. (c) Cut "lowest" and "slow" with these four merges. (d) How many symbols does the whole corpus have (counting repeats) before any merge, and after the four merges?

What this tests. The BPE loop — count, merge the most frequent pair, repeat — and the tie rule. Plan. Multiply every pair by its word's count; recount after each merge.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). e+s: 6 (newest) + 3 (widest) = 9; s+t: 9 as well; w+e: 2 + 6 = 8; l+o and o+w: 7 each; n+e, e+w: 6; w+i, i+d, d+e: 3; e+r: 2. The top two tie at 9, and e+s is met first (in "newest"), so merge 1 is e+s → es (9).
Step 2 — (b). Now es+t = 9 is alone at the top: merge 2 es+t → est (9). Then l+o and o+w tie at 7, and l+o comes first: merge 3 l+o → lo (7). Then lo+w = 7: merge 4 lo+w → low (7).
Step 3 — (c). "lowest": l o w e s t → (es) → (est) → (lo) → (low): low · est. "slow": s l o w → s · low.
Step 4 — (d). Before: \(3\cdot5+5\cdot2+6\cdot6+6\cdot3=79\) letters. After: low (1 symbol) ×5, low e r (3) ×2, n e w est (4) ×6, w i d est (4) ×3: \(5+6+24+12=47\).

answers at a glance: (a) e+s (9, tie with s+t broken by order). (b) es+t (9), l+o (7), lo+w (7). (c) low · est; s · low. (d) 79 → 47.

Remember

BPE turns frequent chunks into single tokens and spells everything else from pieces — so the text gets shorter and no word is ever unknown.

Problem 16mediumanalogy

Toy vectors: India \(=(1,0,3)\), Delhi \(=(1,1.2,3)\), Japan \(=(3,0,1)\), Tokyo \(=(3,1,1)\), Kyoto \(=(3,1,2)\). (a) Compute \(\mathbf q=\) Delhi − India + Japan. (b) Compute the cosine of \(\mathbf q\) with Tokyo, Kyoto, Japan and Delhi. (c) Which word does the search return (skipping the inputs)? (d) Would the answer change if the inputs were allowed?

What this tests. Analogy arithmetic and the cosine search. Plan. Add first, then one cosine per candidate.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(\mathbf q=(1-1+3,\ 1.2-0+0,\ 3-3+1)=(3,\ 1.2,\ 1)\); \(\lVert\mathbf q\rVert=\sqrt{11.44}\approx3.382\).
Step 2 — (b). Tokyo: \(11.2/(3.382\cdot\sqrt{11})\approx0.9984\). Kyoto: \(12.2/(3.382\cdot\sqrt{14})\approx0.9640\). Japan: \(10/(3.382\cdot\sqrt{10})\approx0.9349\). Delhi: \(7.44/(3.382\cdot\sqrt{11.44})\approx0.6503\).
Step 3 — (c). Tokyo, with 0.9984.
Step 4 — (d). No: the inputs Japan (0.9349) and Delhi (0.6503) score below Tokyo here. (In real trained vectors they often would not.)

answers at a glance: (a) \((3,1.2,1)\). (b) 0.9984, 0.9640, 0.9349, 0.6503. (c) Tokyo. (d) no.

Remember

The answer to \(A-B+C\) is a point, not a word; the nearest word by cosine is the answer.
