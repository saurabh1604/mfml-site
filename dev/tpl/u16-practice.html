Practice arena — fourteen problems, solved in full

Fourteen problems, easy to hard: one-hot lists, counting bigrams, perplexity forwards and backwards, cosines of count rows, singular values and a rank-1 copy, word2vec scores, a word2vec step, the cost of a softmax, an analogy, an embedding lookup, the size of a model, and two recorded models to judge. Every number here was checked by machine.

Three habits do most of the work. Write the counts or vectors in a small table first. Turn probabilities into surprises (bits) before averaging — never average the probabilities themselves. And for "similar", divide by the lengths: use the cosine, not the raw dot product.

Problem 1easyone-hot

A vocabulary has 5 words, in the order (tea, milk, bat, ball, run). (a) Write the one-hot vector of "bat". (b) Compute \(\mathbf e_{\text{milk}}\cdot\mathbf e_{\text{ball}}\) and \(\mathbf e_{\text{bat}}\cdot\mathbf e_{\text{bat}}\). (c) How far apart are "tea" and "run"? (d) With a vocabulary of 20 000 words, how many numbers does one one-hot vector hold, compared with a 300-number embedding?

What this tests. What a one-hot list is, and why it carries no meaning. Plan. One 1 in the word's own slot; dot products count shared slots.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). "bat" is word 3: \(\mathbf e_{\text{bat}}=(0,0,1,0,0)\).
Step 2 — (b). milk and ball have their 1s in different slots (2 and 4), so every product is 0: \(\mathbf e_{\text{milk}}\cdot\mathbf e_{\text{ball}}=0\). A word with itself shares its one slot: \(\mathbf e_{\text{bat}}\cdot\mathbf e_{\text{bat}}=1\).
Step 3 — (c). The difference is \((1,0,0,0,-1)\), so the distance is \(\sqrt{1+1}=\sqrt2\approx1.414\) — the same for every pair.
Step 4 — (d). One-hot: 20 000 numbers (19 999 of them zero). Embedding: 300 numbers, all useful. That is \(20\,000/300\approx66.7\) times shorter.

answers at a glance: (a) \((0,0,1,0,0)\). (b) 0 and 1. (c) \(\sqrt2\approx1.414\). (d) 20 000 against 300.

Remember

One-hot lists are all at right angles and all \(\sqrt2\) apart. They are names, not meanings.

Problem 2easybigrams

The corpus is "we play cricket .  we play football .  we watch cricket ." (every sentence starts with "we"; the full stop is a word). (a) Give \(P(\text{play}\mid\text{we})\), \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{play})\) and \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{watch})\). (b) What probability does the bigram model give "we play cricket ."? (c) With add-one smoothing over the 6-word vocabulary, give \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{play})\), \(P(\text{watch}\mid\text{play})\) and \(P(.\mid\text{cricket})\). (d) The smoothed probability of "we play cricket ."?

What this tests. Counting pairs, dividing by the row total, and add-one. Plan. Make the table of next-word counts first.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the counts. After "we": play 2, watch 1 (total 3). After "play": cricket 1, football 1 (total 2). After "watch": cricket 1 (total 1). After "cricket": "." 2 (total 2). After "football": "." 1.
Step 2 — (a). \(P(\text{play}\mid\text{we})=2/3\), \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{play})=1/2\), \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{watch})=1/1=1\).
Step 3 — (b). \(\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12\cdot P(.\mid\text{cricket})=\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12\cdot1=\tfrac13\).
Step 4 — (c). Add 1 to the count and \(V=6\) to the total: \(P(\text{cricket}\mid\text{play})=\tfrac{1+1}{2+6}=0.25\), \(P(\text{watch}\mid\text{play})=\tfrac{0+1}{2+6}=0.125\), \(P(.\mid\text{cricket})=\tfrac{2+1}{2+6}=0.375\).
Step 5 — (d). \(P(\text{play}\mid\text{we})=\tfrac{2+1}{3+6}=\tfrac13\), so the sentence gets \(\tfrac13\cdot0.25\cdot0.375=0.03125\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(2/3\), \(1/2\), \(1\). (b) \(1/3\). (c) 0.25, 0.125, 0.375. (d) 0.03125.

Remember

Smoothing moves probability from seen pairs to unseen ones — the seen sentence becomes less likely, but nothing is impossible any more.

Problem 3easyperplexity

A model reads five words. It gave the words that really came next the probabilities \(0.25,\ 0.5,\ 0.125,\ 0.5,\ 1\). (a) Find each surprise in bits and the average. (b) Find the perplexity. (c) Redo (a)–(b) in nats (natural logs, then \(e^{(\cdot)}\)) and check the perplexity is the same.

What this tests. Surprise, average surprise, perplexity. Plan. \(\log_2(1/p)\) for each word; average; raise 2 to it.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — surprises. \(\log_2 4=2\), \(\log_2 2=1\), \(\log_2 8=3\), \(1\), \(\log_2 1=0\).
Step 2 — average. \((2+1+3+1+0)/5=7/5=1.4\) bits.
Step 3 — perplexity. \(2^{1.4}\approx2.639\).
Step 4 — in nats. The average of \(\ln(1/p)\) is \(1.4\ln2\approx0.9704\) nats, and \(e^{0.9704}\approx2.639\). The same.

answers at a glance: (a) 2, 1, 3, 1, 0 bits; average 1.4 bits. (b) \(2^{1.4}\approx2.639\). (c) 0.9704 nats, \(e^{0.9704}\approx2.639\).

Remember

Perplexity does not depend on the base of the log — bits with 2, nats with \(e\).

Problem 4mediumperplexity, backwards

(a) A model guesses evenly over a vocabulary of 8 words. What is its perplexity? (b) A model always gives the true next word probability 0.2. Its perplexity? (c) On a 3-word text a model gave the first two true words probabilities 0.5 and 0.25. What probability must it give the third word for the perplexity to be exactly 4?

What this tests. Perplexity as \((p_1\cdots p_N)^{-1/N}\), and running it backwards. Plan. Write the product, set it equal, solve.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). Every word gets \(1/8\): perplexity \(8\).
Step 2 — (b). \((0.2^N)^{-1/N}=1/0.2=5\).
Step 3 — (c), set up. \((0.5\cdot0.25\cdot p)^{-1/3}=4\) means \(0.125\,p=4^{-3}=1/64\).
Step 4 — (c), solve. \(p=\dfrac{1/64}{1/8}=\dfrac18=0.125\). Check: surprises 1, 2, 3 bits, average 2, and \(2^2=4\).

answers at a glance: (a) 8. (b) 5. (c) \(p=0.125\).

Remember

A model that always gives the truth probability \(p\) has perplexity \(1/p\).

Problem 5easycosine of counts

Count rows over the neighbours (drink, hot, play): tea \(=(3,4,0)\), coffee \(=(6,8,0)\), cricket \(=(0,3,4)\). (a) Compute cos(tea, coffee) and cos(tea, cricket). (b) Compute the straight-line distances tea–coffee and tea–cricket. (c) Which measure says tea is closer to coffee?

What this tests. Cosine against distance on count rows. Plan. Lengths first: \(\lVert(3,4,0)\rVert=5\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — lengths. tea 5, coffee 10, cricket 5.
Step 2 — (a). tea·coffee \(=18+32=50\), so cos \(=50/(5\cdot10)=1\). tea·cricket \(=0+12+0=12\), so cos \(=12/25=0.48\).
Step 3 — (b). tea − coffee \(=(-3,-4,0)\): distance 5. tea − cricket \(=(3,1,-4)\): distance \(\sqrt{26}\approx5.099\).
Step 4 — (c). The cosine clearly says coffee (1 against 0.48). The distance barely prefers coffee (5 against 5.099) — coffee is just "tea, twice as often", and distance is confused by that size.

answers at a glance: (a) 1 and 0.48. (b) 5 and \(\sqrt{26}\approx5.099\). (c) the cosine, decisively.

Remember

Counts grow with how common a word is; the cosine divides that away.

Problem 6mediumsingular values

(a) Find the singular values of \(A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\ 1&3\end{pmatrix}\) and check that \(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2\) equals the sum of the squares of the entries. (b) A co-occurrence table has singular values \(6,\ 4,\ 2,\ 1\). What share of the energy do \(k=1\) and \(k=2\) keep? (c) What is the smallest \(k\) that keeps at least 90%? (d) What is the rank-2 rebuild error?

What this tests. Energy \(=\sum\sigma_i^2\), and the error of keeping \(k\). Plan. For a symmetric matrix with positive eigenvalues, the singular values are the eigenvalues.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(A\) is symmetric; its eigenvalues solve \((3-\lambda)^2=1\): \(\lambda=4,2\). Both positive, so \(\sigma=(4,2)\). Check: \(16+4=20=9+1+1+9\).
Step 2 — (b). Total \(36+16+4+1=57\). \(k=1\): \(36/57\approx63.2\%\). \(k=2\): \(52/57\approx91.2\%\).
Step 3 — (c). \(k=1\) is below 90%, \(k=2\) is above: the answer is 2.
Step 4 — (d). \(\sqrt{\sigma_3^2+\sigma_4^2}=\sqrt{4+1}=\sqrt5\approx2.236\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(\sigma=(4,2)\); \(16+4=20\). (b) 63.2% and 91.2%. (c) \(k=2\). (d) \(\sqrt5\approx2.236\).

Remember

Square the singular values before you add them — energy lives in \(\sigma^2\).

Problem 7mediumrank-1 copy

For \(A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\ 1&3\end{pmatrix}\) from Problem 6: (a) give the top singular vectors \(\mathbf u_1=\mathbf v_1\). (b) Build the best rank-1 copy \(A_1=\sigma_1\mathbf u_1\mathbf v_1^{\mathsf T}\). (c) Compute \(A-A_1\) and its Frobenius size, and compare with \(\sigma_2\). (d) Treat the rows of \(A\) as two word vectors. What single coordinate does each word get from \(U_1\Sigma_1\)?

What this tests. Building a low-rank copy by hand. Plan. The eigenvector for 4 solves \((A-4I)\mathbf x=0\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \((A-4I)\mathbf x=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\ 1&-1\end{pmatrix}\mathbf x=0\) gives \(\mathbf x\propto(1,1)\), so \(\mathbf u_1=\mathbf v_1=(1,1)/\sqrt2\).
Step 2 — (b). \(\mathbf u_1\mathbf v_1^{\mathsf T}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 1&1\end{pmatrix}\), so \(A_1=4\cdot\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\ 2&2\end{pmatrix}\).
Step 3 — (c). \(A-A_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\ -1&1\end{pmatrix}\), with size \(\sqrt{1+1+1+1}=2=\sigma_2\). Exactly as promised.
Step 4 — (d). \(U_1\Sigma_1=4\,\mathbf u_1=(4/\sqrt2,\ 4/\sqrt2)\approx(2.828,\ 2.828)\): both words get 2.828 — at \(k=1\) the difference between them is squeezed out.

answers at a glance: (a) \((1,1)/\sqrt2\). (b) \(\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\ 2&2\end{pmatrix}\). (c) \(\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\ -1&1\end{pmatrix}\), size 2 \(=\sigma_2\). (d) 2.828 each.

Remember

Each word's squeezed coordinates are its row of \(U_k\Sigma_k\); whatever the dropped directions carried is gone.

Problem 8mediumskip-gram scores

Middle word \(\mathbf v=(1,1)\). Real neighbour \(\mathbf u_+=(1,0)\). Two negatives: \(\mathbf u_1=(0,-1)\) and \(\mathbf u_2=(-1,-1)\). (a) Compute the three dot products. (b) The probability the model says "real" for the neighbour, and "random" for each negative. (c) The skip-gram loss in nats.

What this tests. The negative-sampling loss. Plan. Real pair: \(\sigma(z)\). Negative pair: \(\sigma(-z)\). Loss: minus the log of each, added.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(\mathbf u_+\cdot\mathbf v=1\), \(\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf v=-1\), \(\mathbf u_2\cdot\mathbf v=-2\).
Step 2 — (b). Real: \(\sigma(1)\approx0.7311\). Random for \(\mathbf u_1\): \(\sigma(1)\approx0.7311\). Random for \(\mathbf u_2\): \(\sigma(2)\approx0.8808\).
Step 3 — (c). \(-\ln0.7311-\ln0.7311-\ln0.8808\approx0.3133+0.3133+0.1269=0.7535\).

answers at a glance: (a) 1, −1, −2. (b) 0.7311, 0.7311, 0.8808. (c) \(\approx0.7535\).

Remember

For a negative, the right answer is "random", so its probability is \(\sigma(-\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)\) — the more negative the dot product, the better.

Problem 9hardone word2vec step

\(\mathbf v=(0,1)\), real neighbour \(\mathbf u_+=(1,1)\), one negative \(\mathbf u_-=(1,-1)\), step size \(\eta=0.5\). (a) Find \(g_+=1-\sigma(\mathbf u_+\cdot\mathbf v)\) and \(g_-=\sigma(\mathbf u_-\cdot\mathbf v)\). (b) Do one step of the pull-and-push rules for all three vectors. (c) Give the new scores \(\mathbf u_\pm\cdot\mathbf v\) and the loss before and after.

What this tests. The update rules \(\mathbf u_+\mathrel{+}=\eta g_+\mathbf v\), \(\mathbf u_-\mathrel{-}=\eta g_-\mathbf v\), \(\mathbf v\mathrel{+}=\eta(g_+\mathbf u_+-g_-\mathbf u_-)\), all using the OLD vectors. Plan. Compute the two "how wrong" numbers once, then update.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(\mathbf u_+\cdot\mathbf v=1\): \(g_+=1-\sigma(1)\approx0.2689\). \(\mathbf u_-\cdot\mathbf v=-1\): \(g_-=\sigma(-1)\approx0.2689\).
Step 2 — the neighbours. \(\mathbf u_+\leftarrow(1,1)+0.5(0.2689)(0,1)=(1,\ 1.1345)\). \(\mathbf u_-\leftarrow(1,-1)-0.5(0.2689)(0,1)=(1,\ -1.1345)\).
Step 3 — the middle word. \(g_+\mathbf u_+-g_-\mathbf u_-=0.2689\big((1,1)-(1,-1)\big)=(0,\ 0.5379)\), so \(\mathbf v\leftarrow(0,1)+0.5(0,0.5379)=(0,\ 1.2689)\).
Step 4 — (c). New scores: \(1.1345\times1.2689\approx1.4396\) and \(-1.4396\). Loss before: \(-2\ln\sigma(1)\approx0.6265\). After: \(-2\ln\sigma(1.4396)\approx0.4254\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(g_+=g_-\approx0.2689\). (b) \(\mathbf u_+=(1,1.1345)\), \(\mathbf u_-=(1,-1.1345)\), \(\mathbf v=(0,1.2689)\). (c) scores \(\pm1.4396\); loss 0.6265 → 0.4254.

Remember

Use the old vectors on the right-hand side of all three updates — compute first, then overwrite.

Problem 10mediumcost

Vocabulary \(V=30\,000\), vectors of \(d=300\) numbers, \(k=5\) negatives. A dot product of two 300-number lists costs 300 multiply-adds. (a) How many multiply-adds does one training pair cost with a full softmax (just the scores)? (b) With negative sampling? (c) The ratio. (d) For \(10^9\) training pairs, the totals.

What this tests. \(V\) dot products against \(k+1\). Plan. Multiply counts by 300.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(30\,000\times300=9\,000\,000\).
Step 2 — (b). \((5+1)\times300=1\,800\).
Step 3 — (c). \(9\,000\,000/1\,800=5\,000\) \(=V/(k+1)\).
Step 4 — (d). Full: \(9\times10^{15}\). Negative sampling: \(1.8\times10^{12}\).

answers at a glance: (a) 9 000 000. (b) 1 800. (c) 5 000. (d) \(9\times10^{15}\) against \(1.8\times10^{12}\).

Remember

The saving is \(V/(k+1)\), and it grows with the vocabulary.

Problem 11mediumanalogy

Toy vectors: India \(=(1,0,3)\), Delhi \(=(1,1.2,3)\), Japan \(=(3,0,1)\), Tokyo \(=(3,1,1)\), Kyoto \(=(3,1,2)\). (a) Compute \(\mathbf q=\) Delhi − India + Japan. (b) Compute the cosine of \(\mathbf q\) with Tokyo, Kyoto, Japan and Delhi. (c) Which word does the search return (skipping the inputs)? (d) Would the answer change if the inputs were allowed?

What this tests. Analogy arithmetic and the cosine search. Plan. Add first, then one cosine per candidate.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(\mathbf q=(1-1+3,\ 1.2-0+0,\ 3-3+1)=(3,\ 1.2,\ 1)\); \(\lVert\mathbf q\rVert=\sqrt{11.44}\approx3.382\).
Step 2 — (b). Tokyo: \(11.2/(3.382\cdot\sqrt{11})\approx0.9984\). Kyoto: \(12.2/(3.382\cdot\sqrt{14})\approx0.9640\). Japan: \(10/(3.382\cdot\sqrt{10})\approx0.9349\). Delhi: \(7.44/(3.382\cdot\sqrt{11.44})\approx0.6503\).
Step 3 — (c). Tokyo, with 0.9984.
Step 4 — (d). No: the inputs Japan (0.9349) and Delhi (0.6503) score below Tokyo here. (In real trained vectors they often would not.)

answers at a glance: (a) \((3,1.2,1)\). (b) 0.9984, 0.9640, 0.9349, 0.6503. (c) Tokyo. (d) no.

Remember

The answer to \(A-B+C\) is a point, not a word; the nearest word by cosine is the answer.

Problem 12mediumlookup

A 4-word vocabulary has the table \(E=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\ 0&3\\ 1&1\\ 4&0\end{pmatrix}\). (a) Compute \(\mathbf e_3E\). (b) A model reads "word 2, word 4" and glues the rows. What is \(\mathbf x\)? (c) The blame arriving at \(\mathbf x\) is \(\mathbf g=(0.1,\,-0.2,\,0.3,\,0.5)\). Which rows of \(E\) get a gradient, and what is it? (d) After one step with \(\eta=1\), give the new rows, and count how many of the 8 numbers of \(E\) changed.

What this tests. One-hot × table = row, and backprop into the table. Plan. The first two numbers of \(\mathbf g\) belong to word 2's row, the last two to word 4's.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). Row 3: \((1,1)\).
Step 2 — (b). \(\mathbf x=[\,(0,3);\,(4,0)\,]=(0,3,4,0)\).
Step 3 — (c). Row 2 gets \((0.1,-0.2)\), row 4 gets \((0.3,0.5)\); rows 1 and 3 get nothing.
Step 4 — (d). Row 2: \((0,3)-(0.1,-0.2)=(-0.1,\ 3.2)\). Row 4: \((4,0)-(0.3,0.5)=(3.7,\ -0.5)\). 4 of the 8 numbers changed.

answers at a glance: (a) \((1,1)\). (b) \((0,3,4,0)\). (c) rows 2 and 4: \((0.1,-0.2)\), \((0.3,0.5)\). (d) \((-0.1,3.2)\), \((3.7,-0.5)\); 4 numbers.

Remember

Only the rows of the words in the example move — that is what makes huge tables trainable.

Problem 13mediummodel size

A neural language model has \(V=10\,000\) words, embeddings of \(d=100\), reads the \(n=3\) previous words, and has one hidden layer of \(H=500\) neurons. (a) How many numbers in the embedding table? (b) In the hidden layer (weights and biases)? (c) In the output layer? (d) The total, and which part is biggest?

What this tests. Shapes: table \(V\times d\); hidden \(H\times(nd)\) plus \(H\); output \(V\times H\) plus \(V\). Plan. One line each.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(10\,000\times100=1\,000\,000\).
Step 2 — (b). The glued input has \(3\times100=300\) numbers: \(500\times300+500=150\,500\).
Step 3 — (c). \(10\,000\times500+10\,000=5\,010\,000\).
Step 4 — (d). \(1\,000\,000+150\,500+5\,010\,000=6\,160\,500\). The output layer — the softmax over the vocabulary — is the biggest part.

answers at a glance: (a) 1 000 000. (b) 150 500. (c) 5 010 000. (d) 6 160 500; the output layer.

Remember

With a large vocabulary, the first layer (the table) and the last (the softmax) hold most of the numbers.

Problem 14harddiagnose

Two language models were trained on the same text and tested on new text they had never seen:

modelperplexity on training textperplexity on new text
A45210
B8095

(a) Which model would you use? (b) Give each model's average surprise on the new text, in bits per word. (c) How many bits per word better is the better model, and on a 1000-word text, how many times more probable does it find the text? (Answer as a power of 2.) (d) How many bits per word does model A lose between training text and new text?

What this tests. Perplexity as \(2^{\text{bits}}\), and spotting overfitting (Unit 10). Plan. Take \(\log_2\) of every perplexity.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). Model B: only the score on new text matters, and 95 < 210.
Step 2 — (b). A: \(\log_2210\approx7.714\) bits. B: \(\log_2 95\approx6.570\) bits.
Step 3 — (c). \(\log_2(210/95)\approx1.144\) bits per word. Over 1000 words the probabilities differ by a factor \(2^{1000\times1.144}\approx2^{1144}\).
Step 4 — (d). \(\log_2(210/45)\approx2.222\) bits per word. A has memorised its training text (overfitting); B's gap is only \(\log_2(95/80)\approx0.248\) bits.

answers at a glance: (a) B. (b) A 7.714, B 6.570 bits. (c) 1.144 bits; about \(2^{1144}\) times. (d) 2.222 bits.

Remember

Judge a language model on text it has not seen. A big gap between training and new perplexity means memorising.
