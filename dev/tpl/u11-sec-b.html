Adam — two notebooks and a probation period

The new analyst

A new analyst joins and starts two notebooks about a project. One records which way the decisions have been trending. The other records how big they have been running. On day two both notebooks are nearly empty, and a careful manager knows exactly what to do with a nearly-empty notebook: discount it. Not ignore it — discount it, heavily at first, less each week, until a year in the notebooks are full and can be read at face value. That discount is a number, it shrinks the analyst's authority on purpose, and it retires itself.

You have already met both notebooks. The direction notebook is momentum, written as a fading average instead of a running velocity. The loudness notebook is RMSProp. Adam keeps both, one per knob, and divides the first by the root of the second.

The realization

\[F\leftarrow \rho_f F+(1-\rho_f)\nabla J,\qquad A\leftarrow \rho A+(1-\rho)\nabla J\odot\nabla J,\qquad \mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w-\alpha_t\,\frac{F}{\sqrt A}\]

\[\alpha_t=\alpha\,\frac{\sqrt{1-\rho^{\,t}}}{1-\rho_f^{\,t}}\]

The step is average direction divided by root average loudness. The numerator averages signed slopes, so slopes that disagree cancel; the denominator averages squares, which cannot cancel. A knob being pushed consistently gets a ratio near \(\pm1\); a knob being shaken about gets a ratio near zero. Noise is throttled automatically, and nothing had to detect it.

Now the probation rate. Both notebooks start empty, so after \(t\) steps \(F\) has filled to only a share \(1-\rho_f^{\,t}\) of full and \(A\) to \(1-\rho^{\,t}\). Correct each by its own share, put the corrected pair into an RMSProp-shaped step, and everything cancels except one leftover factor — and that leftover factor is \(\alpha_t\). Only \(A\) sits under a root, so only its correction picks up a square root. That is the whole derivation, and it is folded into the drawer below.

What it buys is visible in one number. Drag the step slider to \(t=1\): the stride is exactly \(0.1\) in both knobs. RMSProp's \(0.3162\) spike is gone, and AdaGrad's early fade never happens. Watch \(\alpha_t\) on the log axis: it starts at \(0.0316\), dips to a minimum of \(0.015224\) at \(t=12\) — the two notebooks fill at different speeds, so the correction is not monotone — and then climbs back to the plain \(\alpha=0.1\) and stays there. The scaffolding comes down by itself.

Two practical notes. Libraries call \((\rho_f,\rho)\) by the names \((\beta_1,\beta_2)\), with the same defaults \(0.9\) and \(0.999\). And count \(t\) from \(1\): at \(t=0\) the denominator \(1-\rho_f^{\,0}\) is zero and the whole rate blows up. That off-by-one is one of the most-committed bugs in machine learning.

Pause & predict

A knob's slope alternates \(+4,-4,+4,-4,\dots\) while another knob's stays steady at \(+4\). Both use Adam. Which one takes the bigger steps, and why?

In one sentence: Adam carries a signed memory and a squared memory per knob, steps by their ratio so that disagreement is thrown away and loudness is divided out, and wears a shrinking probation rate \(\alpha_t\) that makes the very first step exactly \(\alpha\) and then retires itself.

Five walkers, one valley — and the dial that decides the race

A fair race

Same valley, same start \([1,1]\), same stride \(\alpha=0.1\), five rules. Everything you are about to see comes from the update rule alone — no tuning, no tricks, no head start. Press play, then do the thing the race is really for: turn the stiffness dial and watch the result change shape.

At the lecture setting the story is already rich. Plain descent looks excellent — because \(\alpha=0.1\) happens to annihilate the steep direction in one step, which is luck, not skill. Momentum pays an early debt (the loss rises to \(4.31\) at step 2) and then rings its way down. AdaGrad evens the two directions immediately and then loses its stride. RMSProp reaches the floor first and by a distance. Adam walks a steady \(\alpha\)-sized pace, rolls through the bottom around step eleven because it still carries momentum, and swings back.

Now do not crown a winner. This valley is tiny, clean, noiseless, only ten-to-one stiff, and the stride was chosen to suit it. Change any one of those and the order changes. That is not a weakness of the demo — it is the demo, and the dial is how you see it.

Push stiffness to \(c=300\) with the same \(\alpha=0.1\). Plain descent is now outside its own safety limit and leaves the map inside five steps; so does momentum, which inherits that limit and adds energy to it. The two adaptive methods do not notice. Drop \(\alpha\) to \(0.005\) to rescue descent and it survives — and now crawls, because the stride that keeps the stiff direction stable is far too small for the gentle one. That is §1's impossible choice returning at scale.

The realization

\[\begin{aligned}\text{plain descent needs}\quad &\alpha<\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}},\\[2pt] \text{and progress along }\lambda_{\min}\text{ costs}\quad &\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}}=\kappa\ \text{ times more steps.}\end{aligned}\]

The safe window for the shared stride is set by the stiffest direction; the number of steps is set by the softest. Their ratio is the condition number \(\kappa\), and it is the tax that plain descent pays and the adaptive methods refuse to. AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam all divide by a measurement of the slope's own size, so the size never reaches the stride — they cannot see \(\kappa\) at all.

That is the honest reason Adam and its cousin AdamW are the default for transformers and large language models. At that scale, slope sizes differ across layers by orders of magnitude, they drift while training, and the readings are noisy mini-batch estimates. Nobody is going to hand-tune a stride per layer. A method that self-scales, smooths and starts fairly is the practical pick — not because it wins a race on a two-knob toy, but because it never needed the race to be fair.

Then someone builds a wall

A problem you can do in your head

Find the smallest value of \(f(x)=x^2\) — but you may only pick \(x\ge2\). Do it now, before reading on. The bowl bottoms at \(x=0\), that point is banned, so you slide as close to it as the rule lets you and stop at \(x=2\), where \(f=4\). It took three seconds, and you just broke every method in this course. At \(x=2\) the slope of \(f\) is \(4\). Not zero. Four. Every technique you have — set the derivative to zero, walk until the tilt vanishes, check the Hessian — is hunting for a flat spot that is not there.

Slide the wall and watch the readout flip. While the wall sits at \(c\le0\) it is not in your way: the free answer survives and the slope there is zero, the old world. The instant \(c>0\) three things happen at once, and they happen in almost every constrained problem you will ever meet.

One: the free answer is thrown away. The rule did not adjust the answer, it replaced it. Two: the new answer sits exactly on the edge of the allowed set, not comfortably inside it. Three: the gradient at the answer is not zero, and it points straight into the wall — which is precisely why you cannot move.

Point three is the whole reason this half of the unit exists. We need a new test for "I have arrived", one that says: the slope is nonzero, but every direction that would use it is blocked.

Before we can build that test, everything has to be written in one shape. Open the standard form tab. Two mechanical moves do it. A \(\ge\) becomes a \(\le\) by multiplying through by \(-1\). Everything else moves to the left of the sign.

The realization

\[\min_{\mathbf x}\ f(\mathbf x)\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad e(\mathbf x)=0,\qquad g_i(\mathbf x)\le0,\ \ i=1,\dots,m\]

Three roles, and this unit gives each a name you can picture. \(f\) is the score — the number you want small. \(e=0\) is the path — a line you must sit exactly on. Each \(g_i\le0\) is a fence — a wall you must stay behind. So \(x\ge2\) is written \(g=2-x\le0\), and its gradient is \(\nabla g=-1\). Keep that minus sign. More answers are lost to that sign than to any other step in the topic.

One last piece of the shape, and it looks like pedantry until you try to break it. You will never see a strict \(<\). Try to minimise \(f(x)=x\) subject to \(x>2\). Is \(2.1\) the answer? No — \(2.01\) is allowed and smaller. Is \(2.01\)? No. You can keep going for ever, getting closer to \(2\) without ever being allowed to reach it. The problem has no answer at all. Including the boundary is what makes the answer exist — and since the answer almost always sits on the boundary, a form that excluded it would throw away the thing we came for.

Pause & predict

Minimise \(f(x)=(x-5)^2\) subject to \(x\le1\). Where is the answer, and what is the slope there?

In one sentence: Put a wall in a problem and the answer moves onto the wall, where the gradient is not zero — so "stop where the slope vanishes" has to be replaced, and the replacement starts by writing everything as \(\min f\) with \(e=0\) and \(g_i\le0\).
8

Pushing a box against a wall

The corridor

Push a heavy box along a corridor at an angle, so it presses against the wall. While your push has any sideways component along the wall, the box slides. It keeps sliding. It stops only at the instant your push is square-on — when nothing you are doing runs along the wall any more, and all of it goes into the wall. That is not a fact about boxes. It is the stopping condition for every constrained problem, and it is the first of the five conditions we are building.

Drag the point around the fence and watch the bar. The downhill arrow \(-\nabla f\) has been split into two pieces: the part that runs along the fence, and the part that pushes across it. While the along-part exists you are not finished — slide that way and the score improves, legally, without ever leaving the allowed set. The bar shrinks as you approach the answer and reaches zero exactly at it.

When the along-part is gone, all of \(\nabla f\) points across the fence. And there is only one direction that is "across the fence": the fence's own gradient \(\nabla g\), which points out of the allowed side. Two arrows pointing along one line is not a vague picture — it is an equation.

The realization

\[\nabla f(\mathbf x^\star)=-\mu\,\nabla g(\mathbf x^\star),\qquad \mu\ge0\]

Parallel — not equal. The two arrows almost never have the same length, and \(\mu\) is the stretch factor between them. Writing "\(\nabla f=\nabla g\)" is the single most common slip in this topic, and the widget shows you the two lengths side by side so you can see how different they are: on the straight fence \(\nabla f=(4,4)\) and \(\nabla h=(1,1)\), a stretch of \(4\).

There is a second way to read the same picture, and it is worth having both. The level curves of \(f\) are the score's contour lines. Shrink them until the smallest one still touches the allowed set. It touches — it does not cross — and at a touching point two curves share a tangent, which means their normals lie on one line. Tangency and "nothing left to slide" are the same statement seen from two angles.

Open the budget circle tab, because you have been doing this for months without being told. Ridge regression is usually taught as "minimise the error plus \(\lambda\sum\beta_j^2\)". Its honest original form is a constrained problem: minimise the training error subject to \(\sum_j\beta_j^2\le s\) — a budget on how large the coefficients may grow. The grey rings are the error, the red circle is the budget, and the answer is where the smallest reachable ring touches the circle. That picture is the entire geometry of ridge, and the \(\lambda\) in your notes is the stretch factor \(\mu\) at that touching point. Lasso is the same picture with a diamond instead of a circle — and corners are exactly where coefficients become zero.

Pause & predict

At a candidate point on a fence you find that \(\nabla f\) has a component of size \(0.7\) running along the fence. What does that tell you?

In one sentence: You stop when nothing is left to slide, which means all of \(\nabla f\) points across the fence — so \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\) lie on one line, related by a stretch factor \(\mu\ge0\), and that factor is about to turn out to be a price.
