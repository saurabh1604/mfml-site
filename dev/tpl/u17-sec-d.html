Encoder–decoder: translate through one summary

Imagine this

An interpreter at a meeting listens to a whole sentence in English. She holds its meaning in her head. Then she speaks it in Hindi, word by word.

Now give her a rule: she may keep only one small card of notes between listening and speaking. A short sentence fits easily. A very long speech does not.

To translate, the input and the output have different lengths and different word orders. "The train to Delhi is late" is six English words; in Hindi it is "Delhi ki train late hai", five words, in another order. So we use two recurrent cells:

  • The encoder reads the English sentence word by word. Its final note, \(\mathbf s=\mathbf h_T\), is the summary of the whole sentence (people also call it the context vector).
  • The decoder starts from that summary and writes the Hindi sentence one word at a time. After each word it reads what it just wrote as its next input, and it stops when it writes a special word, \(\langle\text{end}\rangle\).

Training uses one small trick. While learning, we feed the decoder the true previous Hindi word, not its own guess. This is called teacher forcing — like a teacher who corrects a student after every word, so one early slip does not ruin the rest of the sentence. At test time there is no teacher, and the decoder must use its own words.

The bottleneck. The summary has a fixed size — say 8 numbers in our toy, 512 in a real system. A 5-word sentence and a 50-word sentence must both squeeze into those same 8 numbers. If each word brings 8 numbers of its own, that is 40 numbers into 8 for the short sentence, and 400 into 8 for the long one. And the first word must survive the whole encoder: if the encoder keeps 90% of an old word at each step (a toy rate), word 1 of a 50-word sentence speaks in the summary at \(0.9^{49}\approx0.0057\) of its first strength. This is exactly what the first translation systems of 2014 showed: good on short sentences, worse and worse on long ones.

The realization

\[\begin{gathered}\text{English}\xrightarrow{\ \text{encoder}\ }\mathbf s\ (\text{fixed size})\\ \mathbf s\xrightarrow{\ \text{decoder}\ }\text{Hindi}\end{gathered}\]

An encoder–decoder translates through one summary vector. It works, and it was a breakthrough. But everything the decoder knows about the source sentence must pass through that one fixed-size vector — a bottleneck that hurts more with every extra word. What if the decoder could look back at every English word, whenever it needs to? That question is Unit 18.

Picking the words: greedy or beam search

Imagine this

You are driving across a city. At every junction you take the road that looks fastest right now. Sometimes that road leads straight into a traffic jam, and a road that looked a little slower at first would have got you home sooner.

Keeping two or three routes in mind, instead of one, often finds the better trip.

At each step the decoder gives a probability for every possible next word. The probability of a whole sentence is the product of its word probabilities (the chain rule of Unit 14). We want the sentence with the biggest product. But there are far too many sentences to try them all.

Greedy decoding takes the single most likely word at each step and never looks back. Beam search keeps the \(k\) best partial sentences at every step (\(k\) is the beam width), grows each by one word, and again keeps only the best \(k\). With \(k=1\) it is greedy.

A tiny example. The decoder is writing the English for "hamari train late hai". First word: "the" 0.5, "our" 0.4, "a" 0.1. Second word, after "the": "train" 0.4, "bus" 0.3, "rain" 0.3. After "our": "train" 0.9, "bus" 0.1. After "a": "train" 0.6, "bus" 0.4.

  • Greedy: take "the" (0.5), then its best, "train" (0.4). Sentence "the train": \(0.5\times0.4=0.20\).
  • Beam, \(k=2\): keep "the" (0.5) and "our" (0.4). Grow both: the train 0.20, the bus 0.15, the rain 0.15, our train \(0.4\times0.9=0.36\), our bus 0.04. The best is "our train", 0.36.

Greedy lost because "our" looked slightly worse at the first step, even though it led to a far surer second word. In practice we add log-probabilities instead of multiplying (products of many small numbers become too tiny for a computer): \(\ln0.36\approx-1.022\) beats \(\ln0.20\approx-1.609\).

Try: Press ▶ search with width 1 (greedy): it commits to "the" and ends at 0.20. Switch to width 2 and search again: "our" survives the first cut and wins with 0.36. Width 3 keeps everything and finds the same 0.36.

The realization

\[\begin{gathered}P(\text{sentence})=\prod_t P(\text{word}_t\mid\dots)\\ \text{score}=\sum_t\ln P(\text{word}_t\mid\dots)\end{gathered}\]

Greedy decoding picks the best word now; beam search keeps the \(k\) best sentences-so-far and can recover when a slightly weaker first word leads somewhere much better. It costs about \(k\) times the work of greedy, and it is still not a guarantee of the very best sentence — just a much better bet.

In one sentence: A sentence's probability is the product of its word probabilities; greedy takes the best word each time and can land on 0.20, while a beam of width 2 keeps two candidates and finds 0.36.

What to carry forward

The whole unit, one card at a time.

Order needs memory

A bag of words loses order; a window loses the past. A running note that changes the old note before adding the new word keeps both.

The recurrent cell

\(\mathbf h_t=\tanh(W\mathbf h_{t-1}+U\mathbf x_t+\mathbf b)\), the same weights at every word: \(h(h+d)+h\) numbers for any length.

Unrolled

A deep network as long as the sentence, whose layers are twins. Read out with \(\operatorname{softmax}(V\mathbf h_t)\); the loss adds up over steps.

Backprop through time

Rule A and Rule B on the unrolled chain. The shared weight's gradient is the sum over steps. The blame to an early word is a product of one factor per step.

Fade or explode

The factors are powers of one matrix, so the biggest \(|\lambda|\) decides: \(0.5^{10}\approx0.001\), \(1.5^{10}\approx58\).

Clipping

If \(\|\mathbf g\|>c\), rescale to length \(c\). A cure for explosions, not for fading.

LSTM

\(\mathbf c_t=\mathbf f\odot\mathbf c_{t-1}+\mathbf i\odot\mathbf g\): an express lane where blame is multiplied only by the forget gate.

GRU

\(\mathbf h_t=(1-\mathbf z)\odot\mathbf h_{t-1}+\mathbf z\odot\tilde{\mathbf h}_t\): one blend dial. Blocks: plain 1, GRU 3, LSTM 4.

Encoder–decoder

Squeeze the source into one summary, write the target from it; teacher forcing to train, beam search to decode. The summary is a bottleneck.

Where this goes next.

  • Unit 18 · Attention and Transformers. Two problems are left over from this unit. The decoder sees the source only through one summary, and every word must wait for the word before it. Attention fixes both at once: every word can look directly at every other word, with weights from dot products (Unit 3) and softmax (Unit 14). The residual path of a transformer is another express lane — the same idea as the LSTM's.
  • Unit 19 · The Maths Inside an LLM. A language model at huge scale: next-word probabilities, and how the machine picks its words — greedy, beam, and the random sampling with temperature, top-k and top-p that chat models use.
The realization

\[\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial\mathbf h_T}{\partial\mathbf h_k}\ \text{carries}\ W^{T-k}\\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{eigenvalues decide}\end{gathered}\]

A machine with memory multiplies by the same matrix at every word, so its memory and its blame live or die by that matrix's eigenvalues. Gates build an express lane where the multiplier is a number the network controls, close to 1 when it wants to remember.

In one sentence: A recurrent cell reads one word at a time with shared weights, trains by backprop through time where powers of one matrix make memory fade or explode, is rescued by gates that open an express lane, and translates through a single summary — the bottleneck that attention removes next.
