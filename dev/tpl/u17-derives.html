Prove it · tanh squeezes, and its slope is 1 − tanh²

Claim. \(\tanh z=\dfrac{e^{z}-e^{-z}}{e^{z}+e^{-z}}\) always lies strictly between −1 and 1, and its slope is \(\tanh'(z)=1-\tanh^2 z\), which is at most 1 (reached only at \(z=0\)).

1
The top \(e^{z}-e^{-z}\) is always smaller in size than the bottom \(e^{z}+e^{-z}\), because \(e^{\pm z}>0\). So \(|\tanh z|<1\). This is why the note never overflows the card: every entry of \(\mathbf h_t\) stays inside \((-1,1)\).
2
Quotient rule, with \(A=e^z-e^{-z}\), \(B=e^z+e^{-z}\), \(A'=B\), \(B'=A\): \[\begin{aligned}\tanh'(z)&=\frac{B\cdot B-A\cdot A}{B^2}\\ &=1-\frac{A^2}{B^2}\\ &=1-\tanh^2z.\end{aligned}\] ∎ Handy: the slope comes straight from the stored note. At \(h_1=0.7616\), the slope is \(1-0.5800\approx0.42\).
Prove it · written out, the first word is buried under powers of w

Claim. For the scalar cell with \(b=0\), \(h_0=0\): \(h_3=\tanh\!\big(w\tanh(w\tanh(ux_1)+ux_2)+ux_3\big)\). If every number stays small (so \(\tanh v\approx v\)), then \(h_3\approx w^2ux_1+wux_2+ux_3\).

1
Substitute step by step: \(h_1=\tanh(ux_1)\), \(h_2=\tanh(wh_1+ux_2)\), \(h_3=\tanh(wh_2+ux_3)\). Put each inside the next. The same \(w\) and \(u\) appear at every level: one machine, used three times.
2
For small arguments \(\tanh v\approx v\) (its slope at 0 is 1). Replace each tanh by its argument: \[\begin{aligned}h_3&\approx w(w\,ux_1+ux_2)\\ &\quad+ux_3\\ &=w^2ux_1+wux_2\\ &\quad+ux_3.\end{aligned}\] ∎ Word \(k\) of \(T\) enters with weight \(w^{T-k}\). With \(w=0.5\) the first of three words gets \(0.25\): the fading of §2, in one line.
Prove it · the weight count does not depend on the length

Claim. A recurrent cell with note size \(h\) and word size \(d\) learns \(h(h+d)+h\) numbers, whatever the number of words \(T\). Without sharing, the \(T\)-step chain would need \(T\) times as many.

1
Shapes: \(W\mathbf h_{t-1}\) must give \(h\) numbers from \(h\) numbers, so \(W\) is \(h\times h\). \(U\mathbf x_t\) gives \(h\) numbers from \(d\), so \(U\) is \(h\times d\). \(\mathbf b\) has \(h\). Total \(h^2+hd+h=h(h+d)+h\). The shape rule of Unit 15: rows for where the numbers go, columns for where they come from.
2
Unrolling makes \(T\) copies, but every copy points at the same \(W,U,\mathbf b\). The list of distinct numbers is the same list. If each step had its own, we would have \(T\big(h(h+d)+h\big)\). ∎ Example: \(h=4\), \(d=3\): 32 numbers shared, or \(320\) for 10 unshared steps. Sharing is also what lets the cell read a sentence longer than any it saw in training.
Prove it · each read-out sends back "prediction minus truth"

Claim. With \(\hat{\mathbf y}_t=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf o_t)\), \(\mathbf o_t=V\mathbf h_t+\mathbf c\) and \(L=\sum_t-\ln\hat y_t[\text{true}_t]\), the blame at each read-out is \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf o_t=\hat{\mathbf y}_t-\mathbf y_t\) (one-hot truth), and \(\partial L/\partial V=\sum_t(\hat{\mathbf y}_t-\mathbf y_t)\,\mathbf h_t^{\top}\).

1
Only the \(t\)-th term of the sum depends on \(\mathbf o_t\). For that term, softmax plus cross-entropy gives prediction minus truth (Unit 15): \(\hat{\mathbf y}_t-\mathbf y_t\). Example from §4: truth "Delhi", \(\hat{\mathbf y}=(0.609,0.224,0.136,0.030)\), so the blame is \((-0.391,0.224,0.136,0.030)\).
2
\(V\) is used at every step, so (Rule B plus sharing) its gradient adds one outer product per step: \(\sum_t(\hat{\mathbf y}_t-\mathbf y_t)\mathbf h_t^{\top}\). ∎ And each read-out also sends \(V^{\top}(\hat{\mathbf y}_t-\mathbf y_t)\) into its note \(\mathbf h_t\), where it joins the blame arriving from later steps.
Prove it · temperature keeps the order and only changes the gaps

Claim. With \(p_i=e^{s_i/T}/\sum_je^{s_j/T}\), the ratio of two shares is \(p_i/p_j=e^{(s_i-s_j)/T}\). So the order of the words never changes; as \(T\to0\) the top word takes everything (greedy), and as \(T\to\infty\) every share tends to \(1/n\) (a fair die).

1
Both shares have the same denominator, so it cancels: \(\dfrac{p_i}{p_j}=\dfrac{e^{s_i/T}}{e^{s_j/T}}=e^{(s_i-s_j)/T}\). If \(s_i>s_j\) this ratio is above 1 for every \(T>0\): the favourite stays the favourite. Delhi over Mumbai (gap 1): \(e^{1}\approx2.718\) at \(T=1\), \(e^{2}\approx7.389\) at \(T=0.5\), \(e^{0.5}\approx1.649\) at \(T=2\).
2
As \(T\to0\), every gap to the top word is divided by a tiny number, so \(p_j/p_{\text{top}}=e^{-(s_{\text{top}}-s_j)/T}\to0\): the top word's share goes to 1. As \(T\to\infty\) every exponent goes to 0, every ratio to 1, and each of the \(n\) words gets \(1/n\). ∎ Check with §5's numbers: \(0.842/0.114\approx7.39\approx e^{2}\), and \(0.434/0.263\approx1.65\approx e^{0.5}\).
Prove it · adding up the surprises is scoring the whole sentence

Claim. For a text \(w_1,\dots,w_T\), the teacher-forcing loss \(L=\sum_{t=1}^{T}-\ln p(w_t\mid w_1,\dots,w_{t-1})\) equals \(-\ln P(w_1,\dots,w_T)\). So making \(L\) small is exactly maximum likelihood (Unit 14).

1
The chain rule of probability writes a sentence as a string of guesses: \(P(w_1,\dots,w_T)=P(w_1)\,P(w_2\mid w_1)\cdots P(w_T\mid w_1,\dots,w_{T-1})\). Each factor is one read-out of the language model, made with the true text so far fed in — teacher forcing supplies exactly these conditions.
2
Take \(-\ln\) of both sides; the logarithm turns the product into a sum: \(-\ln P=\sum_t-\ln P(w_t\mid\dots)=L\). ∎ In §5's widget the sentence "the train to pune is late." costs a total surprise of 6.507 over its 26 characters, so the tiny model gives the whole sentence probability \(e^{-6.507}\approx0.0015\) — about 1 in 670.
Prove it · the blame to an early note is a product of one factor per step

Claim. For \(h_t=\tanh(z_t)\), \(z_t=wh_{t-1}+ux_t+b\): \(\dfrac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_k}=\displaystyle\prod_{t=k+1}^{T}w\,(1-h_t^2)\).

1
One step: \(\dfrac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}}=\tanh'(z_t)\cdot\dfrac{\partial z_t}{\partial h_{t-1}}=(1-h_t^2)\cdot w\). Rule A (the tanh slope) times Rule B (the weight on the wire).
2
\(h_T\) depends on \(h_k\) only through the chain \(h_k\to h_{k+1}\to\cdots\to h_T\). The chain rule multiplies the steps: \[\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_k}=\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_{T-1}}\cdots\frac{\partial h_{k+1}}{\partial h_k}.\] ∎ §6's numbers: \(0.4838\times0.4340\approx0.2100\). For a vector note the factors are matrices, \(\operatorname{diag}(1-\mathbf h_t^2)\,W\), multiplied in order.
Prove it · a shared weight's gradient is the sum of every step's report

Claim. \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w}=\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_t}\,h_{t-1}\).

1
Pretend each step had its own copy \(w_t\), all set equal to \(w\). Then \(L\) is a function \(L(w_1,\dots,w_T)\) evaluated at \(w_1=\dots=w_T=w\). A trick: give each use of the weight its own name.
2
Moving \(w\) moves every copy at once, so by the chain rule (Unit 6) \(\dfrac{dL}{dw}=\sum_t\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w_t}\). Rule B gives each copy's share: \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w_t}=\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z_t}\,h_{t-1}\). ∎ Step machine: \(-0.1444-0.1588-0.1623+0=-0.4655\). The copy at step 1 reports 0 because \(h_0=0\).
Prove it · with a note of many numbers, each step back multiplies by the same matrix

Claim. For \(\mathbf h_t=\tanh(W\mathbf h_{t-1}+U\mathbf x_t+\mathbf b)\): \(\dfrac{\partial\mathbf h_t}{\partial\mathbf h_{t-1}}=\operatorname{diag}(1-\mathbf h_t^2)\,W\), and the blame moves back as \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf h_{t-1}}=W^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(1-\mathbf h_t^2)\,\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf h_t}\).

1
Entry \(i\) of the new note is \(\tanh(z_i)\) with \(z_i=\sum_jW_{ij}h_{t-1,j}+\dots\). So \(\partial h_{t,i}/\partial h_{t-1,j}=(1-h_{t,i}^2)\,W_{ij}\): Rule A (the slope of entry \(i\)) times Rule B (the weight on the wire from \(j\) to \(i\)). As a matrix, that is row \(i\) of \(W\) scaled by \(1-h_{t,i}^2\): \(\operatorname{diag}(1-\mathbf h_t^2)\,W\). This is the Jacobian of Unit 6, one step of the chain.
2
By the chain rule in matrix form (Unit 6), the blame for \(h_{t-1,j}\) collects \(\sum_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_{t,i}}(1-h_{t,i}^2)W_{ij}\): that is the transposed Jacobian times the blame, \(W^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(1-\mathbf h_t^2)\,\partial L/\partial\mathbf h_t\). ∎ Walking back \(T-k\) steps, the blame meets \(W^{\top}\) exactly \(T-k\) times, with a tanh slope (never more than 1) between each — the powers of §7.
Prove it · truncated BPTT: how many chunks, and how far the blame reaches

Claim. Cutting a text of \(N\) words into chunks of \(k\) words gives \(\lceil N/k\rceil\) chunks, and the blame from word \(t\) reaches back at most to the first word of its chunk, \(k-1\) words earlier. For \(N=1000\), \(k=20\): 50 chunks, and word 45's blame stops at word 41.

1
Chunk \(m\) holds words \((m-1)k+1\) to \(mk\). The last chunk ends at or after word \(N\) when \(mk\ge N\), so there are \(\lceil N/k\rceil\) chunks: \(\lceil1000/20\rceil=50\). Word 45 is in chunk \(\lceil45/20\rceil=3\), which holds words 41 to 60.
2
The backward walk stops at the start of the chunk, so from word \(t\) it covers only words \(t,t-1,\dots\) down to the chunk's first word: at most \(k-1\) steps back. ∎ The forward pass is not cut: the note leaving chunk 2 is the note entering chunk 3, so word 45 still feels word 5 — it just can never be taught to use it.
Prove it · powers of W, one eigen-direction at a time

Claim. If \(W\mathbf e_1=\lambda_1\mathbf e_1\), \(W\mathbf e_2=\lambda_2\mathbf e_2\) and \(\mathbf v=a\mathbf e_1+b\mathbf e_2\), then \(W^t\mathbf v=a\lambda_1^t\mathbf e_1+b\lambda_2^t\mathbf e_2\). For \(W=\begin{pmatrix}0.9&0.4\\ 0.1&0.6\end{pmatrix}\), \(\mathbf v=(1,0)\): \(W^t\mathbf v=0.2(4,1)+0.2\cdot0.5^t(1,-1)\).

1
A matrix spreads over sums: \(W\mathbf v=aW\mathbf e_1+bW\mathbf e_2=a\lambda_1\mathbf e_1+b\lambda_2\mathbf e_2\). Apply \(W\) again and each part gets one more factor of its own \(\lambda\). Along an eigenvector the matrix is just a number (Unit 4).
2
For the worked \(W\): \(\det(W-\lambda I)=\lambda^2-1.5\lambda+0.5=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-0.5)\). Check the directions: \(W(4,1)=(3.6+0.4,\ 0.4+0.6)=(4,1)\) and \(W(1,-1)=(0.5,-0.5)\). Trace 1.5 is the sum of the eigenvalues; determinant 0.5 is their product.
3
Split \((1,0)=a(4,1)+b(1,-1)\): \(4a+b=1\) and \(a-b=0\), so \(a=b=0.2\). Hence \(W^t(1,0)=0.2(4,1)+0.2\cdot0.5^t(1,-1)\to(0.8,0.2)\). ∎ \(t=1\): \((0.8+0.1,\ 0.2-0.1)=(0.9,0.1)\) — the same as \(W(1,0)\) directly.

Claim. For the scalar cell, \(\Big|\dfrac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_k}\Big|\le|w|^{T-k}\). So if \(|w|<1\) the blame fades at least as fast as \(|w|^{T-k}\). For a vector note the same holds with the largest stretch of \(W\) in place of \(|w|\).

1
Each factor is \(w(1-h_t^2)\), and \(0<1-h_t^2\le1\) because \(|h_t|<1\). So each factor's size is at most \(|w|\). The squeeze of tanh never amplifies; it only shrinks, and shrinks most when the note is near ±1 ("saturated").
2
A product of \(T-k\) numbers, each of size at most \(|w|\), has size at most \(|w|^{T-k}\). ∎ The bound only works in one direction: with \(|w|>1\) the tanh can still tame an explosion, but while the notes stay small, the blame grows like \(|w|^{T-k}\).

Claim. \(R=\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\ b&a\end{pmatrix}\) with \(b\ne0\) has eigenvalues \(a\pm bi\), of size \(r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\), and \(\|R\mathbf v\|=r\|\mathbf v\|\) for every \(\mathbf v\). For the rotate preset, \(a=0.8\), \(b=0.6\), \(r=1\).

1
\(\det(R-\lambda I)=(a-\lambda)^2+b^2=0\) gives \(\lambda=a\pm bi\), each of size \(\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\). No real solution: there is no direction that \(R\) leaves on its own line — every direction gets turned.
2
\(\|R(x,y)\|^2=(ax-by)^2+(bx+ay)^2=(a^2+b^2)(x^2+y^2)\). So each step multiplies the length by \(r\) and turns by the angle \(\theta\) with \(\cos\theta=a/r\). ∎ \(0.8^2+0.6^2=1\), \(\theta\approx36.87°\): after \(t\) steps the note has turned \(36.87°\,t\) and kept its length — the spiral staircase.

Claim. The clipped gradient \(\mathbf g'=\min(1,c/\|\mathbf g\|)\,\mathbf g\) points the same way as \(\mathbf g\), has length \(\min(\|\mathbf g\|,c)\), and the step \(\eta\mathbf g'\) is never longer than \(\eta c\). It is still a downhill direction.

1
\(\mathbf g'\) is \(\mathbf g\) times a positive number \(s=\min(1,c/\|\mathbf g\|)\), so it points the same way, and \(\|\mathbf g'\|=s\|\mathbf g\|=\min(\|\mathbf g\|,c)\). Example: \(s=5/50=0.1\) turns \((30,40)\) into \((3,4)\).

The slope of the loss along the step \(-\eta\mathbf g'\) is \(-\eta\,\mathbf g\cdot\mathbf g'=-\eta s\|\mathbf g\|^2<0\). ∎ Downhill at the start, as a plain step is (Unit 9) — only shorter. \(\|Q\mathbf v\|^2=(Q\mathbf v)^{\top}(Q\mathbf v)=\mathbf v^{\top}Q^{\top}Q\,\mathbf v=\mathbf v^{\top}\mathbf v=\|\mathbf v\|^2\). The motions that don't lie of Unit 3: turns and mirrors.

Claim. For sentences \(A\) and \(B\), \(\prod_tp_t^A>\prod_tp_t^B\) exactly when \(\sum_t\ln p_t^A>\sum_t\ln p_t^B\). A 200-word sentence whose words each have probability 0.01 has product \(10^{-400}\), which a computer's usual numbers round to 0, but log-score \(200\ln0.01\approx-921.0\).

1

Prove it · the pieces of BLEU, and why one zero sinks it

Claim. With clipped precisions \(p_n=\dfrac{\sum_g\min(\text{count}_{\text{cand}}(g),\,\text{count}_{\text{ref}}(g))}{\sum_g\text{count}_{\text{cand}}(g)}\) over the candidate's \(n\)-grams \(g\), and \(\text{BP}=\min(1,e^{1-r/c})\): \(\text{BLEU-2}=\text{BP}\sqrt{p_1p_2}\) is 0 whenever \(p_1\) or \(p_2\) is 0, and for "the train is late" against "the train is running late" it is \(0.7788\sqrt{2/3}\approx0.636\).

1
Unigrams: the, train, is, late each appear once in the reference: \(p_1=4/4\). Bigrams: "the train" and "train is" appear, "is late" does not: \(p_2=2/3\). Lengths \(c=4<r=5\), so \(\text{BP}=e^{1-5/4}=e^{-0.25}\approx0.7788\). The \(\min\) is the clipping: "the the the the" gets \(\min(4,1)/4=1/4\).
2
\(\sqrt{p_1p_2}=e^{\frac12(\ln p_1+\ln p_2)}\) is the geometric mean: if one precision is 0 its logarithm is \(-\infty\) and the mean is 0. Here \(\sqrt{1\cdot2/3}\approx0.8165\), and \(0.7788\cdot0.8165\approx0.636\). ∎ A geometric mean rewards doing well on every piece size: word salad ("late is the train", \(p_1=1\), \(p_2=1/3\)) falls to \(0.7788\sqrt{1/3}\approx0.450\).
