Problem 1easy

A straight line \(\hat y=wx+b\) is fitted to the two points \((1,3)\) and \((2,5)\) by minimising \(L(w,b)=\tfrac12\big[(w+b-3)^2+(2w+b-5)^2\big]\). (a) Derive \(\partial L/\partial w\) and \(\partial L/\partial b\) in simplest form and write the two update lines. (b) From \((w_0,b_0)=(0,0)\) with \(\alpha=0.1\), run two iterations; report \((w_1,b_1)\), \((w_2,b_2)\) and the loss after each. (c) A second run started from the same \((0,0)\) with a different rate and landed at \((0.26,\,0.16)\) after one step. Recover that rate and check it on both coordinates. (d) From the Hessian of \(L\), give the two eigenvalues, the largest safe rate, and decide whether \(\alpha=0.3\) is safe — and by how much.

The five rules used throughout this set. All start from the current point \(\mathbf w_t\) with \(\mathbf g_t=\nabla f(\mathbf w_t)\); squares, roots and divisions of vectors are taken element by element; \(\epsilon\approx10^{-8}\) only prevents division by zero and is dropped in hand calculation.

What this tests. Chain rule into a gradient, the plain rule run without slips, the fact that one step is exactly \(\alpha\) times the gradient vector — so a landing point hands \(\alpha\) back — and the speed limit \(\alpha<2/\lambda_{\max}\). Plan. Differentiate each square by the chain rule and collect; then per iteration in this order: gradient at the current point, update both knobs with that same gradient, loss at the new point; for (c) write one step with \(\alpha\) unknown and solve from each coordinate; for (d) read trace and determinant off the Hessian.

Show the full solution
(a) Step 1 — differentiate, do not expand. Each term is \(\tfrac12(\text{stuff})^2\), whose derivative is \((\text{stuff})\times(\text{derivative of the stuff})\). The \(w\)-derivative of \(w+b-3\) is \(1\); of \(2w+b-5\) it is \(2\): \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}&=(w+b-3)(1)+(2w+b-5)(2)\\ &=w+b-3+4w+2b-10\\ &=5w+3b-13. \end{aligned}\] Both \(b\)-derivatives are \(1\): \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}&=(w+b-3)+(2w+b-5)\\ &=3w+2b-8. \end{aligned}\] So the two update lines are \[\begin{aligned} w&\leftarrow w-\alpha\,(5w+3b-13),\\ b&\leftarrow b-\alpha\,(3w+2b-8). \end{aligned}\]
(b) Step 2 — iteration 1 from \((0,0)\). Gradient first: \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}\Big|_{(0,0)}&=5(0)+3(0)-13=-13,\\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}\Big|_{(0,0)}&=3(0)+2(0)-8=-8,\\ L_0&=\tfrac12\big[(-3)^2+(-5)^2\big]=\tfrac12(34)=17. \end{aligned}\] Now move both knobs with that one gradient: \[\begin{aligned} w_1&=0-0.1(-13)=1.3,\\ b_1&=0-0.1(-8)=0.8. \end{aligned}\] The new loss, residual by residual: \[\begin{aligned} w_1+b_1-3&=1.3+0.8-3=-0.9,\\ 2w_1+b_1-5&=2.6+0.8-5=-1.6,\\ L_1&=\tfrac12\big[0.81+2.56\big]\\ &=\tfrac12(3.37)=1.685. \end{aligned}\]
Step 3 — iteration 2. New gradient at \((1.3,0.8)\): \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}&=5(1.3)+3(0.8)-13\\ &=6.5+2.4-13=-4.1,\\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}&=3(1.3)+2(0.8)-8\\ &=3.9+1.6-8=-2.5. \end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned} w_2&=1.3+0.1(4.1)=1.71,\\ b_2&=0.8+0.1(2.5)=1.05. \end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned} w_2+b_2-3&=1.71+1.05-3=-0.24,\\ 2w_2+b_2-5&=3.42+1.05-5=-0.53,\\ L_2&=\tfrac12\big[0.0576+0.2809\big]\\ &=\tfrac12(0.3385)=0.16925. \end{aligned}\] The whole run on one page:

(c) Step 4 — read the rate off the landing point. One step from \((0,0)\) with the gradient \((-13,-8)\) is \[(w_1,b_1)=\big(0+13\alpha,\ 0+8\alpha\big).\] Match each coordinate: \[\begin{aligned} 13\alpha&=0.26\ \Rightarrow\ \alpha=\tfrac{0.26}{13}=0.02,\\ 8\alpha&=0.16\ \Rightarrow\ \alpha=\tfrac{0.16}{8}=0.02. \end{aligned}\] Both coordinates give \(\alpha=0.02\) — the agreement is the check that the run really was plain gradient descent.
(d) Step 5 — curvature, eigenvalues, speed limit. Differentiate the two partials once more: \[H=\begin{bmatrix}5&3\\3&2\end{bmatrix},\quad \operatorname{tr}H=7,\quad \det H=10-9=1.\] Eigenvalues from \(\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr})\lambda+\det=0\): \[\begin{aligned} \lambda^2-7\lambda+1&=0,\\ \lambda&=\frac{7\pm\sqrt{49-4}}{2}=\frac{7\pm\sqrt{45}}{2},\\ \lambda_{\max}&=6.854102,\\ \lambda_{\min}&=0.145898. \end{aligned}\] The safe range is \(\alpha<2/\lambda_{\max}\): \[\begin{aligned} \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}&=\frac{2}{6.854102}=0.291796,\\ 0.3-0.291796&=0.008204. \end{aligned}\] So \(\alpha=0.3\) is not safe — but it misses by only \(0.008204\), about \(2.8\%\) over the limit, so the divergence would be slow and easy to mistake for noise. (The condition number \(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}=46.98\) is what makes the safe window so narrow in the first place.)

(c) \(\alpha=0.02\), from both coordinates. (d) \(\lambda=6.854102,\ 0.145898\); safe for \(\alpha<0.291796\); \(0.3\) is unsafe by \(0.008204\).

Remember

One step is exactly \(\alpha\) times the gradient vector, which is why a landing point gives \(\alpha\) back — and why both coordinates must agree. The classic slip is updating \(w\) first and then using the new \(w\) inside the \(b\)-gradient: both knobs must move on the same old gradient. The second slip is testing safety against \(\lambda_{\min}\); the speed limit is set by the steepest direction, \(2/\lambda_{\max}\).

Problem 2easy

Four runs on \(f(x)=x^2\) were logged, all starting from \(x_0=1\). A: \(1,\ 0.8,\ 0.64,\ 0.512\). B: \(1,\ -0.5,\ 0.25,\ -0.125\). C: \(1,\ -1.2,\ 1.44,\ -1.728\). D: \(1,\ 0.8,\ 0.54,\ 0.302\). (a) Three of the four are plain gradient descent. Name them, give each one's \(\alpha\), and list the five bands of behaviour of \(\alpha\) on this loss. (b) Name the odd run out, recover its \(\alpha\) and its \(\beta\) (momentum, \(\mathbf v_0=0\)), and verify them on its third step. (c) Re-run B with the decaying rate \(\alpha_t=0.75/(1+t)\): give \(\alpha_0,\alpha_1,\alpha_2\) and \(x_1,x_2,x_3\). (d) Give the first \(t\) with \(\alpha_t<0.1\) and that \(\alpha_t\); the percentage drop from \(\alpha_0\); and the first \(t\) at which the factor \(1-2\alpha_t\) turns positive, so the sign stops flipping.

What this tests. Diagnosis: on \(x^2\) every plain run is a geometric sequence, so the ratio is the fingerprint, and a run whose ratio drifts must be carrying the loaded trolley. Plan. Divide each term by the one before; a constant ratio identifies plain descent and gives \(\alpha\) from \(1-2\alpha\); for the drifting run use \(\mathbf v_0=0\) to get \(\alpha\) from step 1 alone, then \(\beta\) from step 2, then check step 3; finally do the decay arithmetic with two inequalities.

(b) Step 2 — D is the loaded trolley. Its ratios \(0.8,\ 0.675,\ 0.5593\) shrink instead of repeating, so no single \(\alpha\) explains it. (d) Step 5 — three small inequalities. (d) \(t=7\), \(\alpha_7=0.09375\); \(87.5\%\) drop; the factor turns positive at \(t=1\).

Remember

On a quadratic, plain descent is a geometric sequence — so always divide consecutive iterates first. A constant ratio means plain descent and hands you \(\alpha\); a drifting ratio means something is carrying memory. The slip is trying to fit \(\beta\) from step 1, where the trolley is still empty and \(\beta\) cannot appear: step 1 gives \(\alpha\), step 2 gives \(\beta\), step 3 checks them both.

Problem 3medium

Let \(f(x,y)=2x^2+xy+y^2-2x-y\), which is \(\tfrac12\mathbf w^{\!\top}A\mathbf w-\mathbf b^{\!\top}\mathbf w\) with \(A=\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf b=(2,1)\); start at \(\mathbf w_0=(0,0)\), where \(\nabla f=(4x+y-2,\ x+2y-1)\). (a) With direction \(\mathbf d_1=(1,0)\), show it is a descent direction, write \(h(\alpha)=f(\mathbf w_0+\alpha\mathbf d_1)\), minimise it exactly, and give \(\mathbf w_1\) and \(f(\mathbf w_1)\). (b) Take \(\mathbf d_2=-\nabla f(\mathbf w_1)\) and repeat: give \(h(\alpha)\), \(\alpha_2^*\), \(\mathbf w_2\) and \(f(\mathbf w_2)\). (c) Derive the closed form \(\alpha^*=-\dfrac{\nabla f(\mathbf w)^{\!\top}\mathbf d}{\mathbf d^{\!\top}A\mathbf d}\) and evaluate it for (a) and (b). (d) Give the exact minimiser \(\mathbf w^*\) and \(f^*\), and the amount by which \(f(\mathbf w_2)\) still exceeds \(f^*\). Also verify the free check \(\nabla f(\mathbf w_1)^{\!\top}\mathbf d_1=0\) and \(\nabla f(\mathbf w_2)^{\!\top}\mathbf d_2=0\).

What this tests. That "how far do I walk?" is itself a one-variable minimisation, that on a quadratic it has a formula, and that an exact line search always leaves the new gradient perpendicular to the direction just walked. Plan. Substitute the line into \(f\) to get a parabola in \(\alpha\); set \(h'(\alpha)=0\); step; repeat once; then do the same substitution with symbols to get the formula, and finish with \(\nabla f=\mathbf 0\) for the exact answer.

Show the full solution
(a) Step 1 — is \(\mathbf d_1\) downhill? At \(\mathbf w_0=(0,0)\), \[\nabla f(\mathbf w_0)=(-2,\,-1),\qquad \nabla f^{\!\top}\mathbf d_1=-2<0,\] so yes: moving along \(+x\) lowers \(f\).
Step 2 — the walk becomes a parabola. Put \((x,y)=(\alpha,0)\) into \(f\): \[\begin{aligned} h(\alpha)&=2\alpha^2+\alpha(0)+0-2\alpha-0\\ &=2\alpha^2-2\alpha. \end{aligned}\] Minimise it: \[\begin{aligned} h'(\alpha)&=4\alpha-2=0,\\ \alpha_1^*&=0.5,\qquad h''=4>0. \end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned} \mathbf w_1&=(0,0)+0.5(1,0)=(0.5,\,0),\\ f(\mathbf w_1)&=2(0.25)-2(0.5)=-0.5. \end{aligned}\]
(b) Step 3 — the second leg. The gradient at \(\mathbf w_1\): \[\begin{aligned} \nabla f(\mathbf w_1)&=\big(4(0.5)+0-2,\ 0.5+0-1\big)\\ &=(0,\,-0.5),\\ \mathbf d_2&=-\nabla f(\mathbf w_1)=(0,\,0.5). \end{aligned}\] The line is \((0.5,\,0.5\alpha)\). Substitute, term by term: \[\begin{aligned} 2x^2&=2(0.25)=0.5,\\ xy&=0.5(0.5\alpha)=0.25\alpha,\\ y^2&=0.25\alpha^2,\\ -2x&=-1,\qquad -y=-0.5\alpha. \end{aligned}\] Add: \[\begin{aligned} h(\alpha)&=0.25\alpha^2+0.25\alpha-0.5\alpha+0.5-1\\ &=0.25\alpha^2-0.25\alpha-0.5. \end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned} h'(\alpha)&=0.5\alpha-0.25=0,\\ \alpha_2^*&=0.5,\\ \mathbf w_2&=(0.5,\,0)+0.5(0,0.5)\\ &=(0.5,\,0.25),\\ f(\mathbf w_2)&=0.25(0.25)-0.25(0.5)-0.5\\ &=0.0625-0.125-0.5=-0.5625. \end{aligned}\]
(c) Step 4 — the formula, once and for all. Expand \(f(\mathbf w+\alpha\mathbf d)\) with \(f=\tfrac12\mathbf w^{\!\top}A\mathbf w-\mathbf b^{\!\top}\mathbf w\): \[\begin{aligned} f(\mathbf w+\alpha\mathbf d)&=\tfrac12(\mathbf w+\alpha\mathbf d)^{\!\top}A(\mathbf w+\alpha\mathbf d)\\ &\qquad-\mathbf b^{\!\top}(\mathbf w+\alpha\mathbf d). \end{aligned}\] Because \(A\) is symmetric, \(\mathbf w^{\!\top}A\mathbf d=\mathbf d^{\!\top}A\mathbf w\), so the two cross terms merge: \[\begin{aligned} &=f(\mathbf w)+\alpha\,\mathbf d^{\!\top}(A\mathbf w-\mathbf b)\\ &\qquad+\tfrac{\alpha^2}{2}\,\mathbf d^{\!\top}A\mathbf d. \end{aligned}\] But \(A\mathbf w-\mathbf b=\nabla f(\mathbf w)\), so this is a parabola in \(\alpha\) with slope \(\nabla f^{\!\top}\mathbf d\) and curvature \(\mathbf d^{\!\top}A\mathbf d\). Setting the derivative to zero: \[\alpha^*=-\frac{\nabla f(\mathbf w)^{\!\top}\mathbf d}{\mathbf d^{\!\top}A\mathbf d}.\] Check it on both legs: \[\begin{aligned} \text{(a)}\quad \mathbf d_1^{\!\top}A\mathbf d_1&=4,\\ \alpha_1^*&=-\frac{-2}{4}=0.5\ \checkmark\\ \text{(b)}\quad \nabla f(\mathbf w_1)^{\!\top}\mathbf d_2&=-0.25,\\ \mathbf d_2^{\!\top}A\mathbf d_2&=2(0.5)^2=0.5,\\ \alpha_2^*&=-\frac{-0.25}{0.5}=0.5\ \checkmark \end{aligned}\]
(d) Step 5 — the exact bottom, and how close we got. Set \(\nabla f=\mathbf 0\): \[\begin{aligned} 4x+y&=2,\\ x+2y&=1. \end{aligned}\] From the first, \(y=2-4x\); substitute: \[\begin{aligned} x+2(2-4x)&=1,\\ -7x&=-3,\qquad x=\tfrac37,\\ y&=2-\tfrac{12}{7}=\tfrac27. \end{aligned}\] Evaluate over a denominator of \(49\): \[\begin{aligned} f^*&=\tfrac{18}{49}+\tfrac{6}{49}+\tfrac{4}{49}-\tfrac{42}{49}-\tfrac{14}{49}\\ &=-\tfrac{28}{49}=-\tfrac47\approx-0.571429. \end{aligned}\] Two exact line searches got to \(-0.5625\), still \[-0.5625-(-0.571429)=0.008929\] above the floor.
Step 6 — the free check. After an exact line search the parabola is at its bottom, so the directional derivative along \(\mathbf d\) is zero — i.e. the new gradient is perpendicular to the direction just walked: \[\begin{aligned} \nabla f(\mathbf w_1)^{\!\top}\mathbf d_1&=(0,-0.5)\cdot(1,0)=0,\\ \nabla f(\mathbf w_2)&=(0.25,\,0),\\ \nabla f(\mathbf w_2)^{\!\top}\mathbf d_2&=(0.25,0)\cdot(0,0.5)=0. \end{aligned}\] Both are exactly zero, which is why consecutive exact-search directions turn a right angle — the zig-zag is not a bug, it is a theorem.

answers at a glance: (a) descent since \(-2<0\); \(h=2\alpha^2-2\alpha\), \(\alpha_1^*=0.5\), \(\mathbf w_1=(0.5,0)\), \(f=-0.5\). (b) \(h=0.25\alpha^2-0.25\alpha-0.5\), \(\alpha_2^*=0.5\), \(\mathbf w_2=(0.5,0.25)\), \(f=-0.5625\). (c) \(\alpha^*=-\nabla f^{\!\top}\mathbf d/(\mathbf d^{\!\top}A\mathbf d)\); \(2/4=0.5\) and \(0.25/0.5=0.5\). (d) \(\mathbf w^*=(3/7,\,2/7)\), \(f^*=-4/7\approx-0.571429\), excess \(0.008929\); both orthogonality checks give \(0\).

Remember

"How far?" is its own one-variable problem, and on a quadratic its answer is \(-\nabla f^{\!\top}\mathbf d/(\mathbf d^{\!\top}A\mathbf d)\) — slope over curvature, measured along the direction you are actually walking. Always spend the one line on \(\nabla f(\mathbf w_{\text{new}})^{\!\top}\mathbf d=0\): if it is not zero, the line search was not exact. The slip is minimising \(f\) over \((x,y)\) instead of over the single number \(\alpha\).

Problem 14hard

A one-dimensional dataset has two points: \(x_1=+1\) with \(y_1=+1\), and \(x_2=-1\) with \(y_2=-1\). The largest-margin classifier solves \(\min_{w,b}\tfrac12w^2\) subject to \(y_i(wx_i+b)\ge1\). (a) Write both constraints in standard form and give the Lagrangian with \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2\ge0\). (b) Give the two stationarity equations and reduce the Lagrangian to a function \(q(\alpha)\) of a single variable. (c) Maximise \(q\): give \(\alpha^*\), \(d^*\), \(w^*\), \(b^*\), the primal value, the gap, the decision boundary, the margin edges, the margin width, and the number of support vectors. (d) A third point \(x_3=3\) with \(y_3=+1\) is added: give \(\alpha_3\) and say what happens to the classifier. Then, with a point at \(x_3<1\) of class \(+1\) and the point at \(-1\) both binding, derive \(w\) and \(b\) as formulas in \(x_3\), and evaluate them, the boundary and the margin at \(x_3=0.5\).

What this tests. Every move of the unit chained together — framing, standard form, Lagrangian, dual, solve, recover, read the slackness — on the problem the whole subject is built toward. Plan. Write the two constraints out; differentiate in \(w\) and in \(b\) (the \(b\)-equation is what collapses two multipliers into one); substitute to get a one-variable concave parabola; solve it; then use \(\alpha_i>0\Rightarrow\) constraint tight to pin \(b\).

Show the full solution
(a) Step 1 — the two constraints, written out. For \(i=1\): \(y_1(wx_1+b)=(+1)(w+b)\ge1\). For \(i=2\): \(y_2(wx_2+b)=(-1)(-w+b)=w-b\ge1\). In standard form, \[\begin{aligned} g_1&=1-w-b\le0,\\ g_2&=1-w+b\le0, \end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned} L&=\tfrac12w^2+\alpha_1(1-w-b)\\ &\quad+\alpha_2(1-w+b),\qquad \alpha_i\ge0. \end{aligned}\]
(b) Step 2 — stationarity, and the collapse to one variable. \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}&=w-\alpha_1-\alpha_2=0\\ &\Rightarrow\ w=\alpha_1+\alpha_2,\\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}&=-\alpha_1+\alpha_2=0\\ &\Rightarrow\ \alpha_1=\alpha_2. \end{aligned}\] (These are the two standard identities \(w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i\) and \(\sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0\), written out for this data.) Regroup \(L\): \[\begin{aligned} L&=\tfrac12w^2-w(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)\\ &\quad-b(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)+(\alpha_1+\alpha_2). \end{aligned}\] Write \(\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha\), so the \(b\)-term vanishes and \(w=2\alpha\): \[\begin{aligned} q(\alpha)&=\tfrac12(2\alpha)^2-(2\alpha)(2\alpha)+2\alpha\\ &=2\alpha^2-4\alpha^2+2\alpha\\ &=2\alpha-2\alpha^2. \end{aligned}\] (Cross-check against \(\sum_i\alpha_i-\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_jx_ix_j\): here every product \(y_iy_jx_ix_j=1\), so it reads \(2\alpha-\tfrac12(2\alpha)^2=2\alpha-2\alpha^2\) — the same function.)
(c) Step 3 — solve, recover, read. \[\begin{aligned} q'(\alpha)&=2-4\alpha=0,\\ \alpha^*&=\tfrac12\ \ (\ge0\ \checkmark),\\ d^*&=q\big(\tfrac12\big)=1-\tfrac12=\tfrac12. \end{aligned}\] Recover the classifier: \[w^*=\alpha_1^*+\alpha_2^*=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1.\] Because \(\alpha_1^*=\tfrac12>0\), complementary slackness forces its constraint tight: \[\begin{aligned} w+b&=1,\\ b^*&=1-1=0. \end{aligned}\] Consistency: the second constraint gives \(w-b=1-0=1\), also tight, matching \(\alpha_2^*>0\). The primal value is \(\tfrac12(1)^2=\tfrac12=d^*\), so the gap is \(0\). Geometry: \[\begin{aligned} \text{boundary: }&w^*x+b^*=0\ \Rightarrow\ x=0,\\ \text{margin edges: }&w^*x+b^*=\pm1\ \Rightarrow\ x=\pm1,\\ \text{width: }&\frac{2}{|w^*|}=2. \end{aligned}\] Both \(\alpha_i>0\), so both points are support vectors: \(\mathbf 2\) of them, out of \(2\).
(d) Step 4 — a point that does not matter. Adding \(x_3=3\) with \(y_3=+1\) adds the constraint \(3w+b\ge1\). At the current answer \((w,b)=(1,0)\) it reads \[3(1)+0=3\ \ge1,\] slack by \(2\). In standard form \(g_3=1-3w-b=-2<0\), and complementary slackness \(\alpha_3g_3=0\) with \(g_3\ne0\) forces \[\alpha_3=0.\] Since \(w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i\), a point with \(\alpha_i=0\) contributes nothing: the classifier does not move, and the point can be deleted from the training set without changing a thing.
Step 5 — now move that point inside. Suppose instead the \(+1\) point sits at \(x_3<1\), close enough that its constraint becomes tight, with the \(-1\) point at \(-1\) also tight: \[\begin{aligned} wx_3+b&=1,\\ w-b&=1\ \Rightarrow\ b=w-1. \end{aligned}\] Substitute: \[\begin{aligned} wx_3+w-1&=1,\\ w(x_3+1)&=2,\\ w&=\frac{2}{x_3+1}, \end{aligned}\] \[\begin{aligned} b&=1-wx_3=\frac{x_3+1-2x_3}{x_3+1}\\ &=\frac{1-x_3}{1+x_3}. \end{aligned}\] At \(x_3=0.5\): \[\begin{aligned} w&=\frac{2}{1.5}=1.3333,\\ b&=\frac{0.5}{1.5}=0.3333,\\ \text{boundary }x&=-\frac bw=-\frac{0.3333}{1.3333}=-0.25,\\ \text{margin }&=\frac{2}{|w|}=1.5. \end{aligned}\] Dragging the positive point from \(3\) (where it was ignored entirely) in to \(0.5\) squeezes the margin from \(2\) to \(1.5\) and shifts the boundary from \(0\) to \(-0.25\). Nothing about the point changed except whether its constraint was tight — and that is the whole difference between a data point that matters and one that does not.

Remember

Complementary slackness is why these classifiers are sparse: \(\alpha_i=0\) means the point sits strictly outside the margin and can be thrown away, while \(\alpha_i>0\) means its constraint is tight and it is holding the boundary up. The multiplier is the answer to "which data matter". The slip is solving for \(b\) from stationarity — \(\partial L/\partial b\) only gives \(\sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0\) and never mentions \(b\); \(b\) comes from setting a support vector's constraint to equality.
