## Unit 16 · Words as Vectors
Hub promise: "Teaching a machine what words mean. From counting word pairs to a neural language model (perplexity is the exponential of cross-entropy), a co-occurrence matrix squeezed by the SVD, word2vec, and the geometry of meaning." The aha: "Meaning is a direction: king − man + woman lands next to queen." Builds on 3, 5, 12, 14, 15.

**Hero (3-D):** words float as glowing points in a dark space; tea words (chai, coffee, milk, cup) drift together, cricket words (bat, wicket, over, six) drift together; an arrow man → woman is copied and placed at king, and its tip lands next to queen. HUD: "a word is where it lives". Loop; reduced motion → final still.

**Sections (suggested; ids s1…s12 + spractice):**
- s1 · Why a computer can't read a word — a word is just a label; one-hot vectors (a list of zeros with one 1); every pair of one-hot words is equally far apart and has dot product 0, so "chai" is as far from "coffee" as from "cricket". Roadmap.
- s2 · Guess the next word by counting (n-grams) — tiny corpus (e.g. "I drink chai", "I drink coffee", "I play cricket"…); bigram table; P(next | previous) as row counts ÷ row total; zero counts problem and add-one smoothing. **w-bigram**: type/edit a small corpus → live count table and next-word bars; generate text by sampling.
- s3 · Scoring a language model: perplexity — average surprise from Unit 14; model gives the four actual next words probabilities 0.5, 0.25, 0.5, 0.125 → average surprise 1.75 bits → perplexity 2^1.75 ≈ 3.364 ("as unsure as choosing among about 3.4 equal options"); a uniform guess over V words has perplexity V. **w-perplexity** (sliders for per-word probabilities; the "effective number of choices" as dice).
- s4 · You shall know a word by the company it keeps — the co-occurrence matrix. Worked 4×4 (rows chai, coffee, cricket, football; columns drink, hot, play, match): chai (5,4,0,1), coffee (4,5,0,0), cricket (0,0,5,4), football (0,1,4,5). Cosine(chai, coffee) ≈ 0.964, cosine(chai, cricket) ≈ 0.096, cosine(cricket, football) ≈ 0.964. **w-cooc** (window-size slider over a small corpus builds the matrix live; click two rows → cosine as the angle between arrows).
- s5 · Squeeze it with the SVD (Unit 5) — singular values of that matrix 9.528, 8.528, 1.143, 1.087; keeping 2 keeps 98.5% of the energy (Σσ²); rank-2 rebuild error (Frobenius) ≈ 1.578; 2-D word coordinates U₂Σ₂ ≈ chai (−5.009, 4.032), coffee (−4.531, 4.453), cricket (−4.450, −4.540), football (−5.037, −4.004) (signs may flip — say so); in 2-D cosine(chai, coffee) ≈ 0.995, cosine(chai, cricket) ≈ 0.097. Also mention raw counts are dominated by common words → PPMI (in words, one formula in a drawer). **w-svd-words (3-D)**: a bigger toy corpus's words projected to 3-D by SVD; drag k from 1 to 3+; clusters appear.
- s6 · Learning vectors instead of counting: word2vec — skip-gram as a guessing game ("given chai, is hot a real neighbour or a random word?"); score = sigmoid(u·v) (Unit 13/14); negative sampling. Worked: v_chai = (1, 0), u_hot = (0.5, 0.5), u_football = (−0.5, 1) (a negative): σ(v·u_hot) = σ(0.5) ≈ 0.622, σ(−v·u_football) = σ(0.5) ≈ 0.622, loss = −ln 0.622 − ln 0.622 ≈ 0.948; one gradient step pulls hot toward chai and pushes football away (compute it). **w-w2v**: live skip-gram training in the browser on a small corpus; 2-D (or 3-D) points moving as training runs; loss curve; pick the window and number of negatives.
- s7 · Why not a softmax over every word? — the full softmax (Unit 14) needs a sum over the whole vocabulary (50 000 exps per step); negative sampling asks k small yes/no questions instead. Compare costs.
- s8 · A neural language model — embed the previous words, feed a small network (Unit 15), softmax over the vocabulary, train with cross-entropy; the embedding table is just the first layer's weight matrix (one-hot × matrix = picking a row). **w-nlm**: pick 2 previous words → embeddings concatenate → hidden layer → probability bars for the next word.
- s9 · The geometry of meaning — analogies as parallelograms. Toy 2-D vectors (say plainly they are hand-made): king (3, 2), man (1, 2), woman (1, −2), queen (3, −2) → king − man + woman = (3, −2) = queen; nearest-neighbour search by cosine (exclude the input words — say why). **w-analogy (3-D, flagship "embedding explorer")**: ~60 words in 3-D with clearly labelled toy/SVD-trained vectors; type or pick A − B + C; the parallelogram draws; nearest neighbours listed; directions like gender / royalty / tea-vs-cricket as arrows you can toggle.
- s10 · What embeddings get wrong — one vector per word ("bank" of a river vs a bank account; "bat" in cricket vs the animal); bias copied from text (neutral statement, one example, no politics). Sets up Unit 18's context-dependent vectors. **w-polysemy**: "bat" sits between the cricket cluster and the animal cluster.
- s11 · (optional) Measuring similarity well — cosine vs dot product vs Euclidean; normalisation. **w-cosine** (drag two arrows).
- s12 · What to carry forward + thread to Units 17 (read one word at a time, with memory) and 18 (vectors that change with context).
- spractice · 14 problems: one-hot dot products; bigram probabilities from counts (+ add-one smoothing); perplexity from probabilities; perplexity of a uniform model; cosine similarity of count rows; singular values / rank-k energy of a small matrix; reconstruct a rank-1 approximation; skip-gram sigmoid scores + loss; one SGD step of skip-gram; cost of full softmax vs k negatives; analogy arithmetic + nearest neighbour by cosine; embedding lookup as one-hot × matrix; parameter count of an embedding table + NLM; diagnose which of two models is better from recorded perplexities.

## Unit 17 · Machines with Memory
Hub promise: "Reading one word at a time. The recurrent cell, backprop through time, why gradients fade or explode, LSTM and GRU gates, and the encoder–decoder with its bottleneck." The aha: "The gradient carries powers of one matrix, so its eigenvalues decide whether memory fades away or blows up." Builds on 4, 7, 15, 16.

**Hero (3-D):** a sentence scrolls in word by word ("the train to Delhi is late"); one glowing memory orb (the hidden state) is carried along a conveyor, changing colour with each word; then a red gradient pulse travels back along the conveyor, shrinking at each step (fading) — flip a switch and it grows instead (exploding); final beat: an LSTM "express lane" (the cell state) runs above, carrying the pulse back almost undimmed. Loop; reduced motion → final still.

**Sections (suggested):**
- s1 · Why order matters — "dog bites man" vs "man bites dog"; a bag of words (Unit 16) loses order; a fixed window forgets anything older. Idea: read one word at a time and keep a running note (like a shopkeeper's running total). Roadmap.
- s2 · The recurrent cell — hₜ = tanh(W hₜ₋₁ + U xₜ + b); the SAME weights at every step (unrolling = one network copied along time, weights shared). Worked scalar RNN: w = 0.5, u = 1, b = 0, h₀ = 0, inputs x = (1, 0, 0) → h₁ = tanh(1) ≈ 0.7616, h₂ = tanh(0.3808) ≈ 0.3634, h₃ = tanh(0.1817) ≈ 0.1797 (the memory of the first word fades). **w-rnn-step**: step a small RNN through a sentence; the hidden-state vector as bars/heat strip; weight sliders.
- s3 · Unrolling in time (3-D) — **w-unroll (3-D)**: the loop unfolded into a chain of identical cells; the shared W glows on every copy at once.
- s4 · Many shapes of the same idea — one-to-many (caption), many-to-one (sentiment of a review), many-to-many (tagging each word), and sequence-to-sequence; output yₜ = softmax(V hₜ) for next-character / next-word prediction (Unit 14/16). **w-shapes** picker.
- s5 · Backprop through time — Unit 15's Rule A and Rule B, applied at every time step, with the weight gradient summed over steps (shared weights). Worked: ∂h₃/∂h₂ = w(1 − h₃²) ≈ 0.4838, ∂h₂/∂h₁ = w(1 − h₂²) ≈ 0.4340, so ∂h₃/∂h₁ ≈ 0.2100. **w-bptt**: a step machine (like Unit 15's) walking forward then backward through 4–5 time steps, blame shown in red shrinking with each step.
- s6 · Why memory fades or explodes (flagship idea) — for a linear cell hₜ = w hₜ₋₁ the blame after T steps is w^T: w = 0.5, T = 10 → 0.5¹⁰ ≈ 0.000977; w = 1.5 → 1.5¹⁰ ≈ 57.67. With a matrix it is powers of W, and the eigenvalues decide (Unit 4): W = [[0.9, 0.4], [0.1, 0.6]] has eigenvalues 1 and 0.5 — the part along the first eigenvector survives, the rest halves each step. tanh′ ≤ 1 only makes fading worse. **w-eigen-memory (3-D)**: a 2×2 W you edit; a vector repeatedly multiplied by W traces its path; eigen-directions drawn (Unit 4 colours); a readout of |λ|max and a bar chart of ‖Wᵗ v‖ on a log axis for t = 1…30; presets fade / keep / explode / rotate (complex eigenvalues).
- s7 · First aid for exploding gradients: clipping — if ‖g‖ > c, rescale to length c. Worked: g = (30, 40), ‖g‖ = 50, c = 5 → (3, 4). **w-clip** (a loss cliff, steps with/without clipping). (Fading needs a new design → s8.)
- s8 · LSTM: a memory with an express lane — the cell state cₜ = fₜ ⊙ cₜ₋₁ + iₜ ⊙ gₜ; forget / input / output gates are sigmoids (0 = shut, 1 = open) — like a notebook where you decide what to erase, what to write, what to read out. Worked: f = 0.9, previous c = 1, i = 0.2, candidate g = 0.5 → c = 0.9·1 + 0.2·0.5 = 1.0. Along the express lane the blame is multiplied by f each step: 0.9¹⁰ ≈ 0.349 vs the plain RNN's fading; 0.9⁵⁰ ≈ 0.00515 (it still fades if the gate is not near 1 — honest). **w-lstm (flagship "memory lab")**: a live cell you step through a sentence, gates as valves with gold fill, the cell state as a glowing tank; a copy-task / "remember the first word" experiment comparing RNN vs LSTM vs GRU over lengths 5–50 (trained or with sensible hand-set weights; say which) with the gradient-size-through-time chart.
- s9 · GRU: the lighter cousin — update gate z and reset gate r; hₜ = (1 − zₜ) ⊙ hₜ₋₁ + zₜ ⊙ h̃ₜ (a blend dial between old and new). Compare parameter counts RNN vs GRU vs LSTM for hidden size h and input size d: RNN h(h + d) + h, GRU 3(h(h + d) + h), LSTM 4(h(h + d) + h) (compute an example, e.g. h = 128, d = 64). **w-gates-compare**.
- s10 · Encoder–decoder: translate with a bottleneck — English → Hindi: the encoder reads the whole sentence into one final vector; the decoder writes the translation word by word from it; teacher forcing during training. The bottleneck: a 5-word and a 50-word sentence squeezed into the same size vector — quality drops with length. **w-seq2seq (3-D or lavish 2-D)**: words flow into one "summary" orb then out; a length slider shows the orb overflowing; teaser "what if the decoder could look back at every word?" → Unit 18.
- s11 · Picking the output words — greedy vs beam search (small worked tree with probabilities; beam width 2 finds a better whole sentence than greedy). **w-beam**.
- s12 · What to carry forward + thread to Unit 18 (attention removes the bottleneck and the step-by-step chain) and Unit 19.
- spractice · 14 problems: forward pass of a scalar RNN; a 2-D RNN step with matrices; unrolled parameter sharing (count parameters vs time steps); BPTT gradient through 3 steps; w^T fading/exploding and the T at which it drops below 10⁻³; eigenvalues of a 2×2 W → fade or explode; gradient clipping; one LSTM cell step with given gates; LSTM express-lane decay f^T; GRU blend step; parameter counts RNN/GRU/LSTM; greedy vs beam search on a small tree; sequence-to-sequence shapes; diagnose a recorded run (gradient norms per step → which fix).

## Unit 18 · Attention and Transformers
Hub promise: "Every word asks every other word a question. Queries, keys and values, why we divide by √d, softmax weights, many heads as many subspaces, positions as rotations, residual paths and layer norm, causal masking." Read the full card in `dev/src/index.html` (its aha + Builds-on) and match it.

**Hero (3-D):** a sentence ("the batsman hit the ball because it was loose") as a row of glowing tokens; from "it" a query beam sweeps over every other token, beams brighten by attention weight (gold), the brightest lands on "ball"; the values flow back into "it" and it changes colour — a new, context-aware vector. Then the whole grid lights up: every word asks every word at once (an n × n heat map rising as a 3-D landscape). Loop; reduced motion → final still.

**Sections (suggested):**
- s1 · The problem Unit 17 left — one word at a time is slow and the summary vector is a bottleneck. Idea: let every word look at every other word directly ("a class where every student can ask any other student a question at once"). Roadmap.
- s2 · Attention as a soft lookup — a dictionary returns the value of the one exact matching key; attention returns a weighted blend of ALL values, weights = how well each key matches the query (dot product, Unit 3), turned into probabilities by softmax (Unit 14). **w-lookup**: hard lookup vs soft lookup side by side.
- s3 · Queries, keys and values, worked by hand — keys k₁ = (1, 0), k₂ = (0, 1), k₃ = (1, 1); values v₁ = (2, 0), v₂ = (0, 2), v₃ = (1, 1); d = 2. Query q = (2, 0): scores q·k/√2 = (1.414, 0, 1.414), weights ≈ (0.446, 0.108, 0.446), output ≈ (1.337, 0.663). Q, K, V come from the same word vectors through three learned matrices W_Q, W_K, W_V ("what am I looking for / what do I contain / what do I pass on"). The full matrix form softmax(QKᵀ/√d)V with Q rows (2, 0), (0, 2), (1, 1): attention matrix rows ≈ (0.446, 0.108, 0.446), (0.108, 0.446, 0.446), (0.248, 0.248, 0.504); outputs ≈ (1.337, 0.663), (0.663, 1.337), (1, 1). **w-qkv (3-D)**: the query arrow and key arrows in 3-D (or 2-D lavish), dot products as projections, weights as gold beams, the output as the blended arrow; drag q.
- s4 · Why divide by √d — for random vectors with unit-variance entries the dot product's spread grows like √d (d = 2 → ≈ 1.41, d = 64 → 8, d = 512 → ≈ 22.6); big scores make softmax spiky (nearly one-hot) and its gradients vanish; dividing by √d keeps the spread ≈ 1. **w-scale**: d slider, histogram of raw vs scaled scores, the softmax of a sample row, and its gradient size.
- s5 · The attention map — n × n weights for a real-looking sentence; "it" → "ball". **w-attnmap (flagship "attention lab")**: type a short sentence; token vectors (toy, labelled as such, e.g. from Unit 16's style or hand-designed) → Q, K, V with editable/learned small matrices; heat map per head; click a token to see its beams; causal-mask toggle; temperature; show the output vectors changing. Honest: toy weights, not a trained model — but the maths is exactly the real one.
- s6 · Many heads — split d into h heads of size d/h, each head attends differently (one to the previous word, one to the subject…), concatenate and mix with W_O; cost is the same as one big head. Worked: d = 512, h = 8 → each head 64. **w-heads** (two or more heads with different attention maps on the same sentence).
- s7 · Where is each word? Positions — attention alone is order-blind (shuffle the words, get the same set of outputs — show it). Sinusoidal position vectors (a set of clock hands turning at different speeds) and rotary positions (RoPE): rotate q by mθ and k by nθ and the dot product depends only on m − n. Worked: θ = 30°, q = k = (1, 0): positions (3, 1), (5, 3), (2, 0) all give q·k = cos 60° = 0.5. **w-rope (3-D)**: clock hands / rotating arrows; move both tokens together and the score stays the same.
- s8 · The transformer block — attention → add the input back (residual, a shortcut for blame — Unit 15/17) → layer norm → a small feed-forward network on each token → add & norm again. Layer norm worked: x = (1, 2, 3, 6) → mean 3, variance 3.5 → (−1.069, −0.535, 0, 1.604) (then scale and shift). Parameter count of one block for d = 512, d_ff = 2048 (attention 4d² + biases; FFN 2·d·d_ff + biases — compute exactly with and without biases). **w-block (3-D)**: a token vector travelling up through the block's stations, each changing it; residual stream shown as the express lane.
- s9 · Causal masking: no peeking at the future — for next-word prediction a word may only look left; set the upper triangle to −∞ before softmax. Worked with the s3 matrices: masked weights rows (1, 0, 0), (0.196, 0.804, 0), (0.248, 0.248, 0.504); outputs (2, 0), (0.391, 1.609), (1, 1). Encoder (sees both ways) vs decoder (masked) vs encoder–decoder with cross-attention (the fix for Unit 17's bottleneck). **w-mask**.
- s10 · The price: n² — attention compares every pair: 1 000 tokens → 1 000 000 scores per head per layer; compare with an RNN's n steps (but the RNN cannot run in parallel). **w-cost** (n slider, memory/time curves for RNN vs attention).
- s11 · A tiny transformer, whole — stack blocks, add an embedding table at the bottom and a softmax over the vocabulary at the top (Units 14, 16), train by cross-entropy with backprop (Unit 15). Walk one short sentence through a 1-block, 2-head toy model end to end with every number visible. **w-tiny-transformer** (step machine).
- s12 · What to carry forward + thread to Unit 19 (scale this to an LLM: next-token likelihood, sampling with temperature/top-k/top-p, KV cache, LoRA) and Unit 20.
- spractice · 14 problems: one attention output by hand; full softmax(QKᵀ/√d)V for a 3-token example; effect of √d scaling on a given score pair; spread of dot products for a given d; causal-mask weights; multi-head shapes (d, h, d_k, parameter count of W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O); RoPE score for given positions; sinusoidal position values at given pos/i; layer norm of a vector; parameter count of a block; attention cost n² for given n, heads, layers; residual + layer norm step; which token gets the most attention after changing one key (reverse-engineer); diagnose a spiky attention row (scores too big → which fix).
