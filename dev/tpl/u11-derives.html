Prove it · one stride, one factor per direction

Claim. On \(J=\tfrac12\sum_i\lambda_iw_i^2\), one step of plain descent multiplies each coordinate by \(1-\eta\lambda_i\); the walk converges in every direction exactly when \(0<\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}\); and on \(J=\tfrac12w_1^2+5w_2^2\) at \(\eta=0.1\) the gentle coordinate needs \(44\) steps to fall inside \(1\,\%\) of zero.

1
The gradient separates, because the loss does: \(\partial J/\partial w_i=\lambda_iw_i\), with no other coordinate in it. A diagonal quadratic is \(d\) one-dimensional problems wearing one coat. Everything below is a statement about one coordinate.
2
So one step is a multiplication: \[\begin{aligned}w_i&\leftarrow w_i-\eta\lambda_iw_i=(1-\eta\lambda_i)\,w_i,\\[2pt] w_i^{(k)}&=(1-\eta\lambda_i)^k\,w_i^{(0)}.\end{aligned}\] No approximation anywhere — this is the exact iterate after \(k\) steps.
3
A geometric sequence shrinks to zero exactly when its ratio is strictly inside \((-1,1)\): \[|1-\eta\lambda_i|<1\iff 0<\eta\lambda_i<2\iff 0<\eta<\frac{2}{\lambda_i}.\] All directions survive only if the tightest bound holds, so \(\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}\). The stiffest direction alone sets the speed limit. Every other direction is then travelling slower than it could.
4
Here \(\lambda=(1,10)\), so \(\eta<0.2\). At \(\eta=0.1\) the factors are \(0.9\) and \(0\): the steep coordinate is annihilated in one step, and for the gentle one \[0.9^{\,k}\le0.01\iff k\ge\frac{\ln 0.01}{\ln 0.9}=43.7\ \Rightarrow\ k=44.\] At \(\eta=0.2\) the second factor is exactly \(-1\), so \(w_2\) alternates \(\pm1\) for ever: no convergence, no divergence. ∎ One step for one coordinate, forty-four for the other, from the same \(\eta\). That ratio is the condition number \(\kappa=10\).
Prove it · what your feet could not tell you, written down

Claim. If \(\nabla f\) does not change faster than a rate \(L\) (that is, \(\|\nabla f(\mathbf u)-\nabla f(\mathbf v)\|\le L\|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|\)), then one step guarantees \(f(\mathbf w-\eta\nabla f)\le f(\mathbf w)-\eta\big(1-\tfrac{\eta L}{2}\big)\|\nabla f\|^2\) — so progress is guaranteed exactly while \(\eta<2/L\), and is largest at \(\eta=1/L\).

1
A Lipschitz gradient bounds the Taylor remainder: for any \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\), \[f(\mathbf v)\le f(\mathbf u)+\nabla f(\mathbf u)^{\!\top}(\mathbf v-\mathbf u)+\frac{L}{2}\|\mathbf v-\mathbf u\|^2.\] This is the only place the missing second-order information enters — as a worst case, not a measurement. \(L\) is how sharply the tilt is allowed to turn.
2
Put \(\mathbf v=\mathbf w-\eta\nabla f(\mathbf w)\) and \(\mathbf u=\mathbf w\), writing \(\mathbf g=\nabla f(\mathbf w)\): \[\begin{aligned}f(\mathbf w-\eta\mathbf g)&\le f(\mathbf w)-\eta\|\mathbf g\|^2+\frac{L\eta^2}{2}\|\mathbf g\|^2\\ &=f(\mathbf w)-\eta\Big(1-\frac{\eta L}{2}\Big)\|\mathbf g\|^2.\end{aligned}\] The first correction pays you \(\eta\|\mathbf g\|^2\); the second charges you \(\tfrac12L\eta^2\|\mathbf g\|^2\). One is linear in the stride, the other quadratic, so the charge wins eventually.
3
The bracket is positive exactly when \(\eta<2/L\), and \(\eta(1-\eta L/2)\) is largest at \(\eta=1/L\), where the guaranteed drop is \(\|\mathbf g\|^2/(2L)\). ∎ Read it as the blindfold argument, quantified: the safe stride is set by how fast the tilt can turn, and your feet never measured that. On a quadratic \(L=\lambda_{\max}\) and this reproduces the bound above.
Prove it · the speed-up is \(1/(1-\beta)\)

Claim. If a direction receives the same gradient \(g\) at every step, momentum's step size rises from \(\alpha g\) to \(\alpha g/(1-\beta)\) — a factor of \(10\) at \(\beta=0.9\).

1
Unroll the velocity from \(v_0=0\) with \(g\) fixed: \[\begin{aligned}v_1&=-\alpha g\\ v_2&=\beta v_1-\alpha g=-\alpha g(1+\beta)\\ v_t&=-\alpha g\,(1+\beta+\beta^2+\cdots+\beta^{\,t-1}).\end{aligned}\] Each old push survives multiplied by \(\beta\) once more, so the surviving copies form a geometric series.
2
Sum it. Since \(0\le\beta<1\), \[\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\beta^{\,k}=\frac{1-\beta^{\,t}}{1-\beta}\ \xrightarrow[\ t\to\infty\ ]{}\ \frac{1}{1-\beta},\qquad v_\infty=-\frac{\alpha g}{1-\beta}.\] At \(\beta=0.9\) the limit is \(10\alpha g\), and the series is already at \(0.9\) of it by step \(22\).
3
Now the other half. If the gradient alternates, \(g,-g,g,-g,\dots\), the same unrolling gives \(v_t=-\alpha g(1-\beta+\beta^2-\cdots)\to-\alpha g/(1+\beta)\), which at \(\beta=0.9\) is \(0.53\,\alpha g\) — about a twentieth of the consistent case. ∎ The ratio of the two limits is \((1+\beta)/(1-\beta)=19\) at \(\beta=0.9\). That single number is why the bars stack in one strip and cancel in the other, and it also shows why \(\beta\ge1\) is fatal: the first series stops converging.
Prove it · the two ways of writing momentum are the same rule

Claim. \(\mathbf v_t=\beta\mathbf v_{t-1}-\alpha\nabla J(\mathbf w_{t-1}),\ \mathbf w_t=\mathbf w_{t-1}+\mathbf v_t\) is identical to \(\mathbf w_t=\mathbf w_{t-1}-\alpha\nabla J(\mathbf w_{t-1})+\beta(\mathbf w_{t-1}-\mathbf w_{t-2})\); and on \(J=\tfrac12w_1^2+5w_2^2\) with \(\alpha=0.1,\beta=0.9\) the first three points are \([0.9,0]\), \([0.72,-0.9]\), \([0.486,-0.810]\).

1
From the second line, \(\mathbf v_t=\mathbf w_t-\mathbf w_{t-1}\) — the velocity is the last move. Shift the index: \(\mathbf v_{t-1}=\mathbf w_{t-1}-\mathbf w_{t-2}\). Nothing has been assumed; this is just naming the same quantity twice.
2
Substitute into the first line: \[\mathbf w_t-\mathbf w_{t-1}=\beta(\mathbf w_{t-1}-\mathbf w_{t-2})-\alpha\nabla J(\mathbf w_{t-1}),\] which rearranges to the second form exactly. So "keep a velocity" and "add \(\beta\) times the last move" are the same instruction. Different books pick different halves; nothing changes.
3
Run it. \(\nabla J=(w_1,10w_2)\), start \([1,1]\), \(\mathbf v_0=\mathbf 0\): \[\begin{aligned}\mathbf v_1&=-0.1(1,10)=(-0.1,-1),\quad \mathbf w_1=[0.9,\ 0]\\[2pt] \mathbf v_2&=0.9(-0.1,-1)-0.1(0.9,0)=(-0.18,-0.9),\\ &\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \ \mathbf w_2=[0.72,\ -0.9]\\[2pt] \mathbf v_3&=0.9(-0.18,-0.9)-0.1(0.72,-9)=(-0.234,0.09),\\ &\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \ \mathbf w_3=[0.486,\ -0.810]\end{aligned}\] and \(J\) reads \(5.5\to0.405\to4.3092\to3.3986\). ∎ Step 2 is the overshoot in numbers: the fresh push on \(w_2\) is zero and the memory carries it to \(-0.9\). Step 3 is the return: the slope at \(w_2=-0.9\) is \(-9\), so the fresh push finally turns it around.
Prove it · AdaGrad's first step is exactly \(\alpha\), whatever the slope

Claim. With \(A_0=0\), the first AdaGrad step moves every coordinate by exactly \(\alpha\) in the direction \(-\mathrm{sign}(g_i)\).

1
After one update, \(A_i=0+g_i^2=g_i^2\), so \(\sqrt{A_i}=|g_i|\). The record contains exactly one entry: this step's own square. Its root is therefore the size of this step's own slope.
2
The step is then \[\frac{\alpha\,g_i}{\sqrt{A_i}}=\frac{\alpha\,g_i}{|g_i|}=\alpha\,\mathrm{sign}(g_i).\] ∎ The size of the slope cancels against itself. Only its sign reaches the update — the direction is data, the distance is a constant you chose.
3
Check on the session's valley: \(\nabla J([1,1])=(1,10)\), \(A=[1,100]\), \(\sqrt A=[1,10]\), step \(=0.1\cdot[1/1,\ 10/10]=[0.1,0.1]\), so \(\mathbf w^{(1)}=[0.9,0.9]\). The ten-to-one mismatch of §1 is gone on step one, with no standardisation and no knowledge of the landscape.
Prove it · why the record becomes a brake

Claim. On a constant slope \(g\), AdaGrad's step at time \(t\) is \(\alpha/\sqrt t\); the total distance it can ever cover in \(T\) steps grows only like \(2\alpha\sqrt T\), so reaching a target distance \(D\) costs about \((D/2\alpha)^2\) steps.

1
With \(g\) fixed, \(A_t=A_{t-1}+g^2\) and \(A_0=0\) give \(A_t=t\,g^2\), so \(\sqrt{A_t}=\sqrt t\,|g|\). A running total of identical squares is just \(t\) copies of one square.
2
The step size is therefore \[\frac{\alpha\,|g|}{\sqrt t\,|g|}=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt t}.\] The slope cancels at every step, not just the first. What remains depends only on how long you have been walking.
3
Add up the first \(T\) steps and compare with an integral: \[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt t}\ \approx\ \alpha\!\int_1^{T}\!\frac{\mathrm dt}{\sqrt t}=2\alpha\big(\sqrt T-1\big).\] Setting that equal to \(D\) gives \(T\approx(D/2\alpha+1)^2\). ∎ The distance still grows without limit — AdaGrad is not stuck in theory. But it grows like \(\sqrt T\), so doubling the distance costs four times the steps, and in practice you run out of patience long before you run out of hill.
Prove it · a fading average converges, a running total does not

Claim. On a constant slope \(g\), RMSProp's accumulator is \(A_t=g^2(1-\rho^{\,t})\to g^2\), so its step size tends to \(\alpha\) and never dies.

1
Unroll \(A_t=\rho A_{t-1}+(1-\rho)g^2\) from \(A_0=0\): \[A_t=(1-\rho)g^2\big(1+\rho+\rho^2+\cdots+\rho^{\,t-1}\big).\] Each old square is multiplied by \(\rho\) once per step, which is what "fades as \(\rho^{\,t}\)" means.
2
Sum the geometric series and simplify: \[A_t=(1-\rho)g^2\cdot\frac{1-\rho^{\,t}}{1-\rho}=g^2\big(1-\rho^{\,t}\big)\ \xrightarrow[\ t\to\infty\ ]{}\ g^2.\] The \((1-\rho)\) that scaled the new square is exactly cancelled by the \((1-\rho)\) in the denominator of the sum. The average converges to the thing being averaged — which is the whole point of an average.
3
So the step size is \(\alpha|g|/\big(|g|\sqrt{1-\rho^{\,t}}\big)=\alpha/\sqrt{1-\rho^{\,t}}\to\alpha\). Compare AdaGrad's \(\alpha/\sqrt t\to0\). ∎ Same slope, same \(\alpha\), two different fates: one method settles into a working stride, the other brakes itself to a stop. The only difference is whether the accumulator is a total or an average.
Prove it · the zero start inflates the first step by \(1/\sqrt{1-\rho}\)

Claim. RMSProp's first step is \(\alpha/\sqrt{1-\rho}\) — \(3.162\,\alpha\) at \(\rho=0.9\) and \(31.62\,\alpha\) at \(\rho=0.999\).

1
Put \(t=1\) in the formula above: \(A_1=g^2(1-\rho)\), so \(\sqrt{A_1}=|g|\sqrt{1-\rho}\). The average is holding only a \((1-\rho)\) share of the true size, because the other \(\rho\) share is still the zero it started from.
2
Hence \[\frac{\alpha|g|}{\sqrt{A_1}}=\frac{\alpha|g|}{|g|\sqrt{1-\rho}}=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}.\] At \(\rho=0.9\): \(\alpha/\sqrt{0.1}=3.1623\,\alpha\), which is the \(0.3162\) the widget prints at \(\alpha=0.1\). ∎ Dividing by a root that is too small makes the step too big, and the longer the memory the worse it is — the opposite of the intuition that more memory means more caution.
3
How long does it last? The inflation at step \(t\) is \(1/\sqrt{1-\rho^{\,t}}\), which is within \(1\,\%\) of \(1\) once \(\rho^{\,t}<0.02\), i.e. \(t>\ln(0.02)/\ln\rho\) — about \(37\) steps at \(\rho=0.9\), but about \(3\,900\) at \(\rho=0.999\). That is why the fix matters: at the popular \(\rho=0.999\) the "start-up transient" lasts thousands of steps.
Prove it · where Adam's probation rate \(\alpha_t\) comes from

Claim. Correcting both zero-started averages for how full they are turns the RMSProp step into \(\alpha_t\,F/\sqrt A\) with \(\alpha_t=\alpha\sqrt{1-\rho^{\,t}}\big/(1-\rho_f^{\,t})\) — the square root landing on the \(A\) correction only.

1
A zero-started fading average of a constant \(c\) reaches \(c(1-\rho_f^{\,t})\) after \(t\) steps (the same geometric sum as §4). So \(F\) is systematically short by exactly that factor, and the honest estimate is \[\widehat F=\frac{F}{1-\rho_f^{\,t}},\qquad \widehat A=\frac{A}{1-\rho^{\,t}}.\] This is a division by "how full the notebook is", nothing more. Each notebook fills at its own rate, so each gets its own correction.
2
Put the corrected pair into an RMSProp-shaped step and separate the constants from the raw averages: \[\alpha\,\frac{\widehat F}{\sqrt{\widehat A}}=\alpha\,\frac{F/(1-\rho_f^{\,t})}{\sqrt{A}\big/\sqrt{1-\rho^{\,t}}}=\underbrace{\alpha\,\frac{\sqrt{1-\rho^{\,t}}}{1-\rho_f^{\,t}}}_{\textstyle \alpha_t}\cdot\frac{F}{\sqrt A}.\] ∎ The whole correction collapses into one scalar multiplying the uncorrected ratio — which is why implementations can keep the raw \(F\) and \(A\) and just scale the stride. And the square root sits only on \(A\)'s correction because only \(A\) was under a root.
3
Limits and shape. As \(t\to\infty\) both \(\rho^{\,t}\) and \(\rho_f^{\,t}\) vanish, so \(\alpha_t\to\alpha\). With the defaults \(\rho_f=0.9,\rho=0.999\), \(\alpha_t/\alpha\) reads \(0.3162,\ 0.2353,\ 0.2020,\dots\), dips to \(0.15224\) at \(t=12\), then climbs back. Non-monotone, because the two notebooks fill at very different speeds — \(F\) is nearly full by step \(20\) while \(A\) needs thousands. The correction is not a schedule somebody tuned; it is bookkeeping that retires itself.

Claim. At \(t=1\), Adam moves every coordinate by exactly \(\alpha\) in the direction \(-\mathrm{sign}(g_i)\) — RMSProp's \(1/\sqrt{1-\rho}\) spike is cancelled precisely.

1
At \(t=1\) the two notebooks hold one reading each: \[\begin{aligned}F_i&=(1-\rho_f)g_i,\qquad A_i=(1-\rho)g_i^2,\\[2pt] \sqrt{A_i}&=\sqrt{1-\rho}\,|g_i|.\end{aligned}\] Both are short by their own factor — and those are exactly the factors \(\alpha_1\) is built from.
2
And \(\alpha_1=\alpha\sqrt{1-\rho}\big/(1-\rho_f)\). Multiply: \[\begin{aligned}\alpha_1\,\frac{F_i}{\sqrt{A_i}}&=\alpha\,\frac{\sqrt{1-\rho}}{1-\rho_f}\cdot\frac{(1-\rho_f)\,g_i}{\sqrt{1-\rho}\,|g_i|}\\[2pt] &=\alpha\,\frac{g_i}{|g_i|}=\alpha\,\mathrm{sign}(g_i).\end{aligned}\] ∎ Every factor cancels — \((1-\rho_f)\) against \((1-\rho_f)\), \(\sqrt{1-\rho}\) against \(\sqrt{1-\rho}\), \(|g_i|\) against \(g_i\). What survives is the stride you asked for and the direction the data gave.
3
On the valley: \(F=[0.1,1]\), \(A=[0.001,0.1]\), \(\sqrt A=[0.031623,0.31623]\), \(F/\sqrt A=[3.1623,3.1623]\), \(\alpha_1=0.031623\), step \(=[0.1,0.1]\). Compare RMSProp's \([0.3162,0.3162]\) at the same point. Same landscape, same \(\alpha\), one method three times too eager and the other exactly right — because of one scalar.
Prove it · the best shared stride, and the price of a bad condition number

Claim. On \(J=\tfrac12\sum\lambda_iw_i^2\) the stride that minimises the worst per-step factor is \(\eta^\star=\dfrac{2}{\lambda_{\min}+\lambda_{\max}}\), and at that stride every coordinate shrinks by at best \(\dfrac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\) per step, where \(\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\).

1
From the first drawer, coordinate \(i\) is multiplied by \(1-\eta\lambda_i\) each step, so after \(k\) steps the worst-behaved coordinate governs: \[\begin{aligned}r(\eta)&=\max_i\big|1-\eta\lambda_i\big|\\[2pt] &=\max\big\{|1-\eta\lambda_{\min}|,\ |1-\eta\lambda_{\max}|\big\}.\end{aligned}\] The maximum over an interval of \(\lambda\) is attained at an end, because \(|1-\eta\lambda|\) is a V in \(\lambda\).
2
As \(\eta\) grows, \(1-\eta\lambda_{\min}\) falls from \(1\) and \(|1-\eta\lambda_{\max}|\) eventually rises. The maximum of a falling and a rising curve is smallest where they cross: \[1-\eta\lambda_{\min}=-(1-\eta\lambda_{\max})\ \Longrightarrow\ \eta^\star=\frac{2}{\lambda_{\min}+\lambda_{\max}}.\] At the best stride the softest direction creeps forward and the stiffest one flips sign every step by the same fraction. Optimal does not mean pretty.
3
Substitute back: \[r(\eta^\star)=1-\frac{2\lambda_{\min}}{\lambda_{\min}+\lambda_{\max}}=\frac{\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}}{\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\min}}=\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}.\] ∎ At \(\kappa=1\) the rate is \(0\): one step and you are done. At \(\kappa=10\) it is \(9/11=0.818\) and you need \(\approx23\) steps per decimal place. At \(\kappa=1000\) it is \(0.998\) and you need \(\approx1150\). That curve is the whole reason the adaptive family exists.

Claim. \(\min x\) subject to \(x>2\) has infimum \(2\) and no minimiser; replacing \(>\) by \(\ge\) makes the answer exist, at \(x=2\).

1
Every feasible \(x\) satisfies \(x>2\), so \(2\) is a lower bound for the objective. A lower bound always exists here; the question is whether anything attains it.
2
Suppose some feasible \(x_0\) were the minimum. Then \(x_0>2\), so the midpoint \(x_1=\tfrac{x_0+2}{2}\) satisfies \(2The assumption defeats itself, and it does so for any candidate — there is no special point where the argument fails.

Prove it · nothing left to slide means the two gradients are parallel

Claim. At a minimum sitting on an active fence \(g=0\) (with \(\nabla g\ne\mathbf 0\)), the component of \(\nabla f\) along the fence must vanish, hence \(\nabla f(\mathbf x^\star)=-\mu\,\nabla g(\mathbf x^\star)\) for some \(\mu\ge0\).

1
Split \(\nabla f\) into the part along the fence and the part across it. Writing \(\mathbf n=\nabla g/\|\nabla g\|\) for the unit normal, \[\nabla f=\underbrace{(\nabla f^{\!\top}\mathbf n)\,\mathbf n}_{\text{across}}+\underbrace{\nabla f-(\nabla f^{\!\top}\mathbf n)\,\mathbf n}_{\textstyle \mathbf p\ =\ \text{along}}.\] A plain orthogonal decomposition. \(\mathbf p\) is the piece that lives in the fence's tangent direction.
2
Suppose \(\mathbf p\ne\mathbf 0\). Move a small \(\varepsilon\) along \(-\mathbf p\). To first order this keeps you on the fence (\(\nabla g^{\!\top}\mathbf p=0\), so \(g\) does not change) and changes the score by \[\nabla f^{\!\top}(-\varepsilon\mathbf p)=-\varepsilon\|\mathbf p\|^2<0.\] A legal move that strictly lowers the score. So the point was not a minimum — exactly the box that is still sliding.
3
Therefore \(\mathbf p=\mathbf 0\) at any minimum, so \(\nabla f\) is a multiple of \(\mathbf n\), i.e. of \(\nabla g\): write \(\nabla f=-\mu\nabla g\). Parallel, not equal — \(\mu\) absorbs the difference in length, which is typically large.
4
The sign. Moving into the feasible side means moving along \(-\nabla g\), and that must not lower \(f\): \[\begin{aligned}\nabla f^{\!\top}(-\nabla g)&=(-\mu\nabla g)^{\!\top}(-\nabla g)\\[2pt] &=\mu\|\nabla g\|^2\ \ge0\ \Longrightarrow\ \mu\ge0.\end{aligned}\] ∎ If \(\mu\) were negative you could step inwards, legally, and improve — so the fence was not holding you at all. That is the geometric twin of §9's guard argument.
Prove it · the infinite wall, rebuilt out of straight lines

Claim. \(\displaystyle\max_{\mu\ge0}\ \mu\,g=\begin{cases}0,&g\le0\\ +\infty,&g>0\end{cases}\) — the indicator of the fence, exactly.

1
If \(g\le0\): for every \(\mu\ge0\) the product \(\mu g\le0\), and the value \(0\) is achieved at \(\mu=0\). So the maximum is \(0\). On the allowed side the guard's best move is to charge nothing — any positive rate would reduce the bill the guard is trying to raise.
2
If \(g>0\): taking \(\mu=M\) gives \(Mg\to\infty\) as \(M\to\infty\). So the supremum is \(+\infty\). On the banned side the guard is unbounded, and one violated fence is enough to make the whole bill infinite.
3
Hence for every \(\mathbf x\), \[\begin{aligned}\max_{\boldsymbol\mu\ge0} L(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\mu)&=f(\mathbf x)+\sum_i\max_{\mu_i\ge0}\mu_ig_i(\mathbf x)\\[2pt] &=\begin{cases}f(\mathbf x),&\mathbf x\ \text{feasible}\\ +\infty,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\end{aligned}\] so \(\min_{\mathbf x}\max_{\boldsymbol\mu\ge0}L\) is the constrained problem. ∎ Nothing was relaxed or approximated. The Lagrangian with a maximising guard is the original problem written in a form we can differentiate.
Prove it · why a fence fine can never be negative

Claim. If some \(\mu_i<0\) were allowed, the minimiser of \(L\) would be pushed outside the feasible set on purpose, and the construction above would fail.

1
Take one fence and a point with \(g(\mathbf x)>0\) — illegal. The term contributed to the bill is \(\mu g\). This is the only place the rule enters the objective, so this term alone decides whether breaking the rule is punished or rewarded.
2
With \(\mu<0\) and \(g>0\), \(\mu g<0\): the bill falls. And it falls further the more the fence is broken, since \(\mu g\to-\infty\) as \(g\to\infty\). A subsidy, not a fine. The minimiser will chase it out of bounds and never come back.
3
Correspondingly, \(\max_{\mu\in\mathbb R}\mu g\) is \(+\infty\) for \(g\ne0\) and \(0\) only at \(g=0\) — the indicator of the equality \(g=0\), not of \(g\le0\). Restricting to \(\mu\ge0\) is exactly what turns it into the one-sided rule. ∎ So the sign rule is not a convention: it is the difference between "stay behind the fence" and "sit exactly on it". And it explains \(\lambda\) at once — for a genuine equality we want the two-sided version, so \(\lambda\) is left free.
Prove it · complementary slackness, in four lines, from the room left

Claim. Writing each fence as \(g_i(\mathbf x)+t_i^2=0\) and differentiating \(L\) with respect to \(t_i\) gives \(\mu_i\,g_i(\mathbf x^\star)=0\).

1
The substitution loses nothing: \(t_i^2=-g_i\), and a square is never negative, so \(-g_i\ge0\), which is \(g_i\le0\) — the original fence, recovered. The inequality has been absorbed into the algebra. Every rule in the problem is now an equation, apart from the sign rule on \(\mu_i\).
2
With the slacks in, \(L(\mathbf x,\lambda,\boldsymbol\mu,\mathbf t)=f+\lambda e+\sum_i\mu_i\big(g_i+t_i^2\big)\). The only term containing \(t_i\) is \(\mu_it_i^2\), so \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial t_i}=2\mu_it_i=0.\] A new unknown means a new equation — and this is the one that books usually state without ever deriving.
3
Multiply by \(t_i\) and halve: \(\mu_it_i^2=0\). Multiplying by \(t_i\) is legal even when \(t_i=0\); it simply keeps the identity true in both worlds.
4
Substitute \(t_i^2=-g_i\): \[\mu_i\big(-g_i(\mathbf x^\star)\big)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \mu_i\,g_i(\mathbf x^\star)=0.\] ∎ A product is zero only if a factor is, so per fence: either \(t_i\ne0\) (room left) forcing \(\mu_i=0\), or \(\mu_i\ne0\) forcing \(t_i=0\), i.e. the fence is touched. The name is literal — the rule came out of the slack.
Prove it · the five conditions are four derivatives and a sign

Claim. Setting every partial derivative of \(L(\mathbf x,\lambda,\boldsymbol\mu,\mathbf t)=f+\lambda e+\sum_i\mu_i(g_i+t_i^2)\) to zero yields conditions 1–4, and condition 5 is the sign rule of the previous drawer.

1
In \(\mathbf x\) (\(n\) equations). The slack terms carry no \(\mathbf x\), so they drop: \[\nabla f(\mathbf x^\star)+\lambda\nabla e(\mathbf x^\star)+\sum_i\mu_i\nabla g_i(\mathbf x^\star)=\mathbf 0.\] This is §8's two-arrows statement, written for many rules at once: \(\nabla f\) is a combination of the constraint gradients, so nothing of it is left running along the feasible directions.
2
In \(\lambda\). Only \(\lambda e(\mathbf x)\) contains \(\lambda\), and it is linear in it, so the derivative is \(e(\mathbf x^\star)=0\). Our own path constraint, handed back. A good sign: the machinery did not lose a rule.
3
In \(\mu_i\). Only \(\mu_i(g_i+t_i^2)\) contains \(\mu_i\), so \(g_i(\mathbf x^\star)+t_i^2=0\), i.e. \[g_i(\mathbf x^\star)=-t_i^2\le0.\] Our own fences, handed back — and with the inequality restored for free by the square.
4
In \(t_i\). The previous drawer: \(\mu_ig_i(\mathbf x^\star)=0\). And the sign: \(\mu_i\ge0\), which no derivative will give you — it comes from the guard argument. ∎ Four kinds of unknown, four conditions, plus one rule about what a fine is. Some books list four conditions by folding the sign rule into the setup; count five when you write an answer, so nothing can be said to be missing.
5
Counting: \(n\) variables, one \(\lambda\), \(m\) multipliers, \(m\) slacks — \(n+2m+1\) unknowns, matched by \(n+1+m+m\) equations plus \(m\) sign rules. And condition 4 splits into \(2^m\) branches, since each \(\mu_ig_i=0\) can be satisfied two ways. Books that skip slacks report \(n+m+1\) unknowns. Both counts are right; they describe different setups, and only the slack version hands you condition 4 for free.
Prove it · the chord test implies the tangent test

Claim. If \(f\big(\theta\mathbf x+(1-\theta)\mathbf y\big)\le\theta f(\mathbf x)+(1-\theta)f(\mathbf y)\) for all \(\theta\in[0,1]\), then \(f(\mathbf y)\ge f(\mathbf x)+\nabla f(\mathbf x)^{\!\top}(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)\) for all \(\mathbf x,\mathbf y\).

1
Rewrite the chord condition with the roles arranged around \(\mathbf x\). Put \(\mathbf z_\theta=\mathbf x+\theta(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)\); then \(\mathbf z_\theta=(1-\theta)\mathbf x+\theta\mathbf y\), so \[f(\mathbf z_\theta)\le(1-\theta)f(\mathbf x)+\theta f(\mathbf y).\] Same statement, parametrised by how far along the segment you have walked.
2
Subtract \(f(\mathbf x)\) from both sides and divide by \(\theta>0\): \[\frac{f\big(\mathbf x+\theta(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)\big)-f(\mathbf x)}{\theta}\ \le\ f(\mathbf y)-f(\mathbf x).\] The left side is a difference quotient along the direction \(\mathbf y-\mathbf x\). The right side does not contain \(\theta\) at all — so it is an upper bound that survives any limit.
3
Let \(\theta\to0^+\). The left side is the directional derivative \(\nabla f(\mathbf x)^{\!\top}(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)\), giving \[\nabla f(\mathbf x)^{\!\top}(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)\le f(\mathbf y)-f(\mathbf x).\] ∎ Read it as a promise: the tangent plane at \(\mathbf x\) lies below the whole surface. So if \(\nabla f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf 0\) then \(f(\mathbf y)\ge f(\mathbf x)\) everywhere — one local measurement certifies a global minimum. That is the entire practical value of convexity.
Prove it · the duals of a linear program and a quadratic program

Claim. For \(\min\mathbf c^{\!\top}\mathbf x\) s.t. \(A\mathbf x\le\mathbf b\) the dual is \(\max-\boldsymbol\lambda^{\!\top}\mathbf b\) s.t. \(\mathbf c+A^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf 0,\ \boldsymbol\lambda\ge\mathbf 0\); for \(\min\tfrac12\mathbf x^{\!\top}Q\mathbf x+\mathbf c^{\!\top}\mathbf x\) s.t. \(A\mathbf x\le\mathbf b\) with \(Q\) invertible it is \(\max-\tfrac12(\mathbf c+A^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\lambda)^{\!\top}Q^{-1}(\mathbf c+A^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\lambda)-\boldsymbol\lambda^{\!\top}\mathbf b\) s.t. \(\boldsymbol\lambda\ge\mathbf 0\).

1
Linear. Write the Lagrangian and collect the \(\mathbf x\) terms: \[L=\mathbf c^{\!\top}\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\lambda^{\!\top}(A\mathbf x-\mathbf b)=(\mathbf c+A^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\lambda)^{\!\top}\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\lambda^{\!\top}\mathbf b.\] It is linear in \(\mathbf x\), which makes the inner minimisation trivial and brutal.
2
Minimising a linear function over all of \(\mathbb R^d\) gives \(-\infty\) unless its coefficient vector is zero. So \[d(\boldsymbol\lambda)=\begin{cases}-\boldsymbol\lambda^{\!\top}\mathbf b,&\mathbf c+A^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf 0\\ -\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\] Maximising over \(\boldsymbol\lambda\ge\mathbf 0\) therefore forces the equation as a constraint of the dual. The \(-\infty\) region is not a nuisance; it is where the dual's own constraints come from. A dual variable that sends the inner problem to \(-\infty\) is a price nobody would ever quote.
3
Note the shape swap: the primal has \(d\) variables and \(m\) rules; the dual has \(m\) variables and \(d\) rules. Solve whichever is smaller. This is reason one from §13, in its purest form.
4
Quadratic. Now \(L=\tfrac12\mathbf x^{\!\top}Q\mathbf x+(\mathbf c+A^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\lambda)^{\!\top}\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\lambda^{\!\top}\mathbf b\); setting \(\nabla_{\mathbf x}L=\mathbf 0\) gives \(Q\mathbf x+(\mathbf c+A^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\lambda)=\mathbf 0\), so \(\mathbf x=-Q^{-1}(\mathbf c+A^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\lambda)\). A bowl in \(\mathbf x\) has a genuine minimum, so nothing runs off to \(-\infty\) and there is no hidden constraint this time.
5
Substituting back and writing \(\mathbf u=\mathbf c+A^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\lambda\): \[\begin{aligned}d(\boldsymbol\lambda)&=\tfrac12\mathbf u^{\!\top}Q^{-1}\mathbf u-\mathbf u^{\!\top}Q^{-1}\mathbf u-\boldsymbol\lambda^{\!\top}\mathbf b\\ &=-\tfrac12\mathbf u^{\!\top}Q^{-1}\mathbf u-\boldsymbol\lambda^{\!\top}\mathbf b.\end{aligned}\] ∎ A concave quadratic in \(\boldsymbol\lambda\) with only the sign rules left — and ridge, lasso and the support vector machine are all of exactly this shape. The last one is worked in §14.
Prove it · moving second is never worse (the minimax inequality)

Claim. For any function \(\varphi\) of two arguments, \(\displaystyle\max_y\min_x\varphi(x,y)\le\min_x\max_y\varphi(x,y)\).

1
Name the two optima: let the left side be attained at \((x_A,y_A)\) and the right side at \((x_B,y_B)\), so LHS \(=\varphi(x_A,y_A)\) and RHS \(=\varphi(x_B,y_B)\). Two numbers, four points' worth of information. The whole proof is comparing them through a shared middle term.
2
Fix \(y=y_A\). The left side's inner operation is a minimum over \(x\), and it returned \(x_A\), so it is no larger than the value at any other \(x\) — in particular \(x_B\): \[\varphi(x_A,y_A)\le\varphi(x_B,y_A).\] A minimum is \(\le\) any particular value.
3
Fix \(x=x_B\). The right side's inner operation is a maximum over \(y\), and it returned \(y_B\), so it is no smaller than the value at any other \(y\) — in particular \(y_A\): \[\varphi(x_B,y_B)\ge\varphi(x_B,y_A).\] A maximum is \(\ge\) any particular value. The middle term \(\varphi(x_B,y_A)\) is the same in both lines — that is the whole trick.
4
Chain them: \(\varphi(x_A,y_A)\le\varphi(x_B,y_A)\le\varphi(x_B,y_B)\). ∎ With \(\varphi=L\), \(x=\mathbf x\), \(y=\boldsymbol\mu\): \(d^\star\le p^\star\). No convexity, no differentiability, no assumptions at all — which is why weak duality is free.
5
The one-line version, worth memorising. For any feasible \(\mathbf x\) and any \(\boldsymbol\mu\ge\mathbf 0\), \[\begin{aligned}d(\boldsymbol\mu)&=\min_{\mathbf z}L(\mathbf z,\boldsymbol\mu)\le L(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\mu)\\[2pt] &=f(\mathbf x)+\boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\mathbf g(\mathbf x)\ \le\ f(\mathbf x),\end{aligned}\] because \(\boldsymbol\mu\ge\mathbf 0\) and \(\mathbf g(\mathbf x)\le\mathbf 0\). Take the best on each side. Every dual value you ever compute is a certified lower bound on the primal answer — even if you never solve the primal.
Prove it · the dual function is always concave

Claim. \(d(\boldsymbol\mu)=\min_{\mathbf x}\big[f(\mathbf x)+\boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\mathbf g(\mathbf x)\big]\) is concave in \(\boldsymbol\mu\), whatever \(f\) and \(\mathbf g\) are.

1
Fix any single \(\mathbf x\). As a function of \(\boldsymbol\mu\), \(L(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\mu)=f(\mathbf x)+\boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\mathbf g(\mathbf x)\) is affine: a constant height \(f(\mathbf x)\) plus a constant slope \(\mathbf g(\mathbf x)\). All the nastiness of \(f\) and \(\mathbf g\) has been frozen into two numbers. This is the step that does the work.
2
\(d\) is the pointwise minimum of that whole family of affine functions, one per \(\mathbf x\). Take any \(\boldsymbol\mu_1,\boldsymbol\mu_2\) and \(\theta\in[0,1]\), and let \(\mathbf x_\theta\) attain the minimum at \(\theta\boldsymbol\mu_1+(1-\theta)\boldsymbol\mu_2\). Then \[\begin{aligned}d\big(\theta\boldsymbol\mu_1+(1-\theta)\boldsymbol\mu_2\big)&=L\big(\mathbf x_\theta,\ \theta\boldsymbol\mu_1+(1-\theta)\boldsymbol\mu_2\big)\\ &=\theta L(\mathbf x_\theta,\boldsymbol\mu_1)+(1-\theta)L(\mathbf x_\theta,\boldsymbol\mu_2)\\ &\ge\theta\,d(\boldsymbol\mu_1)+(1-\theta)\,d(\boldsymbol\mu_2),\end{aligned}\] the middle line because \(L\) is affine in \(\boldsymbol\mu\), the last because \(L(\mathbf x_\theta,\cdot)\ge d(\cdot)\) by definition of a minimum. ∎ That is exactly the chord test with the inequality reversed: concave. The picture in the widget is this proof — forty straight lines, and the thing underneath them all bends downward.
3
Say it precisely: the dual function is concave; the dual problem, "maximise a concave function over \(\boldsymbol\mu\ge\mathbf 0\)", is a convex optimization problem. Both statements are true and they are about different objects. "The dual is convex" is loose talk that costs marks. Be specific about which object you mean.
Prove it · the multiplier is the slope of the value function

Claim. Let \(p^\star(b)=\min f(\mathbf x)\) subject to \(g(\mathbf x)\le b\). Then \(\mathrm dp^\star/\mathrm db=-\mu^\star\) — relaxing the fence by \(\varepsilon\) improves the best score by about \(\mu^\star\varepsilon\).

1
Write the Lagrangian with the level \(b\) in it: \(L=f(\mathbf x)+\mu\big(g(\mathbf x)-b\big)\). At the optimum, strong duality gives \(p^\star(b)=L(\mathbf x^\star(b),\mu^\star(b))\). The value function is the Lagrangian evaluated along the optimal pair — which is what lets us differentiate it.
2
Differentiate in \(b\). The terms coming from \(\mathbf x^\star\) and \(\mu^\star\) moving are multiplied by \(\partial L/\partial\mathbf x=\mathbf 0\) (stationarity) and \(\partial L/\partial\mu=g-b=0\) (the fence is active), so both vanish. Only the explicit \(b\) survives: \[\frac{\mathrm dp^\star}{\mathrm db}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}=-\mu^\star.\] ∎ This is the envelope argument: at an optimum, first-order changes in the decisions cost nothing, so only the direct effect of the parameter is left.
3
Check it on \(\min x^2+y^2\) s.t. \(x+y=4,\ x\ge c\). For \(c\ge2\) the answer is \((c,4-c)\), so \(p^\star(c)=c^2+(4-c)^2\) and \(\mathrm dp^\star/\mathrm dc=4c-8\), which is \(4\) at \(c=3\) — exactly \(\mu^\star\). Predicting \(c=2.9\): \(10-4(0.1)=9.60\); the truth is \(8.41+1.21=9.62\). The \(0.02\) is the curvature of the value function; the multiplier is a derivative, so it is exact only in the limit. And at \(c\le2\) the fence stops binding, \(\mu^\star=0\), and \(p^\star\) goes flat at \(8\) — a wall you are not touching is worth nothing.
Prove it · the widest margin, solved through its dual

Claim. For \(x_1=+1\) (class \(+1\)) and \(x_2=-1\) (class \(-1\)), minimising \(\tfrac12w^2\) subject to \(y_i(wx_i+b)\ge1\) has dual \(q(\alpha)=2\alpha-2\alpha^2\) with \(\alpha^\star=\tfrac12\), giving \(w^\star=1\), \(b^\star=0\), boundary \(x=0\) and margin width \(2\); and a third point at \(x_3=3\) gets \(\alpha_3=0\).

1
Standard form. For \(i=1\): \((+1)(w\cdot1+b)\ge1\), so \(g_1=1-w-b\le0\). For \(i=2\): \((-1)(w\cdot(-1)+b)\ge1\), i.e. \(w-b\ge1\), so \(g_2=1-w+b\le0\). Then \[\begin{aligned}L(w,b,\alpha_1,\alpha_2)&=\tfrac12w^2+\alpha_1(1-w-b)\\ &\quad+\alpha_2(1-w+b),\qquad \alpha_i\ge0.\end{aligned}\] Two fences, so \(m=2\); the multipliers are traditionally called \(\alpha\) here rather than \(\mu\).
2
Stationarity in the primal variables: \[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}&=w-\alpha_1-\alpha_2=0\ \Rightarrow\ w=\alpha_1+\alpha_2,\\[3pt] \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}&=-\alpha_1+\alpha_2=0\ \Rightarrow\ \alpha_1=\alpha_2.\end{aligned}\] These are the general identities \(w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i\) and \(\sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0\), written out for this data. The second is what makes \(b\) drop out of the dual entirely.
3
Substitute. Group \(L=\tfrac12w^2-w(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)-b(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)+(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)\); with \(\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha\) the \(b\) term vanishes and \(w=2\alpha\): \[\begin{aligned}q(\alpha)&=\tfrac12(2\alpha)^2-(2\alpha)(2\alpha)+2\alpha\\[2pt] &=2\alpha^2-4\alpha^2+2\alpha=2\alpha-2\alpha^2.\end{aligned}\] Two primal variables gone, one dual variable left, and the result is a downward parabola — concave, as §13 promised.
4
Maximise over \(\alpha\ge0\): \(q'(\alpha)=2-4\alpha=0\Rightarrow\alpha^\star=\tfrac12\ge0\) ✓, and \(d^\star=q(\tfrac12)=1-\tfrac12=\tfrac12\). Check the sign rule before reporting — a peak with \(\alpha<0\) would be out of bounds and the best allowed value would sit at \(\alpha=0\).
5
Recover the classifier. \(w^\star=\alpha_1+\alpha_2=1\). Since \(\alpha_1=\tfrac12>0\), complementary slackness forces \(g_1=0\), i.e. \(w+b=1\), so \(b^\star=0\). Primal value \(\tfrac12(1)^2=\tfrac12=d^\star\): zero gap. Boundary \(x=0\), margin lines \(x=\pm1\), width \(2/|w^\star|=2\), both points tight and therefore support vectors. Note how \(b\) was recovered — not from stationarity (which only gave \(\alpha_1=\alpha_2\)) but from a tight constraint identified by a positive multiplier. That is the standard trick.
6
Add \(x_3=3\), class \(+1\). Its constraint is \(3w+b\ge1\), which at \((1,0)\) reads \(3\ge1\): slack, so \(g_3<0\) and complementary slackness forces \(\alpha_3=0\). The point contributes nothing to \(w=\sum\alpha_iy_ix_i\) and can be deleted. If instead \(x_3<1\), it and the negative point become the binding pair, giving \(w=\dfrac{2}{x_3+1}\) and \(b=\dfrac{1-x_3}{1+x_3}\) — at \(x_3=0.5\), \(w=1.3333\), \(b=0.3333\), boundary \(-0.25\), margin \(1.5\). ∎ A million training points, a handful of nonzero multipliers, and the rest deletable: the sparsity of a support vector machine is complementary slackness and nothing else.
