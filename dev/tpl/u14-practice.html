15

Practice arena — fourteen problems, solved in full

Fourteen problems, easy to hard: a loaded die, two rounds of Bayes, a bell read with z-scores, a covariance matrix taken apart, a Cholesky factor, a coin and a bell fitted by maximum likelihood, a line fitted through its likelihood, softmax at two temperatures, the sigmoid, entropy, cross-entropy and both KLs, one training step of a classifier, and a question about which loss belongs to which noise. Every number was checked by machine.

Three habits do most of the work. Count before you divide: for Bayes, imagine 10 000 people and count them. Take the log early: products of chances become sums you can differentiate. And check that probabilities add to 1 — it catches most slips.

Problem 1easypmf · mean · variance

A loaded die shows 6 with probability \(\tfrac12\) and each of the faces 1–5 with probability \(\tfrac1{10}\). (a) Check that it is a valid pmf. (b) Find \(\mathbb E[X]\). (c) Find \(\operatorname{Var}[X]\) and \(\sigma\). (d) Compare with a fair die.

What this tests. The balance point and the spread computed from a table. Plan. Add the weights; weighted average of \(x\); then the shortcut \(\operatorname{Var}=\mathbb E[X^2]-\mu^2\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — valid (a). \(5\times\tfrac1{10}+\tfrac12=1\) ✓, and every weight is between 0 and 1.
Step 2 — the mean (b). \(\mathbb E[X]=\tfrac{1+2+3+4+5}{10}+6\cdot\tfrac12=1.5+3=4.5\).
Step 3 — the variance (c). \(\mathbb E[X^2]=\tfrac{1+4+9+16+25}{10}+36\cdot\tfrac12=5.5+18=23.5\), so \(\operatorname{Var}=23.5-4.5^2=23.5-20.25=3.25\) and \(\sigma=\sqrt{3.25}\approx1.803\).
Step 4 — compare (d). A fair die has mean 3.5 and variance \(\tfrac{35}{12}\approx2.917\). The loading moves the balance point up by 1 and makes the outcomes a little more spread out.

answers at a glance: (a) weights add to 1 ✓; (b) \(\mathbb E[X]=4.5\); (c) \(\operatorname{Var}=3.25\), \(\sigma\approx1.803\); (d) fair die: 3.5 and 2.917.

Problem 2easyBayes · two tests

A disease affects 2% of people. A test catches 90% of the sick and gives a false alarm to 10% of the healthy. (a) What fraction of all people test positive? (b) You test positive. What is the chance you are sick? (c) A second, independent test is also positive. Now what? (d) If instead the first test had been negative, what is the chance you are sick?

What this tests. Bayes' rule with natural counts, and chaining an update. Plan. Imagine 10 000 people and count. For (c), yesterday's posterior is today's prior.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — count (a). Of 10 000 people, 200 are sick and 180 of them test positive; 9 800 are healthy and 980 of them get a false alarm. Positives: \(180+980=1160\), which is \(11.6\%\).
Step 2 — one positive (b). \(P(\text{sick}\mid+)=\tfrac{180}{1160}\approx0.155\). Only about 1 in 6.4 positives is sick: the false alarms on the big healthy crowd swamp the real cases.
Step 3 — a second positive (c). New prior \(0.155\): \[\begin{gathered}\frac{0.9\times0.155}{0.9\times0.155+0.1\times0.845}\\ \approx0.623.\end{gathered}\] In counts: of the 1160 positives, 180 sick → 162 positive again; 980 healthy → 98 positive again; \(\tfrac{162}{260}\approx0.623\) ✓.
Step 4 — a negative (d). Negatives: 20 sick (missed) and 8 820 healthy. \(P(\text{sick}\mid-)=\tfrac{20}{8840}\approx0.0023\) — a negative is very reassuring.

answers at a glance: (a) 11.6% test positive; (b) \(\approx0.155\); (c) \(\approx0.623\); (d) \(\approx0.0023\).

Remember

A positive test on a rare condition is weak evidence on its own; two independent positives are strong. And the same test can be far more convincing when it says "no" than when it says "yes".

Problem 3easythe bell · z-scores

Marks in a subject follow \(\mathcal N(60,10^2)\). (a) Roughly what share scored between 50 and 70? Between 40 and 80? (b) Roughly what share scored above 80? (c) A student scored 75. What is her z-score? (d) Another subject has \(\mu=70\), \(\sigma=5\) and her friend scored 80 there. Who did better relative to their class?

What this tests. The 68–95–99.7 rule and comparing across different bells with z-scores. Plan. Turn every mark into "how many \(\sigma\)'s from the middle".

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the rule (a). 50 to 70 is \(\pm1\sigma\): about 68% (68.3%). 40 to 80 is \(\pm2\sigma\): about 95% (95.4%).
Step 2 — the tail (b). Outside \(\pm2\sigma\) is about 4.6%, split equally between the two tails by symmetry: about 2.3% scored above 80.
Step 3 — z (c). \(z=\tfrac{75-60}{10}=1.5\).
Step 4 — compare (d). Her friend: \(z=\tfrac{80-70}{5}=2\). The friend is 2 spreads above the class middle, she is 1.5, so the friend did better relative to their class — even though 80 − 70 = 10 is less than 75 − 60 = 15.

answers at a glance: (a) ≈ 68% and ≈ 95%; (b) ≈ 2.3%; (c) \(z=1.5\); (d) the friend (\(z=2\) against \(1.5\)).

Problem 4medium2-D Gaussian · Mahalanobis

A 2-D Gaussian has \(\boldsymbol\mu=\mathbf 0\) and \(\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}5&2\\2&2\end{bmatrix}\). (a) Find the eigenvalues and unit eigenvectors of \(\Sigma\). (b) Describe the contour ellipse. (c) Find \(\det\Sigma\) and the peak density. (d) Find the Mahalanobis distance of \((2,1)\) and of \((1,-2)\) from the mean, and compare with their ordinary distances.

What this tests. Reading a Gaussian's shape from \(\Sigma\) (Unit 4 meets §5). Plan. Trace and determinant give the eigenvalues; eigenvectors give the axes; \(\Sigma^{-1}\) gives the ruler.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — eigenvalues (a). Trace \(7\), determinant \(10-4=6\). So \(\lambda^2-7\lambda+6=0\), \(\lambda=6\) or \(1\).
Step 2 — eigenvectors (a). For \(\lambda=6\): \((5-6)v_1+2v_2=0\Rightarrow v=(2,1)/\sqrt5\). For \(\lambda=1\): \(4v_1+2v_2=0\Rightarrow v=(1,-2)/\sqrt5\). They are at right angles, as the spectral theorem promises.
Step 3 — the ellipse (b). The long axis points along \((2,1)\), the short axis along \((1,-2)\). Half-lengths are in the ratio \(\sqrt6:1\approx2.45:1\).
Step 4 — the peak (c). \(\det\Sigma=6\), so \(p_{\max}=\dfrac1{2\pi\sqrt6}\approx0.0650\).
Step 5 — the ruler (d). \(\Sigma^{-1}=\tfrac16\begin{bmatrix}2&-2\\-2&5\end{bmatrix}\). \[\begin{aligned}(2,1):\ d^2&=\tfrac{8-8+5}{6}=\tfrac56,\\ d&\approx0.913;\\ (1,-2):\ d^2&=\tfrac{2+8+20}{6}=5,\\ d&\approx2.236.\end{aligned}\] Both points have ordinary distance \(\sqrt5\approx2.236\). The first lies on the long axis (\(d^2=5/6\), i.e. \(\|\mathbf x\|^2/\lambda_1\)); the second on the short axis (\(d^2=5/1\)).

answers at a glance: (a) \(\lambda=6\) along \((2,1)/\sqrt5\), \(\lambda=1\) along \((1,-2)/\sqrt5\); (b) ellipse tilted along \((2,1)\), axes in ratio \(\sqrt6:1\); (c) \(\det=6\), peak \(\approx0.0650\); (d) Mahalanobis \(0.913\) and \(2.236\), ordinary distance \(2.236\) for both.

Remember

Along an eigenvector, the Mahalanobis distance is the ordinary distance divided by \(\sqrt\lambda\): long axes forgive, short axes do not.

Problem 5mediumsampling · Cholesky

You need samples from \(\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma)\) with \(\boldsymbol\mu=(1,1)\) and \(\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}9&6\\6&5\end{bmatrix}\). (a) Find the Cholesky factor \(L\). (b) Check \(LL^{\!\top}=\Sigma\). (c) The computer gives the round sample \(\mathbf z=(1,-1)\). What sample \(\mathbf x\) do you produce? (d) What is the correlation between \(x_1\) and \(x_2\)?

What this tests. The stretch recipe \(\mathbf x=\boldsymbol\mu+L\mathbf z\). Plan. Fill \(L\) entry by entry, top-left first.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — L (a). \(\ell_{11}=\sqrt9=3\); \(\ell_{21}=6/3=2\); \(\ell_{22}=\sqrt{5-2^2}=1\). So \(L=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\2&1\end{bmatrix}\).
Step 2 — check (b). \(LL^{\!\top}=\begin{bmatrix}9&6\\6&4+1\end{bmatrix}=\Sigma\) ✓.
Step 3 — one sample (c). \(L\mathbf z=(3\cdot1,\;2\cdot1+1\cdot(-1))=(3,1)\), so \(\mathbf x=(1,1)+(3,1)=(4,2)\).
Step 4 — correlation (d). \(\rho=\dfrac{6}{\sqrt9\,\sqrt5}=\dfrac{6}{3\sqrt5}=\dfrac2{\sqrt5}\approx0.894\): strongly tied together, a narrow tilted cloud.

answers at a glance: (a) \(L=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\2&1\end{bmatrix}\); (b) ✓; (c) \(\mathbf x=(4,2)\); (d) \(\rho\approx0.894\).

Problem 6easylikelihood · coin

A coin shows 3 heads in 12 tosses. (a) Write \(L(p)\) and \(\log L(p)\). (b) Find \(\hat p\). (c) Compute \(\log L\) at \(\hat p\) and at \(p=0.5\) (natural log). (d) How many times better does \(\hat p\) explain the data than a fair coin?

What this tests. Maximum likelihood and comparing models by their likelihood. Plan. Differentiate the log; compare in logs and exponentiate the difference.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the functions (a). \(L(p)=p^3(1-p)^9\) and \(\log L=3\log p+9\log(1-p)\).
Step 2 — the peak (b). \(\tfrac3p-\tfrac9{1-p}=0\Rightarrow3(1-p)=9p\Rightarrow\hat p=\tfrac3{12}=0.25\).
Step 3 — two log-likelihoods (c). \(\log L(0.25)=3\ln0.25+9\ln0.75\approx-4.159-2.589=-6.748\). \(\log L(0.5)=12\ln0.5\approx-8.318\).
Step 4 — the ratio (d). \(e^{-6.748-(-8.318)}=e^{1.570}\approx4.81\). The best coin explains the tosses about 4.8 times better than a fair one.

answers at a glance: (a) \(p^3(1-p)^9\); (b) \(\hat p=0.25\); (c) \(-6.748\) and \(-8.318\); (d) about 4.81 times.

Problem 7easyGaussian MLE · N vs N − 1

Measurements: 1, 3, 4, 6, 6. (a) Find the maximum-likelihood \(\hat\mu\) and \(\hat\sigma^2\). (b) Find the "divide by \(N-1\)" variance. (c) Which one is too small on average, and why?

What this tests. The Gaussian MLE formulas and the reason for the \(N-1\) correction. Plan. Average; squared distances from the average; divide two ways.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the middle (a). \(\hat\mu=\tfrac{1+3+4+6+6}{5}=\tfrac{20}5=4\).
Step 2 — the spread (a). Squared distances: \(9,1,0,4,4\), total 18. \(\hat\sigma^2=\tfrac{18}{5}=3.6\), \(\hat\sigma\approx1.897\).
Step 3 — N − 1 (b). \(\tfrac{18}{4}=4.5\).
Step 4 — which is biased (c). The MLE, 3.6. It measures the spread around \(\hat\mu=4\), which was chosen to be as close to these very points as possible, so the points look slightly tighter than they would around the true middle. Dividing by \(N-1\) undoes that on average.

answers at a glance: (a) \(\hat\mu=4\), \(\hat\sigma^2=3.6\); (b) \(4.5\); (c) the MLE is slightly too small on average.

Problem 8mediumleast squares via likelihood

Three points: \((1,2)\), \((2,3)\), \((3,5)\). Assume \(y=wx+b+\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\). (a) Why does maximising the likelihood mean minimising \(\sum(y_i-\hat y_i)^2\)? (b) Find \(w\) and \(b\). (c) Find the squared error. (d) The maximum-likelihood noise variance is \(\hat\sigma^2=\tfrac1N\sum(y_i-\hat y_i)^2\). Compute it.

What this tests. The chain "bell noise → −log L → squared error → the least-squares formulas". Plan. Write \(-\log L\); use the centred slope formula.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — why (a). \(-\log L=\tfrac1{2\sigma^2}\sum(y_i-\hat y_i)^2+N\log(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})\). For any fixed \(\sigma\) the last term is fixed and \(\tfrac1{2\sigma^2}>0\), so the line that makes \(-\log L\) smallest is the one with the smallest squared error.
Step 2 — the line (b). \(\bar x=2\), \(\bar y=\tfrac{10}3\). Deviations: \(x\): \(-1,0,1\); \(y\): \(-\tfrac43,-\tfrac13,\tfrac53\). \[\begin{aligned}w&=\frac{\tfrac43+0+\tfrac53}{1+0+1}=\frac32,\\ b&=\frac{10}3-\frac32\cdot2=\frac13.\end{aligned}\]
Step 3 — the error (c). Predictions \(\tfrac{11}6,\tfrac{10}3,\tfrac{29}6\); errors \(\tfrac16,-\tfrac13,\tfrac16\); squared error \(\tfrac1{36}+\tfrac4{36}+\tfrac1{36}=\tfrac16\approx0.167\).
Step 4 — the noise (d). \(\hat\sigma^2=\tfrac{1/6}{3}=\tfrac1{18}\approx0.0556\).

answers at a glance: (a) the other term of \(-\log L\) does not depend on the line; (b) \(w=1.5\), \(b=\tfrac13\); (c) \(\tfrac16\approx0.167\); (d) \(\hat\sigma^2=\tfrac1{18}\approx0.0556\).

Problem 9easysoftmax · temperature

Scores \((3,1,1)\). (a) Compute the softmax at \(T=1\). (b) At \(T=2\). (c) What happens as \(T\to0\) and as \(T\to\infty\)? (d) What does softmax give for \((103,101,101)\)?

What this tests. The recipe, the dial and shift invariance. Plan. Exponentiate, add, divide — after dividing the scores by \(T\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — T = 1 (a). \(e^3=20.086\), \(e^1=2.718\), \(e^1=2.718\); total \(25.522\). Probabilities \((0.787,\,0.107,\,0.107)\).
Step 2 — T = 2 (b). Scores become \((1.5,0.5,0.5)\): \(4.482,\,1.649,\,1.649\), total \(7.779\). Probabilities \((0.576,\,0.212,\,0.212)\) — more relaxed.
Step 3 — the ends (c). \(T\to0\): all the probability goes to the first class, \((1,0,0)\). \(T\to\infty\): \((\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13)\). The two tied classes stay tied at every temperature.
Step 4 — shift (d). Adding 100 to every score changes nothing: \((0.787,\,0.107,\,0.107)\). Computing \(e^{103}\) directly would be foolish; subtract the max first.

answers at a glance: (a) \((0.787,0.107,0.107)\); (b) \((0.576,0.212,0.212)\); (c) \((1,0,0)\) and \((\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13)\); (d) same as (a).

Problem 10easytwo classes · sigmoid

A two-class model gives scores \(z_1=2.5\), \(z_2=1\). (a) Compute \(q_1\) with softmax. (b) Compute \(\sigma(z_1-z_2)\) and compare. (c) An SVM gives a new point \(f(\mathbf x)=-1.5\). What probability of class \(+1\) does \(\sigma(f)\) suggest? (d) What does a point on the SVM's boundary get?

What this tests. That two-class softmax is the sigmoid of the score difference. Plan. Compute both ways; then read an SVM score as a difference.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — softmax (a). \(e^{2.5}=12.182\), \(e^{1}=2.718\): \(q_1=\tfrac{12.182}{14.901}\approx0.818\).
Step 2 — sigmoid (b). \(\sigma(1.5)=\tfrac1{1+e^{-1.5}}=\tfrac1{1+0.223}\approx0.818\). The same number.
Step 3 — the SVM score (c). \(\sigma(-1.5)\approx0.182\): about an 18% chance of class \(+1\), 82% of class \(-1\).
Step 4 — the boundary (d). \(\sigma(0)=0.5\): exactly undecided.

answers at a glance: (a) and (b) \(0.818\); (c) \(0.182\); (d) \(0.5\).

Remember

In practice the SVM score is first scaled and shifted (\(\sigma(af+b)\), fitted on held-out data) before it is trusted as a probability — but the idea is exactly this.

Problem 11easysurprise · entropy

A four-outcome distribution is \((\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\tfrac18)\). (a) Find the surprise of each outcome in bits. (b) Find the entropy in bits and in nats. (c) Compare with four equally likely outcomes.

What this tests. Surprise, average surprise, and the units. Plan. \(-\log_2\) of each chance; weight by the chance; multiply by \(\ln2\) for nats.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — surprises (a). \(1,\,2,\,3,\,3\) bits.
Step 2 — entropy (b). \(\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot2+\tfrac18\cdot3+\tfrac18\cdot3=0.5+0.5+0.375+0.375=1.75\) bits \(=1.75\ln2\approx1.213\) nats.
Step 3 — compare (c). Four equal outcomes: 2 bits. The lopsided one is more predictable, so it carries less uncertainty. A neat reading: ask "is it the first?" (yes half the time), then "the second?", then "the third?" — on average 1.75 questions.

answers at a glance: (a) 1, 2, 3, 3 bits; (b) 1.75 bits ≈ 1.213 nats; (c) less than the 2 bits of the uniform.

Problem 12mediumcross-entropy · both KLs

The truth is \(p=(0.25,0.75)\) and a model believes \(q=(0.5,0.5)\). In bits: (a) find \(H(p)\); (b) find \(H(p,q)\); (c) find \(\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)\) two ways; (d) find \(\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)\).

What this tests. The identity \(H(p,q)=H(p)+\mathrm{KL}\) and the lopsidedness of KL. Plan. Compute each sum directly, then check the identity.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — entropy (a). \(H(p)=0.25\cdot2+0.75\log_2\tfrac43=0.5+0.311=0.811\).
Step 2 — cross-entropy (b). \(H(p,q)=-(0.25+0.75)\log_20.5=1\).
Step 3 — KL two ways (c). Directly: \(0.25\log_2\tfrac{0.25}{0.5}+0.75\log_2\tfrac{0.75}{0.5}=-0.25+0.439=0.189\). From the identity: \(1-0.811=0.189\) ✓.
Step 4 — the other direction (d). \(0.5\log_2\tfrac{0.5}{0.25}+0.5\log_2\tfrac{0.5}{0.75}=0.5-0.292=0.208\). Not the same as 0.189.

answers at a glance: (a) 0.811; (b) 1; (c) 0.189; (d) 0.208 bits.

Problem 13hardcross-entropy loss · q − y

A three-class model gives scores \(\mathbf z=(0,1,3)\). The true class is the second. (a) Compute \(\mathbf q\). (b) Compute the loss in nats. (c) Compute the gradient with respect to \(\mathbf z\), and say what one step of size 1 does to each score. (d) Take that step and recompute the loss.

What this tests. The loss of a confidently wrong model, and one step of the cleanest gradient in the course. Plan. Softmax; \(-\ln q_2\); \(\mathbf q-\mathbf y\); \(\mathbf z\leftarrow\mathbf z-\eta(\mathbf q-\mathbf y)\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the belief (a). \(e^0=1\), \(e^1=2.718\), \(e^3=20.086\); total \(23.804\). \(\mathbf q=(0.042,\,0.114,\,0.844)\). The model is fairly sure of the third class.
Step 2 — the loss (b). \(-\ln0.114\approx2.170\) nats. Large: the truth got only 11% belief.
Step 3 — the gradient (c). \(\mathbf y=(0,1,0)\): \[\begin{gathered}\mathbf q-\mathbf y\\ =(0.042,\;-0.886,\;0.844).\end{gathered}\] A step downhill adds \(0.886\) to the true score, takes \(0.844\) off the third (the class that stole the most), and \(0.042\) off the first.
Step 4 — after the step (d). \(\mathbf z=(-0.042,\,1.886,\,2.156)\), so \(\mathbf q=(0.059,\,0.407,\,0.534)\) and the loss falls to \(-\ln0.407\approx0.899\).

answers at a glance: (a) \((0.042,0.114,0.844)\); (b) \(2.170\) nats; (c) \((0.042,-0.886,0.844)\); (d) loss \(\approx0.899\).

Remember

The gradient's entries always add to zero, and each wrong class is pushed down by exactly the probability it wrongly held. That is the whole training signal of a classifier.

Problem 14mediumwhich loss from which noise

Five recorded auto fares for the same short ride, in tens of rupees: 1, 2, 3, 4 and — one mistyped — 100. You model them as one true fare \(c\) plus noise. (a) With bell-shaped noise, which loss do you minimise, and what is \(\hat c\)? (b) With Laplace noise? (c) Which estimate would you trust, and why?

What this tests. That a loss is a noise belief, with a definite numerical answer for each. Plan. Bell → squared error → mean; Laplace → absolute error → median.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — bell noise (a). \(-\log L\) is the squared error \(\sum(y_i-c)^2\), smallest at the mean: \(\hat c=\tfrac{1+2+3+4+100}{5}=22\).
Step 2 — Laplace noise (b). \(-\log L\) is the absolute error \(\sum|y_i-c|\). Its slope is (number of points below \(c\)) − (number above), which is zero at the median: \(\hat c=3\).
Step 3 — judge (c). The median, 3: four of the five fares agree with it. The mean, 22, is nowhere near any real fare — one wild value dragged it, because a bell says such a value is practically impossible and bends everything to explain it.

answers at a glance: (a) squared error, \(\hat c=22\) (the mean); (b) absolute error, \(\hat c=3\) (the median); (c) trust the median.

Remember

Choosing a loss is choosing a noise model. If wild values can happen, say so with a heavy-tailed noise — and the loss will follow.
