You shall know a word by the company it keeps

Imagine this

You hear a word you do not know: "kadak". Then you hear it used: "a cup of kadak chai", "I like my chai kadak", "kadak and sweet, please". You still have no dictionary. But you already know it has something to do with tea.

You learned the word from its neighbours. A linguist named J. R. Firth put it in one line in 1957: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps."

Let us turn that idea into numbers. Pick some target words (chai, coffee, cricket, football) and some neighbour words (drink, hot, play, match). Read a corpus. Each time a neighbour word appears within 2 words of a target word, add 1 to their box. This window of "2 words either side" is called the window size.

Here is the table we get from a small made-up corpus (the widget below shows the sentences):

drinkhotplaymatch
chai5401
coffee4500
cricket0054
football0145

This is a co-occurrence matrix: "how often do these two words occur together". Now read each row as a vector. Chai is \((5,4,0,1)\). Coffee is \((4,5,0,0)\). Cricket is \((0,0,5,4)\). These are our first word vectors, and they are no longer one-hot: chai and coffee share neighbours, so their rows look alike.

How alike? Use the angle between the rows, from Unit 3. The cosine of that angle is

\[\cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf b\rVert}.\]

For chai and coffee: the dot product is \(5\cdot4+4\cdot5+0+0=40\). The lengths are \(\sqrt{42}\) and \(\sqrt{41}\). So

\[\cos(\text{chai},\text{coffee})=\frac{40}{\sqrt{42}\,\sqrt{41}}\approx0.964.\]

For chai and cricket the dot product is only \(1\cdot4=4\), so the cosine is \(4/(\sqrt{42}\sqrt{41})\approx0.096\). And cricket with football gives \(40/(\sqrt{41}\sqrt{42})\approx0.964\) again. The two tea words point almost the same way. The two sport words point almost the same way. The two groups are nearly at right angles.

Why the cosine and not the plain dot product? A word that appears ten times as often has counts ten times as big — a longer arrow — but the same kind of company. The cosine ignores the length and keeps only the direction: which company a word keeps, not how often it talks.

Squeeze it with the SVD

Imagine this

A school gives a long survey with 100 questions. When the teacher reads the answers, she notices that most of them can be summed up in two scores: "loves sport" and "loves music". Two numbers per student say almost everything the 100 answers say.

A co-occurrence table is like that survey. It is huge, but most of what it says fits in a few numbers per word.

A real table has one row for every word and one column for every neighbour word: 50 000 × 50 000, and mostly zeros. Rows that long are clumsy. We want short rows that keep the pattern and drop the noise.

The tool is the SVD from Unit 5. It writes any matrix as \(C=U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}\): a turn, a stretch by the singular values \(\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots\), and another turn. The big singular values are the strong patterns. The small ones are mostly noise. Keep only the top \(k\) and you get the best rank-\(k\) copy of the table (Unit 5's layer cake).

For our 4 × 4 table the singular values are

\[\sigma\approx(9.528,\ 8.528,\ 1.143,\ 1.087).\]

Two big, two small. How much do the top two keep? Measure a table's "energy" as the sum of the squares of all its entries — here \(42+41+41+42=166\). That is also \(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2+\sigma_3^2+\sigma_4^2\). The top two give \(9.528^2+8.528^2\approx163.5\), which is

\[\frac{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2+\dots+\sigma_4^2}\approx\frac{163.5}{166}\approx98.5\%\]

of the energy. If we rebuild the table from just those two, the error (the Frobenius size of what is left over) is \(\sqrt{\sigma_3^2+\sigma_4^2}\approx1.578\) — small next to entries of 4 and 5.

Each word now gets just two numbers: its row of \(U_2\Sigma_2\).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{chai}&\approx(5.009,\,-4.032)\\ \text{coffee}&\approx(4.530,\,-4.452)\\ \text{cricket}&\approx(4.450,\,4.540)\\ \text{football}&\approx(5.037,\,4.004)\end{aligned}\]

(The SVD is free to flip the sign of any direction, so another program may print some of these with the opposite sign. The angles do not change.) Read them: the first number is about 5 for every word — it says "this word is used a lot". The second number is the interesting one: negative for tea, positive for sport. In two dimensions, \(\cos(\text{chai},\text{coffee})\approx0.995\) and \(\cos(\text{chai},\text{cricket})\approx0.097\). The squeeze made the tea words even more alike — it threw away the noise that kept them apart.

One more fix: common words shout. In real text the word "the" sits near everything. Its column is huge, so raw counts mostly measure "how common is this word". The fix is to ask a better question: does this pair appear together more often than chance would give? If chai and "hot" together are 3 times more common than you would expect from how common each one is alone, that is interesting; if "the" and chai are just as common together as chance predicts, that is nothing. Take the log of that ratio and keep only positive values — people call it PPMI (positive pointwise mutual information). The formula is in the drawer below. Try the switch in the next widget and watch the clusters snap apart.

The realization

\[\begin{gathered}C\approx U_k\Sigma_kV_k^{\mathsf T},\\ \text{word vectors}=\text{rows of }U_k\Sigma_k\end{gathered}\]

The SVD finds the few strong directions hiding in a big table of counts. Keep those, drop the rest, and each word gets a short list of numbers. The energy kept, \(\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i^2/\sum_i\sigma_i^2\), tells you how much of the table survived. Weighting the counts by PPMI first stops common words from hogging the first direction.

In one sentence: The SVD squeezes a co-occurrence table to its top \(k\) directions; the rows of \(U_k\Sigma_k\) are short word vectors, the energy kept is \(\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i^2/\sum\sigma_i^2\) (98.5% for our table at \(k=2\)), and PPMI stops common words from taking over.

6

Learning vectors instead of counting: word2vec

Imagine this

Two friends play a game on a long train ride. One reads a word from a newspaper — "chai" — and then a second word. Sometimes the second word really was next to "chai" in the paper ("hot"). Sometimes it is a random word picked from anywhere ("football"). The other friend must say: real neighbour, or random?

To win this game again and again, you have to learn what chai is like. That is the whole idea of word2vec.

Counting and squeezing works, but the table is enormous and the SVD of a 50 000 × 50 000 table is slow. In 2013, Tomas Mikolov and his team at Google tried another way: skip the table, and learn the short lists directly by playing the guessing game above. Their method is called word2vec, and the game version is called skip-gram with negative sampling.

Each word gets two lists of numbers: \(\mathbf v\) for when it is the middle word, and \(\mathbf u\) for when it is a neighbour. The game's score for a pair is the dot product \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v\), squeezed into a probability with the sigmoid from Unit 14:

\[P(\text{real neighbour})=\sigma(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}}.\]

A big positive dot product means "surely real". A big negative one means "surely random". For each real pair we also pick a few random words — the negatives — and want the model to say "random" to them.

A worked round. Use lists of just two numbers. Middle word chai: \(\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}=(1,0)\). A real neighbour, hot: \(\mathbf u_{\text{hot}}=(0.5,0.5)\). A random negative, football: \(\mathbf u_{\text{football}}=(-0.5,1)\).

  • Real pair: \(\mathbf u_{\text{hot}}\cdot\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}=0.5\), so \(\sigma(0.5)\approx0.622\). The model is 62% sure hot is real. Good, but not great.
  • Negative pair: \(\mathbf u_{\text{football}}\cdot\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}=-0.5\). The chance it says "random" is \(\sigma(+0.5)\approx0.622\).
  • The loss is the surprise of both right answers (in nats, as in Unit 14): \(-\ln0.622-\ln0.622\approx0.948\).

Now take one step downhill (Unit 9) with step size \(\eta=1\). Each guess is "wrong by" \(1-0.622\approx0.378\). The update rules turn out to be very simple (the drawer has the algebra):

  • Pull the real neighbour toward chai: \(\mathbf u_{\text{hot}}\leftarrow\mathbf u_{\text{hot}}+0.378\,\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}=(0.878,\ 0.5)\).
  • Push the random word away from chai: \(\mathbf u_{\text{football}}\leftarrow\mathbf u_{\text{football}}-0.378\,\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}=(-0.878,\ 1)\).
  • Turn chai toward hot and away from football: \(\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}\leftarrow\mathbf v_{\text{chai}}+0.378\,(\mathbf u_{\text{hot}}-\mathbf u_{\text{football}})=(1.378,\ -0.189)\).

After this one step, the real pair scores \(\sigma(1.114)\approx0.753\), the negative pair gives "random" with \(\sigma(1.398)\approx0.802\), and the loss has dropped from 0.948 to about 0.505.

word2vec is just logistic regression (Unit 14) on pairs of words. Every step pulls real neighbours together and pushes random words apart. Two words that keep the same company get pulled toward the same neighbours, so they end up pointing the same way — without anyone ever counting a table.
