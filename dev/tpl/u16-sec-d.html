What embeddings get wrong

Imagine this

"He hit a six with his bat." "A bat flew out of the old fort at dusk." Same word, two completely different things.

Or: "We sat on the river bank." "I opened an account at the bank." You never mix them up, because the sentence tells you which one is meant.

Everything so far gives each word one vector, whatever the sentence. So what does "bat" get? The training game pulls it toward cricket words every time it appears in a cricket sentence, and toward animal words every time it appears in an animal sentence. It ends up roughly a blend of the two meanings, weighted by how often each one is used.

A tiny example. Let the cricket meaning point along \((1,0)\) and the animal meaning along \((0,1)\). If 80% of the sentences with "bat" are about cricket, the single vector is about

\[0.8\,(1,0)+0.2\,(0,1)=(0.8,\ 0.2).\]

Its cosine with the cricket meaning is \(0.8/\sqrt{0.68}\approx0.970\), and with the animal meaning only \(0.2/\sqrt{0.68}\approx0.243\). The rare meaning is almost drowned. And a word used half and half sits in the middle, close to neither group.

Vectors copy the text they learn from. The lists are a mirror of how words are used in the corpus — nothing more. If the training text more often writes "the doctor … he" and "the nurse … she", then "doctor" leans toward the male end of the female–male direction, and "nurse" toward the female end. That is a fact about the text, not about doctors or nurses. It matters because a system built on these vectors will repeat the pattern. People measure such leanings with exactly the directions you toggled in §9, and can remove a direction when it should not matter.

Both problems point to the same fix: a word's vector should depend on the sentence it sits in. That is where Units 17 and 18 go.

The realization

\[\begin{aligned}\mathbf v_{\text{bat}}\approx\ &s\,\mathbf v_{\text{cricket meaning}}\\ +\ &(1-s)\,\mathbf v_{\text{animal meaning}}\end{aligned}\]

One vector per word must blend all its meanings, weighted by how often each is used — so rare meanings fade and two-faced words sit between groups. And the vectors faithfully copy the patterns of their text, including the ones we would rather they did not. The cure is a vector that changes with its sentence.

Measuring similarity well

Imagine this

Two customers at a tea stall. One buys 2 cups of chai and 1 biscuit. The other, buying for his family, takes 4 cups of chai and 2 biscuits. Their taste is identical — twice as much chai as biscuits. Only the size of the order differs.

A good "similar taste" measure should say they are the same.

We have met three ways to compare two lists. Take the orders \(\mathbf a=(2,1)\) and \(\mathbf b=(4,2)\), and a third customer who likes biscuits more, \(\mathbf c=(1,2)\).

  • Dot product: \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=10\), \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c=4\). It mixes taste with size: bigger orders give bigger numbers.
  • Distance (straight-line, Euclidean): \(\lVert\mathbf a-\mathbf b\rVert=\sqrt{2^2+1^2}\approx2.236\), but \(\lVert\mathbf a-\mathbf c\rVert=\sqrt{1^2+1^2}\approx1.414\). Distance says \(\mathbf a\) is closer to \(\mathbf c\) — the size difference beats the taste. (With an office order of 20 and 10 it would be worse: about 20.12 against 1.414.)
  • Cosine: \(\cos(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)=1\) exactly (same direction), \(\cos(\mathbf a,\mathbf c)=4/5=0.8\). Cosine sees only taste.

For word vectors, the length mostly tracks how often a word appears, and the direction tracks what it means. That is why cosine is the standard choice.

The peace treaty: normalise. Divide every vector by its length so it has length 1 (it sits on the unit circle). Then the dot product is the cosine, and the distance follows from it:

\[\lVert\hat{\mathbf a}-\hat{\mathbf b}\rVert^2=2-2\cos\theta.\]

Bigger cosine means smaller distance, so all three measures agree on who is nearest. Real systems normalise once and then use fast dot products.

Three rulers for "similar"Drag the tips of the two arrows. The dot product, the cosine (with its angle) and the distance update together.

Try: Drag \(\mathbf b\) along the same line as \(\mathbf a\), far out: the cosine stays 1 while the distance grows. Swing it to a right angle: cosine 0. Then press normalise: both arrows snap to length 1, and the distance becomes \(\sqrt{2-2\cos\theta}\).

The realization

\[\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf b\rVert\cos\theta\]

The dot product is length × length × cosine. For meaning we want only the cosine, so we either divide by the lengths or normalise every vector once. After normalising, dot product, cosine and distance all rank neighbours the same way.

What to carry forward

The whole unit fits on one card. Here it is, piece by piece.

One-hot is blind

Every pair of one-hot words has dot product 0 and distance \(\sqrt2\). Names, no meaning.

Count the next word

\(P(w'\mid w)=\text{count}(w\,w')/\text{count}(w)\). Add-one smoothing: \((c+1)/(n+V)\).

Perplexity

\(2^{\text{average surprise}}\): the number of equally likely words the model is as unsure as. 1 is perfect, \(V\) is clueless.

Company

A word's row of neighbour counts is a vector. Similar meaning = cosine near 1.

Squeeze

Keep the top \(k\) singular values: rows of \(U_k\Sigma_k\). Energy kept \(\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i^2/\sum\sigma_i^2\). PPMI tames common words.

Learn: word2vec

Score \(\sigma(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)\). Pull real neighbours in, push \(k\) random words out.

Cheap questions

A full softmax costs \(V\) dot products per pair; negative sampling costs \(k+1\).

Neural LM

Look up rows (one-hot × table), glue, hidden layer, softmax. The table is the first layer.

Meaning is a direction

\(A-B+C\), then nearest by cosine, skipping \(A,B,C\). One vector per word blends its meanings and copies its text.

The realization

\[\text{king}-\text{man}+\text{woman}\approx\text{queen}\]

A word becomes a point in space, placed by the company it keeps — counted and squeezed, or learned by a guessing game. In that space, closeness is similarity and a difference of meaning is a direction. Every language model you will meet from here on starts by turning its words into such vectors.

In one sentence: Give each word a short list of numbers learned from the company it keeps — by counting and the SVD, or by word2vec's guessing game — and meaning becomes geometry: similar words point the same way and differences of meaning are directions.
