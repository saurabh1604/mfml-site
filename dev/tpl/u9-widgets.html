The loss \(L(a,b)=\sum_i\big(y_i-(ax_i+b)\big)^2\) is a bowl, but a lopsided one: its Hessian has one stiff eigenvalue (\(\approx 66.8\)) and one flat (\(\approx 1.2\)). Gradient descent must keep \(\gamma<2/66.8\approx0.030\) to survive the stiff direction, and at that speed it barely moves along the flat one — the trench you see the ball crawl along.

For \(f(\mathbf x)=\tfrac12\mathbf x^{\top}A\mathbf x+\mathbf b^{\top}\mathbf x\) the gradient is \(A\mathbf x+\mathbf b\) and the minimiser is \(\mathbf x^*=-A^{-1}\mathbf b\). Along each eigen-direction of \(A\) a fixed step multiplies the error by \(1-\gamma\lambda\): the stiff direction (big \(\lambda\)) forces \(\gamma\) small, and then the flat direction barely moves. Exact line search picks the \(\gamma\) that minimises \(f\) along the ray — which is exactly why consecutive steps are perpendicular.

On \(f=\tfrac12\lambda x^2\) the update is \(x_{k+1}=x_k-\gamma\lambda x_k=(1-\gamma\lambda)\,x_k\): a pure geometric sequence. The factor must sit strictly inside \((-1,1)\) — that is the rule \(\gamma<2/\lambda\). In two dimensions each eigen-direction has its own factor, and the stiffest one (the largest \(\lambda\)) sets the speed limit for all.

Along the ray \(\mathbf x_k-\gamma\mathbf g\) the bowl becomes a one-variable function \(h(\gamma)=f(\mathbf x_k-\gamma\mathbf g)\). Its derivative is \(h'(\gamma)=-\mathbf g^{\top}\nabla f(\text{bead})\), so \(h'(\gamma^*)=0\) says precisely that the new gradient has no component along the old direction — the two are perpendicular. For a quadratic, \(\gamma^*=\mathbf g^{\top}\mathbf g/\mathbf g^{\top}A\mathbf g\) in closed form.

—

Bracketing methods assume \(h\) is unimodal on \([a,b]\) — one dip, nothing else — and then squeeze: binary search halves the width each time (two evaluations per step), golden section shrinks it by \(0.618\) with only one new evaluation. Armijo asks a humbler question: is \(h(\alpha)\le h(0)+c\,\alpha\,h'(0)\)? If not, halve \(\alpha\) and ask again.

Exponential decay \(\alpha_0e^{-kt}\) shrinks geometrically, inverse decay \(\alpha_0/(1+kt)\) only harmonically, step decay \(\alpha_0/3^{\lfloor t/5\rfloor}\) drops in cliffs. The bold driver ignores the clock: after every step it looks at the loss — improved? grow \(\alpha\) by 5 %; worse? undo the step and halve \(\alpha\).

The full gradient averages a residual term over all \(N=40\) points; a minibatch averages the same term over \(|S|\) of them. The estimate is unbiased — on average it is the true gradient — with variance shrinking like \(1/|S|\). Each step costs \(|S|\) residuals instead of \(N\), so a batch of 4 buys ten steps for the price of one exact one.
