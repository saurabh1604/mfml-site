17

Practice arena — sixteen problems, solved in full

Sixteen problems, easy to hard: a margin read off a given line, a two-point street from scratch, five points on a line, the kernel identity checked by hand, counting hidden features, rebuilding a model from its prices, adding a point in one dimension, verifying a claimed answer in two, the "on the edge but no price" trap, reading a soft-margin model, auditing a proposed soft-margin answer — and then five full kernel problems, including one whose boundary is a hyperbola and one where a point's price is zero. Every number here was checked by machine.

Three habits do most of the work. Write the two identities first: \(\sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0\) and \(\mathbf w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\mathbf x_i\) (or the kernel vote \(f(\mathbf x)=\sum_i\alpha_iy_iK(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x)+b\)). Dots first, kernel second: finish every dot product before you square it. And find \(b\) from a support vector, then check it with a second one — never assume \(b=0\).

Problem 1easygeometry

A classifier has \(\mathbf w=(3,4)\) and \(b=-10\), in canonical form (the nearest points score exactly \(\pm1\)). (a) Write the boundary and both street edges. (b) What is the width of the street? (c) How far is the point \((1,1)\) from the boundary, and on which side? (d) You rescale to \(\mathbf w=(6,8)\), \(b=-20\). What changes and what does not?

What this tests. That geometry lives in ratios like \(|f|/\|\mathbf w\|\), and that "width \(=2/\|\mathbf w\|\)" is only true in canonical form. Plan. \(\|\mathbf w\|\) first; then the three parallel lines; then the distance formula; for (d), list what moves and what does not.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the length of w. \(\|\mathbf w\|=\sqrt{9+16}=5\) (the 3-4-5 triangle).
Step 2 — the three lines (a). Set \(f=3x_1+4x_2-10\) to \(0\) and \(\pm1\): \[\begin{aligned}\text{boundary: }&3x_1+4x_2=10,\\ f=+1:\ &3x_1+4x_2=11,\\ f=-1:\ &3x_1+4x_2=9.\end{aligned}\]
Step 3 — the width (b). \(2/\|\mathbf w\|=2/5=0.4\). Check: each edge is \(1/\|\mathbf w\|=0.2\) from the boundary, and \(0.2+0.2=0.4\).
Step 4 — the distance of (1, 1) (c). \[\begin{gathered}f(1,1)=3+4-10=-3,\\ \frac{|f|}{\|\mathbf w\|}=\frac35=0.6.\end{gathered}\] It is on the negative side. Since \(|f|=3>1\), it is outside the street; as a training point it would have \(\alpha=0\).
Step 5 — the rescaling (d).

Remember

A score is not a distance until you divide by \(\|\mathbf w\|\), and \(2/\|\mathbf w\|\) is the width only when the nearest points score exactly 1. Renormalise before you quote a margin.

Problem 2easyhard margin

Two training points: \(\mathbf x_1=(1,1)\) with \(y_1=+1\), \(\mathbf x_2=(4,5)\) with \(y_2=-1\). (a) Find \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2\). (b) Find \(\mathbf w\) and \(b\). (c) Give the boundary, both edges and the width. (d) Check that the primal and dual values agree.

What this tests. The whole machinery on the smallest problem there is: balance, the edge conditions, \(\mathbf w=\sum\alpha_iy_i\mathbf x_i\) — then two independent checks. Plan. With two points both must be support vectors. Balance makes the prices equal; the two edge conditions give two equations in \(\alpha\) and \(b\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — both points must push. If \(\alpha_1=0\), balance \(\alpha_1-\alpha_2=0\) forces \(\alpha_2=0\), then \(\mathbf w=\mathbf 0\), which cannot give any point a score of 1. So both prices are positive and both points sit on an edge. Balance: \(\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\alpha\).
Step 2 — the dot products. \(\mathbf x_1\cdot\mathbf x_1=2\), \(\mathbf x_1\cdot\mathbf x_2=4+5=9\), \(\mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf x_2=16+25=41\).
Step 3 — the two edge conditions. With \(f(\mathbf x)=\alpha(\mathbf x_1\cdot\mathbf x)-\alpha(\mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf x)+b\): \[\begin{aligned}&(+1)\big[2\alpha-9\alpha+b\big]=1\\ &\qquad\Rightarrow\ -7\alpha+b=1,\\ &(-1)\big[9\alpha-41\alpha+b\big]=1\\ &\qquad\Rightarrow\ 32\alpha-b=1.\end{aligned}\]
Step 4 — solve (a). Add the two equations; \(b\) cancels: \(25\alpha=2\), so \(\alpha=\tfrac{2}{25}=0.08\). Check with the two-point shortcut: \(2/\|\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_2\|^2=2/(9+16)=0.08\) ✓.
Step 5 — w and b (b). \(b=1+7(0.08)=1.56\), and \[\begin{aligned}\mathbf w&=0.08\big[(1,1)-(4,5)\big]\\ &=0.08(-3,-4)\\ &=(-0.24,\,-0.32).\end{aligned}\] \(\mathbf w\) points along \(\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_2\): the boundary is at right angles to the segment joining the two points.
Step 6 — the lines and the width (c). Multiply \(-0.24x_1-0.32x_2+1.56=0\) by \(-25\): the boundary is \(6x_1+8x_2=39\), and the edges \(f=\pm1\) are \(6x_1+8x_2=39\mp25\), i.e. \(14\) and \(64\). Check: \((1,1)\) gives \(14\) ✓, \((4,5)\) gives \(64\) ✓. \(\|\mathbf w\|=0.08\cdot5=0.4\), so the width is \(2/0.4=5\) — the distance between the two points, \(\sqrt{9+16}=5\) ✓.
Step 7 — strong duality (d). Primal: \(\tfrac12\|\mathbf w\|^2=\tfrac12(0.16)=0.08\). Dual: \(\sum\alpha_i-\tfrac12\|\mathbf w\|^2=0.16-0.08=0.08\). Equal ✓.

answers at a glance: \(\alpha_1=\alpha_2=0.08\); \(\mathbf w=(-0.24,-0.32)\), \(b=1.56\); boundary \(6x_1+8x_2=39\), edges \(=14\) and \(=64\), width \(5\); primal \(=\) dual \(=0.08\).

Remember

With exactly two points, \(\alpha=2/\|\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_2\|^2\), \(\mathbf w\) is parallel to \(\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_2\), and the width is the distance between them. Use it as a five-second check.

Problem 3easyhard margin · 1-D

One-dimensional data: \(x=1,2,4\) have \(y=+1\); \(x=-1,-3\) have \(y=-1\). (a) Which points are support vectors? (b) Find \(w\), \(b\) and the width. (c) Find every \(\alpha_i\). (d) Which points could be deleted without changing the answer?

What this tests. Guessing the active points from a sketch, then proving the guess; and complementary slackness in its most useful form. Plan. In 1-D the street is an interval, so the classes touch at the closest opposite pair. Solve their two edge equations, then check every other point.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the collision. The classes come closest at \(x=1\) and \(x=-1\). These are the candidates.
Step 2 — two edge equations. \((+1)(w+b)=1\) and \((-1)(-w+b)=1\), i.e. \(w+b=1\) and \(w-b=1\). Adding: \(w=1\); subtracting: \(b=0\). So \(f(x)=x\), boundary \(x=0\), width \(2/|w|=2\): the street runs from \(-1\) to \(1\).

Problem 4easykernel

\(\mathbf x=(2,1)\), \(\mathbf z=(1,3)\), \(K(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)=(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf z+1)^2\). (a) Evaluate the kernel. (b) Write the six-feature map \(\varphi\), compute \(\varphi(\mathbf x)\) and \(\varphi(\mathbf z)\), and confirm \(\varphi(\mathbf x)\cdot\varphi(\mathbf z)\) matches. Problem 5easykernel theory

Input dimension \(n=3\), polynomial degree \(d=4\). (a) How many features does \((\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf z+1)^4\) secretly use? (b) And \((\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf z)^4\)? (c) With 500 training points, how many kernel evaluations does the kernel table need, and how does that compare with building the lift?

What this tests. The two counting formulas — and that they describe two different models, not two ways of counting one. Plan. With the \(+1\): every degree up to \(d\), \(\binom{n+d}{d}\). Without: degree exactly \(d\), \(\binom{n+d-1}{d}\). Then count the entries of a symmetric \(500\times500\) table.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — non-homogeneous (a). \(\binom{3+4}{4}=\binom74=\dfrac{7\cdot6\cdot5}{3\cdot2\cdot1}=35\). Check degree by degree in 3 variables: \(1+3+6+10+15=35\) ✓.
Step 2 — homogeneous (b). \(\binom{3+4-1}{4}=\binom64=\dfrac{6\cdot5}{2\cdot1}=15\). This is a genuinely different model: it has only the 15 quartic products — no constant, no linear, no quadratic, no cubic terms.
Step 3 — the table (c). A symmetric \(500\times500\) table needs \(\dfrac{500\cdot501}{2}=125\,250\) distinct kernel values, each a 3-term dot product, one addition and one fourth power — about six operations.
Step 4 — against the lift. The explicit lift stores \(500\times35=17\,500\) numbers and takes 35-term dot products. At \(n=3,d=4\) the trick barely pays. At \(n=100,d=5\) the lift has \(\binom{105}{5}=96\,560\,646\) coordinates per point, and the kernel cost does not move. Past some size the kernel is not a speed-up; it is the only thing that runs at all.

answers at a glance: (a) \(\binom74=35\). (b) \(\binom64=15\). (c) \(125\,250\) evaluations at about six operations each, against \(17\,500\) stored coordinates and 35-term dot products for the lift — a gap that explodes with \(n\) and \(d\).

Problem 6mediumhard margin

A trained hard-margin SVM reports: \((2,2)\), \(y=+1\), \(\alpha=0.25\); \((4,1)\), \(y=+1\), \(\alpha=0\); \((0,0)\), \(y=-1\), \(\alpha=0.25\). (a) Recover \(\mathbf w\) and \(b\). (b) Give the boundary and the width. (c) Verify that \((4,1)\) deserves \(\alpha=0\). (d) Classify \((1,0)\).

What this tests. Using \(\mathbf w=\sum\alpha_iy_i\mathbf x_i\) the way you do in practice, recovering \(b\) (which the dual never hands you), and knowing that \(\alpha=0\) is a claim you can check. Plan. \(\mathbf w\) straight from the sum; balance as a free error check; \(b\) from one support vector, confirmed by the other.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — w (a). \[\begin{aligned}\mathbf w&=0.25(+1)(2,2)+0\cdot(4,1)\\ &\quad+0.25(-1)(0,0)\\ &=(0.5,\ 0.5).\end{aligned}\] Balance: \(0.25+0-0.25=0\) ✓.
Step 2 — b. From \((0,0)\): \(b=y_s-\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x_s=-1-0=-1\). Confirm from \((2,2)\): \(b=1-(0.5,0.5)\cdot(2,2)=1-2=-1\) ✓. Two support vectors must agree; if they do not, the prices are wrong.
Step 3 — boundary and width (b). \(0.5x_1+0.5x_2-1=0\iff x_1+x_2=2\). \(\|\mathbf w\|=1/\sqrt2\), so the width is \(2\sqrt2\approx2.83\).
Step 4 — does (4, 1) deserve α = 0? (c) \(f(4,1)=2+0.5-1=1.5\), score \(1.5>1\): strictly outside the street, so complementary slackness forces \(\alpha=0\) ✓.
Step 5 — classify (1, 0) (d). \(f(1,0)=0.5-1=-0.5<0\): class \(-1\). But \(|f|<1\), so the point is inside the street — a correct but unsure call. Its distance to the boundary is \(0.5\sqrt2\approx0.71\), less than the half-width \(\sqrt2\approx1.41\).

answers at a glance: \(\mathbf w=(0.5,0.5)\), \(b=-1\); boundary \(x_1+x_2=2\), width \(2\sqrt2\); \((4,1)\) scores \(1.5>1\), so \(\alpha=0\) is right; \((1,0)\to-1\) with \(f=-0.5\), inside the street.

Problem 7mediumhard margin · KKT

A hard-margin SVM, \(\min\tfrac12w^2\) s.t. \(y_i(wx_i+b)\ge1\), is trained on \(x_1=4,y_1=+1\); \(x_2=7,y_2=+1\); \(x_3=1,y_3=-1\); \(x_4=-3,y_4=-1\). (a) Write the KKT conditions, find the active points and solve for \(w,b\) and all \(\alpha_i\). (b) Give the width and check strong duality. (c) A fifth point \(x_5=3\), \(y_5=+1\), is added. Is the old answer still optimal? If not, find the new one.

What this tests. Writing all four KKT conditions, using a sketch to spot the active points, and reasoning about a new point without starting over. Plan. Draw the number line: the closest opposite pair is \(1\) and \(4\). For (c), test the new point against the old \(f\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — KKT. With \(L=\tfrac12w^2-\sum_i\alpha_i[y_i(wx_i+b)-1]\): stationarity \(w=\sum\alpha_iy_ix_i\) and \(\sum\alpha_iy_i=0\); primal feasibility \(y_i(wx_i+b)\ge1\); dual feasibility \(\alpha_i\ge0\); complementary slackness \(\alpha_i[y_i(wx_i+b)-1]=0\).
Step 2 — the active pair. \((+1)(4w+b)=1\) and \((-1)(w+b)=1\). Subtract: \(3w=2\), so \(w=\tfrac23\) and \(b=-1-\tfrac23=-\tfrac53\). Boundary: \(f=0\) at \(x=2.5\).
Step 3 — check the rest. \(f(7)=\tfrac{14}3-\tfrac53=3\ge1\) ✓; \(f(-3)=-2-\tfrac53=-\tfrac{11}3\), score \(\tfrac{11}3\ge1\) ✓. So \(\alpha_2=\alpha_4=0\).
Step 4 — the prices. Balance: \(\alpha_1=\alpha_3\). Stationarity: \(w=4\alpha_1-1\cdot\alpha_3=3\alpha_1=\tfrac23\), so \(\alpha_1=\alpha_3=\tfrac29\). Shortcut check: \(2/(4-1)^2=\tfrac29\) ✓. All four KKT items hold, so \((w,b)=(\tfrac23,-\tfrac53)\) is optimal, with \(\boldsymbol\alpha=(\tfrac29,0,\tfrac29,0)\).
Step 5 — width and duality (b). Width \(2/|w|=3\): the street runs from 1 to 4. Primal \(\tfrac12w^2=\tfrac29\). Dual: \(\sum\alpha_i-\tfrac12w^2=\tfrac49-\tfrac29=\tfrac29\). Equal ✓.
Step 6 — the new point (c). \(f(3)=2-\tfrac53=\tfrac13\), score \(\tfrac13<1\): the new point sits inside the old street, so the old answer is no longer legal. The new closest pair is \(1\) and \(3\): \(3w+b=1\), \(w+b=-1\), so \(w=1\), \(b=-2\). Check: \(f(4)=2\), \(f(7)=5\), \(f(-3)=-5\) — all fine. Prices: \(\alpha_3=\alpha_5\) and \(w=3\alpha_5-\alpha_3=2\alpha_5=1\), so \(\alpha_3=\alpha_5=\tfrac12\), the rest 0. The point \(x=4\) has been released: it now scores 2.

answers at a glance: (a) \(w=\tfrac23\), \(b=-\tfrac53\), \(\boldsymbol\alpha=(\tfrac29,0,\tfrac29,0)\). (b) width 3; primal \(=\) dual \(=\tfrac29\). (c) \(f(3)=\tfrac13<1\), so the answer changes: \(w=1\), \(b=-2\), \(\alpha_3=\alpha_5=\tfrac12\), others 0; width 2.

Problem 8mediumhard margin · KKT

A hard-margin SVM is trained on \(\mathbf x_1=(0,3)\), \(y=+1\); \(\mathbf x_2=(-2,2)\), \(y=+1\); \(\mathbf x_3=(2,1)\), \(y=-1\); \(\mathbf x_4=(3,0)\), \(y=-1\). The answer is claimed to be \(\mathbf w=(-\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\), \(b=-\tfrac12\). (a) Use KKT to find all \(\alpha_i\) and so verify the claim. (b) Give the width, the support vectors, the boundary and both edges. Does removing \(\mathbf x_2\) change the answer? (c) Classify \((1,1)\). Is it inside the street?

What this tests. Verification rather than discovery: score every point, zero the prices of the ones with room, solve for the rest, then tick every KKT item. Plan. \(f(\mathbf x)=-\tfrac12x_1+\tfrac12x_2-\tfrac12\); score all four; solve the two-component equation \(\mathbf w=\sum\alpha_iy_i\mathbf x_i\).

Problem 9mediumhard margin · a trap

Three points: \((1,1)\to+1\), \((2,0)\to+1\), \((0,0)\to-1\). (a) Find \(\mathbf w\), \(b\) and the width. (b) Find every \(\alpha_i\). (c) Which points are support vectors? Answer carefully.

What this tests. The one definitional trap in the subject: a point can sit exactly on an edge and still have \(\alpha=0\). Plan. Try "all three on an edge": three linear equations for \(w_1,w_2,b\). Then get the prices from \(\mathbf w=\sum\alpha_iy_i\mathbf x_i\) and see what is forced.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — all three tight. \(w_1+w_2+b=1\), \(2w_1+b=1\), \(-(0+b)=1\).
Step 2 — solve back to front. \(b=-1\); then \(2w_1-1=1\Rightarrow w_1=1\); then \(1+w_2-1=1\Rightarrow w_2=1\). So \(\mathbf w=(1,1)\), \(b=-1\), boundary \(x_1+x_2=1\), width \(2/\sqrt2=\sqrt2\).
Step 3 — the prices (b). \[\begin{aligned}\mathbf w&=\alpha_1(1,1)+\alpha_2(2,0)\\ &\quad-\alpha_3(0,0)\\ &=(\alpha_1+2\alpha_2,\ \alpha_1)=(1,1).\end{aligned}\] Second coordinate: \(\alpha_1=1\). First: \(1+2\alpha_2=1\Rightarrow\alpha_2=0\) — forced. Balance: \(\alpha_1+\alpha_2-\alpha_3=0\Rightarrow\alpha_3=1\).
Step 4 — check. All \(\alpha\ge0\) ✓; all scores equal 1 ✓; complementary slackness holds since every rule is tight ✓; duality: \(\tfrac12\|\mathbf w\|^2=1\) and \(\sum\alpha_i-\tfrac12\|\mathbf w\|^2=2-1=1\) ✓.
Step 5 — the trap (c). All three points lie exactly on an edge, but \((2,0)\) has \(\alpha=0\): it touches the street without pushing on it. Delete it and nothing changes; delete either of the others and the boundary moves. So the support vectors are \((1,1)\) and \((0,0)\) only — the points with \(\alpha>0\).

answers at a glance: \(\mathbf w=(1,1)\), \(b=-1\), boundary \(x_1+x_2=1\), width \(\sqrt2\); \(\boldsymbol\alpha=(1,0,1)\); support vectors \((1,1)\) and \((0,0)\) — \((2,0)\) is on an edge with \(\alpha=0\).

Remember

Say it this way: support vectors are the points with \(\alpha_i>0\); they always lie on an edge, but not every point on an edge is one.

Problem 10mediumsoft margin

A soft-margin SVM trained with \(C=1\) returns these prices: A: \(\alpha=0\); B: \(0.4\); C: \(1.0\); D: \(1.0\); E: \(0.15\). (a) Classify each as a non-support vector, a free support vector or a bounded one. (b) What do you know about each point's slack and score? (c) Which may be used to compute \(b\)? (d) Which are definitely misclassified?

What this tests. The three price regimes of §10, read from the prices to the geometry. Plan. Compare each \(\alpha\) with 0 and with \(C=1\); that alone fixes the regime, and the regime fixes \(\xi\) and the score.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the three regimes (with \(z=y\,f(\mathbf x)\)): \(\alpha=0\Rightarrow z\ge1\); \(0<\alpha<C\Rightarrow z=1\); \(\alpha=C\Rightarrow z\le1\).

Only B and E are known to score exactly 1, so only they give \(b=y_s-\sum_i\alpha_iy_iK(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_s)\). In practice, average over all free support vectors.
Step 3 — misclassified? (d) None, necessarily. \(\alpha=C\) says only \(z\le1\): at best on the edge, at worst deep on the wrong side. Misclassified needs \(z<0\) (\(\xi>1\)), which the price alone cannot tell you.

answers at a glance: A non-support; B and E free (\(\xi=0\), \(z=1\)); C and D bounded (\(\xi\ge0\), \(z\le1\)); only B and E give \(b\); none is definitely misclassified.

Problem 11hardsoft margin

A colleague proposes \(\mathbf w=(1,1)\), \(b=-3\) for: \((1,1)\to+1\), \((3,3)\to+1\), \((2,0)\to-1\), \((0,2)\to-1\), \((2,2)\to-1\). (a) For each point compute the score \(z\) and slack \(\xi\), and say where it is. (b) Give the total objective \(\tfrac12\|\mathbf w\|^2+C\sum\xi_i\) at \(C=1\) and \(C=10\). (c) Could this \((\mathbf w,b)\) be the soft-margin answer for any \(C>0\)?

What this tests. The hinge formula and the two halves of the objective; then running complementary slackness backwards to audit someone else's answer. Plan. Tabulate score and slack; add the halves; in (c), give each point its price regime and test the balance condition.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — scores and slacks (a). \(f=x_1+x_2-3\), \(\xi=\max(0,1-z)\):
point\(y\)\(f\)\(z\)\(\xi\)where
\((1,1)\)+1−1−12misclassified
\((3,3)\)+1+330outside the street
\((2,0)\)−1−110on an edge
\((0,2)\)−1−110on an edge
\((2,2)\)−1+1−12misclassified
Step 2 — the objective (b). \(\tfrac12\|\mathbf w\|^2=\tfrac12(1+1)=1\) and \(\sum\xi_i=4\). At \(C=1\): \(1+4=5\). At \(C=10\): \(1+40=41\).
Step 3 — regimes (c). \((3,3)\): \(z>1\Rightarrow\alpha=0\). \((1,1)\) and \((2,2)\): \(\xi>0\Rightarrow\alpha=C\). \((2,0)\) and \((0,2)\): \(z=1\Rightarrow\alpha=p,q\in[0,C]\).
Step 4 — balance fails. \[\begin{aligned}\sum_i\alpha_iy_i&=C+0-p-q-C\\ &=-(p+q)=0\\ &\Rightarrow\ p=q=0.\end{aligned}\] Then \(\mathbf w=\sum\alpha_iy_i\mathbf x_i=C(1,1)-C(2,2)=(-C,-C)\). To equal \((1,1)\) we would need \(C=-1\), impossible. So the proposal is not optimal for any \(C>0\).

(The points \((1,1)\) and \((2,2)\) sit on each other's wrong side, so no hard-margin answer exists at all.)

answers at a glance: \(\boldsymbol\xi=(2,0,0,0,2)\) — two misclassified, two on an edge, one outside; objective 5 at \(C=1\), 41 at \(C=10\); (c) no — balance forces the edge prices to 0 and then \(\mathbf w=(-C,-C)\). The true \(C=1\) answer is \(\mathbf w=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\), \(b=-2\), objective 3.25.

Problem 12mediumkernel SVM

Four points form an XNOR pattern (same signs ⇒ class \(+1\)): \(\mathbf x_1=(1,2)\), \(y_1=+1\); \(\mathbf x_2=(-1,-2)\), \(y_2=+1\); \(\mathbf x_3=(1,-2)\), \(y_3=-1\); \(\mathbf x_4=(-1,2)\), \(y_4=-1\). Use \(K(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)=(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf z)^2\). (a) Compute the \(4\times4\) kernel table. (b) Solve the hard-margin dual and name the support vectors. (c) Find \(b\), then classify \((2,1)\) and \((-1,3)\).

What this tests. A kernel table without slips, symmetry to shrink the dual to one unknown, the balance condition, \(b\) from a support vector, and classification by kernel values. Plan. Dots first, square second. Rows 1–2 and 3–4 are identical ⇒ equal prices. One-variable dual, then \(b\) from \(\mathbf x_1\), then the two classifications.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — dot products. Row 1: \(5,-5,-3,3\). By the same arithmetic the full table of \(\mathbf x_i\cdot\mathbf x_j\) is \[\begin{bmatrix}5&-5&-3&3\\-5&5&3&-3\\-3&3&5&-5\\3&-3&-5&5\end{bmatrix}.\]
Step 2 — square every entry (a). \[K=\begin{bmatrix}25&25&9&9\\25&25&9&9\\9&9&25&25\\9&9&25&25\end{bmatrix}.\] The diagonal is \(\|\mathbf x_i\|^4=25\) and the table is symmetric.
Step 3 — symmetry and balance. Rows 1–2 are identical with the same label, as are rows 3–4, so take \(\alpha_1=\alpha_2=a\), \(\alpha_3=\alpha_4=c\). Balance: \(2a-2c=0\Rightarrow c=a\).
Step 4 — the dual with numbers. \(\sum\alpha_i=4a\). The double sum, block by block: same-class blocks \(2\times4\) entries of 25 give \(+200a^2\); cross blocks, 8 entries of 9 with \(y_iy_j=-1\), give \(-72a^2\). Total \(128a^2\): \[\begin{aligned}D(a)&=4a-\tfrac12\cdot128a^2\\ &=4a-64a^2,\\ D'(a)&=4-128a=0\\ \Rightarrow\ a&=\tfrac1{32}.\end{aligned}\] All four prices are \(\tfrac1{32}>0\): every point is a support vector (b).
Step 5 — b (c). From \(\mathbf x_1\) (column 1 of \(K\)): \(\tfrac1{32}(25+25-9-9)+b=1\Rightarrow1+b=1\Rightarrow b=0\). Confirm with \(\mathbf x_3\): \(-\big[\tfrac1{32}(9+9-25-25)+0\big]=-[-1]=1\) ✓.
Step 6 — classify. \((2,1)\): dots \(4,-4,0,0\), squares \(16,16,0,0\), \(f=\tfrac{32}{32}=1\): class \(+1\), exactly on the edge. \((-1,3)\): dots \(5,-5,-7,7\), squares \(25,25,49,49\), \(f=\tfrac{50-98}{32}=-1.5\): class \(-1\).
Step 7 — the curve (optional). \(f(\mathbf x)=\tfrac1{32}\big[2(x_1+2x_2)^2-2(x_1-2x_2)^2\big]=\tfrac{x_1x_2}{2}\): the boundary is the pair of axes, and \(f=\pm1\) are \(x_1x_2=\pm2\), through all four points. Upstairs, \(\mathbf w=(0,\tfrac{\sqrt2}4,0)\) and the width is \(4\sqrt2\).

answers at a glance: \(K\) as above; \(\alpha_i=\tfrac1{32}\) for all four (all support vectors); \(b=0\); \((2,1)\to+1\) with \(f=1\), \((-1,3)\to-1\) with \(f=-1.5\); \(f=x_1x_2/2\).

Remember

Here \(\mathbf x_1\) and \(\mathbf x_2\) look identical to the kernel, so only \(\alpha_1+\alpha_2\) is really fixed; the equal split is the natural (smallest) choice, and \(\mathbf w\), \(b\), \(f\) are the same for any split. The slip to avoid: squaring the coordinates before finishing the dot product.

Problem 13hardkernel SVM

An inner point and two outer ones: \(\mathbf x_1=(0,0)\), \(y_1=+1\); \(\mathbf x_2=(2,0)\), \(y_2=-1\); \(\mathbf x_3=(0,-2)\), \(y_3=-1\). Use \(K(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)=(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf z+1)^2\). (a) Compute the \(3\times3\) kernel table. (b) Solve the dual for the \(\alpha_i\) and find \(b\). (c) Classify \((1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\).

What this tests. The "add 1, then square" habit, a dual whose classes are not balanced (one point against two), and reading a curved boundary out of \(f\). Plan. Rows 2 and 3 are mirror images, so \(\alpha_2=\alpha_3=c\); balance then gives \(\alpha_1=2c\). One-variable dual; \(b\) from \(\mathbf x_1\), whose column is all ones.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — dots, add 1, square (a). \(\mathbf x_1\) is the origin, so every dot product with it is 0; \(\mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf x_2=4\), \(\mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf x_3=0\), \(\mathbf x_3\cdot\mathbf x_3=4\). Then \((0+1)^2=1\) and \((4+1)^2=25\): \[K=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\1&25&1\\1&1&25\end{bmatrix}.\] Without the \(+1\), the origin's row would be all zeros and it would carry no information at all.
Step 2 — balance and symmetry. \(\alpha_1-\alpha_2-\alpha_3=0\), and \(\alpha_2=\alpha_3=c\), so \(\alpha_1=2c\).
Step 3 — the dual. \(\sum\alpha_i=4c\). The double sum, term by term: \((1,1)\): \(4c^2\); \((2,2)\), \((3,3)\): \(25c^2\) each; \((1,2)\)+\((2,1)\): \(2(2c)(c)(-1)(1)=-4c^2\); \((1,3)\)+\((3,1)\): \(-4c^2\); \((2,3)\)+\((3,2)\): \(2c^2\). Total \(48c^2\): \[\begin{aligned}D(c)&=4c-24c^2,\\ D'(c)&=4-48c=0\\ \Rightarrow\ c&=\tfrac1{12}.\end{aligned}\] So \(\alpha_1=\tfrac16\), \(\alpha_2=\alpha_3=\tfrac1{12}\) — all support vectors.
Step 4 — b (b). From \(\mathbf x_1\): \(\tfrac16-\tfrac1{12}-\tfrac1{12}+b=1\Rightarrow b=1\). Confirm with \(\mathbf x_2\): \(-\big[\tfrac16-\tfrac{25}{12}-\tfrac1{12}+1\big]=-[-1]=1\) ✓.
Step 5 — classify (c). \((1,1)\): dots \(0,2,-2\) → kernel \(1,9,1\): \(f=\tfrac16-\tfrac9{12}-\tfrac1{12}+1=\tfrac13\) → class \(+1\). \((1,-1)\): dots \(0,2,2\) → \(1,9,9\): \(f=\tfrac16-\tfrac9{12}-\tfrac9{12}+1=-\tfrac13\) → class \(-1\). Both are inside the street.
Step 6 — the circle (optional). \(f=\tfrac76-\tfrac1{12}\big[(2x_1+1)^2+(2x_2-1)^2\big]\), so \(f=0\iff(x_1+\tfrac12)^2+(x_2-\tfrac12)^2=3.5\): a circle centred at \((-\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\), radius \(\sqrt{3.5}\approx1.87\).

answers at a glance: \(K=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\1&25&1\\1&1&25\end{bmatrix}\); \(\boldsymbol\alpha=(\tfrac16,\tfrac1{12},\tfrac1{12})\), all support vectors; \(b=1\); \((1,1)\to+1\) (\(f=\tfrac13\)), \((1,-1)\to-1\) (\(f=-\tfrac13\)); boundary: the circle \((x_1+\tfrac12)^2+(x_2-\tfrac12)^2=3.5\).

Problem 14hardkernel SVM · b ≠ 0

\(\mathbf x_1=(1,1)\), \(y_1=+1\); \(\mathbf x_2=(-1,-1)\), \(y_2=+1\); \(\mathbf x_3=(2,0)\), \(y_3=-1\); \(\mathbf x_4=(-2,0)\), \(y_4=-1\). Use \(K=(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf z)^2\). (a) Compute the kernel table. (b) Solve the dual and name the support vectors. (c) Find \(b\) and classify \((0,2)\) and \((3,0)\).

What this tests. The same skills as Problem 12 — but the bias is not zero, so anyone who "remembers \(b=0\)" loses the classifications. It also shows a kernel SVM drawing a hyperbola. Plan. Dots, square; rows 1–2 and 3–4 identical ⇒ equal prices; balance; one-variable dual; \(b\) from \(\mathbf x_1\), confirmed from \(\mathbf x_3\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — dots (a). \(\mathbf x_1\cdot\mathbf x_1=2\), \(\mathbf x_1\cdot\mathbf x_2=-2\), \(\mathbf x_1\cdot\mathbf x_3=2\), \(\mathbf x_1\cdot\mathbf x_4=-2\); \(\mathbf x_3\cdot\mathbf x_3=4\), \(\mathbf x_3\cdot\mathbf x_4=-4\). Square: \[K=\begin{bmatrix}4&4&4&4\\4&4&4&4\\4&4&16&16\\4&4&16&16\end{bmatrix}.\]
Step 2 — symmetry, balance, dual (b). \(\alpha_1=\alpha_2=a\), \(\alpha_3=\alpha_4=c\), balance \(c=a\). Blocks: \(4\times4\) gives \(16a^2\), \(4\times16\) gives \(64a^2\), cross blocks \(8\times4\) with a minus: \(-32a^2\). Total \(48a^2\): \[D(a)=4a-24a^2\ \Rightarrow\ a=\tfrac1{12}.\] All four prices are \(\tfrac1{12}\): all support vectors.
Step 3 — b (c). From \(\mathbf x_1\): \(\tfrac1{12}(4+4-4-4)+b=1\Rightarrow b=1\). Confirm from \(\mathbf x_3\): \(-\big[\tfrac1{12}(4+4-16-16)+1\big]=-[-2+1]=1\) ✓. Had you assumed \(b=0\), \(\mathbf x_1\) would score 0 — on the boundary, which is absurd for a support vector.
Step 4 — classify. \((0,2)\): dots \(2,-2,0,0\) → \(4,4,0,0\): \(f=\tfrac8{12}+1=\tfrac53\) → \(+1\). \((3,0)\): dots \(3,-3,6,-6\) → \(9,9,36,36\): \(f=\tfrac{18-72}{12}+1=-3.5\) → \(-1\).
Step 5 — the curve (optional). \(f=\dfrac{(x_1+x_2)^2-4x_1^2}{6}+1\). The boundary is \((x_1+x_2)^2-4x_1^2=-6\), i.e. \((x_2-x_1)(x_2+3x_1)=-6\) — a hyperbola. Upstairs the four points collapse to two, \((1,\sqrt2,1)\) and \((4,0,0)\); \(\mathbf w=\tfrac16(-3,\sqrt2,1)\), \(\|\mathbf w\|=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\), width \(2\sqrt3\) — the distance between those two points.

answers at a glance: \(K\) as above; \(\alpha_i=\tfrac1{12}\), all support vectors; \(b=1\); \((0,2)\to+1\) (\(f=\tfrac53\)), \((3,0)\to-1\) (\(f=-3.5\)); boundary a hyperbola \((x_2-x_1)(x_2+3x_1)=-6\).

Problem 15hardkernel SVM · given prices

\(\mathbf x_1=(0,0)\), \(y_1=+1\); \(\mathbf x_2=(1,1)\), \(y_2=-1\); \(\mathbf x_3=(1,-1)\), \(y_3=-1\); \(\mathbf x_4=(3,0)\), \(y_4=-1\). A hard-margin SVM with \(K=(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf z+1)^2\) reports \(\alpha_1=\tfrac12\), \(\alpha_2=\tfrac14\), \(\alpha_3=\tfrac14\), \(\alpha_4=0\). (a) Compute the kernel table. (b) Check the balance, name the support vectors and explain \(\alpha_4=0\). (c) Find \(b\) from one support vector and confirm it from another. (d) Classify \((1,0)\) and \((-2,1)\).

What this tests. The table again; what a zero price means; \(b\) when the prices are handed to you; classifying with support vectors only. Plan. Dots, add 1, square. Balance in one line. \(b\) from \(\mathbf x_1\) (its column is all ones), confirm with \(\mathbf x_2\), then check \(\mathbf x_4\) really is outside the street.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the table (a). Dots: everything with \(\mathbf x_1\) is 0; \(\mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf x_2=2\), \(\mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf x_3=0\), \(\mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf x_4=3\), \(\mathbf x_3\cdot\mathbf x_3=2\), \(\mathbf x_3\cdot\mathbf x_4=3\), \(\mathbf x_4\cdot\mathbf x_4=9\). Add 1 and square: \[K=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1&1\\1&9&1&16\\1&1&9&16\\1&16&16&100\end{bmatrix}.\]
Step 2 — balance and support vectors (b). \(\tfrac12-\tfrac14-\tfrac14-0=0\) ✓. Support vectors: \(\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_2,\mathbf x_3\). \(\alpha_4=0\) means \(\mathbf x_4\)'s rule has room: it lies strictly outside the street and plays no part in \(f\) (checked in Step 4).
Step 3 — b (c). From \(\mathbf x_1\): \(\tfrac12-\tfrac14-\tfrac14-0+b=1\Rightarrow b=1\). Confirm with \(\mathbf x_2\) (column \(1,9,1,16\)): \(\tfrac12-\tfrac94-\tfrac14=-2\), and \(y_2(-2+1)=(-1)(-1)=1\) ✓.
Step 4 — is x₄ really outside? Column 4 is \(1,16,16,100\): \(f(\mathbf x_4)=\tfrac12-4-4+1=-6.5\), score \(6.5>1\) ✓. Consistent with \(\alpha_4=0\). (If a given price ever fails this check, the numbers are not an answer — say so.)
Step 5 — classify (d). \((1,0)\): dots with the support vectors \(0,1,1\) → \(1,4,4\): \(f=\tfrac12-1-1+1=-\tfrac12\) → \(-1\). \((-2,1)\): dots \(0,-1,-3\) → \(1,0,4\): \(f=\tfrac12-0-1+1=\tfrac12\) → \(+1\).
Step 6 — the circle (optional). \(f=\tfrac32-\tfrac12\big[(x_1+1)^2+x_2^2\big]\), so \(f=0\iff(x_1+1)^2+x_2^2=3\): a circle centred at \((-1,0)\) with radius \(\sqrt3\approx1.73\). The origin gives \(f=1\), \((1,\pm1)\) give \(f=-1\), \((3,0)\) gives \(-6.5\) ✓.

answers at a glance: \(K\) as above; balance holds; support vectors \(\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_2,\mathbf x_3\), and \(\mathbf x_4\) is outside the street (score 6.5); \(b=1\); \((1,0)\to-1\) (\(f=-\tfrac12\)), \((-2,1)\to+1\) (\(f=\tfrac12\)); boundary \((x_1+1)^2+x_2^2=3\).

Problem 16hardkernel SVM · 1-D

One-dimensional data: \(x_1=1\), \(y_1=+1\); \(x_2=-1\), \(y_2=+1\); \(x_3=3\), \(y_3=-1\); \(x_4=-3\), \(y_4=-1\). No single threshold separates them. Use \(K(x,z)=(xz+1)^2\). (a) Compute the kernel table. (b) Solve the hard-margin dual. (c) Find \(b\), write \(f(x)\) in closed form, give the boundary on the line, and classify \(x=2\) and \(x=-2.5\). (d) Write the feature map \(\varphi(x)\) and use it to check \(K(1,3)\).

What this tests. Whether the recipe survives with scalars instead of vectors; mirror-image symmetry; turning \(f\) into a formula; the meaning of \(\varphi\). Plan. Products, add 1, square. Reflecting \(x\to-x\) swaps the points in pairs, so \(\alpha_1=\alpha_2\), \(\alpha_3=\alpha_4\); balance equates them.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the table (a). Products \(x_ix_j\), add 1, square: \((1+1)^2=4\), \((-1+1)^2=0\), \((3+1)^2=16\), \((-3+1)^2=4\), \((9+1)^2=100\), \((-9+1)^2=64\): \[K=\begin{bmatrix}4&0&16&4\\0&4&4&16\\16&4&100&64\\4&16&64&100\end{bmatrix}.\] \(K_{12}=0\): the points \(1\) and \(-1\) are at right angles upstairs — kernel values may be zero.
Step 2 — symmetry and balance. \(\alpha_1=\alpha_2=a\), \(\alpha_3=\alpha_4=c\); balance \(2a-2c=0\Rightarrow c=a\).
Step 3 — the dual (b). Same-class blocks: \((4+0+0+4)a^2=8a^2\) and \((100+64+64+100)a^2=328a^2\); cross blocks: \(-2(16+4+4+16)a^2=-80a^2\). Total \(256a^2\): \[D(a)=4a-128a^2\ \Rightarrow\ a=\tfrac1{64}.\] All four prices \(\tfrac1{64}\): all support vectors.
Step 4 — b and f (c). From \(x_1=1\) (column \(4,0,16,4\)): \(\tfrac1{64}(4+0-16-4)+b=1\Rightarrow b=1+\tfrac14=\tfrac54\). Confirm from \(x_3\): \(-\big[\tfrac1{64}(16+4-100-64)+\tfrac54\big]=-\big[-\tfrac{144}{64}+\tfrac{80}{64}\big]=1\) ✓. Closed form, using \((u+1)^2+(1-u)^2=2u^2+2\): \[\begin{aligned}f(x)&=\tfrac1{64}\big[(2x^2+2)\\ &\qquad-(18x^2+2)\big]+\tfrac54\\ &=\frac{5-x^2}{4}.\end{aligned}\]
Step 5 — boundary and classification. \(f=0\iff x=\pm\sqrt5\approx\pm2.24\): two thresholds, "class \(+1\) if \(|x|<\sqrt5\)". Checks: \(f(\pm1)=1\), \(f(\pm3)=-1\) ✓. \(f(2)=\tfrac{5-4}4=\tfrac14\) → \(+1\) (inside the street); \(f(-2.5)=\tfrac{5-6.25}4=-0.3125\) → \(-1\).
Step 6 — the feature map (d). \((xz+1)^2=x^2z^2+2xz+1=(x^2)(z^2)+(\sqrt2x)(\sqrt2z)+(1)(1)\), so \(\varphi(x)=(x^2,\sqrt2x,1)\). Check: \(\varphi(1)=(1,\sqrt2,1)\), \(\varphi(3)=(9,3\sqrt2,1)\), dot product \(9+6+1=16=(3+1)^2\) ✓. Upstairs \(\mathbf w=(-\tfrac14,0,0)\): the cut is \(x^2=5\), with edges \(x^2=1\) and \(x^2=9\) — a flat street 8 wide in the lifted space, two thresholds on the line.

answers at a glance: \(K\) as above; \(\alpha_i=\tfrac1{64}\) for all four; \(b=\tfrac54\); \(f(x)=(5-x^2)/4\), boundary \(x=\pm\sqrt5\); \(x=2\to+1\), \(x=-2.5\to-1\); \(\varphi(x)=(x^2,\sqrt2x,1)\), \(K(1,3)=16\).

Remember

As in Problem 12, symmetry picks the natural prices, but here other price sets give the very same machine too (for example \(\boldsymbol\alpha=(0,\tfrac1{32},\tfrac1{96},\tfrac1{48})\)); \(b\) and \(f\) never change. And a kernel SVM in one dimension can still draw two thresholds: flat upstairs, curved down here.
