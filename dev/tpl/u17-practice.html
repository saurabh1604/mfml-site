Practice arena — sixteen problems, solved in full

Sixteen problems, easy to hard: a forward pass by hand, one step with matrices, counting weights for every design in the unit, a read-out and its loss, temperature forwards and backwards, blame through three steps, truncated BPTT, the step at which memory is gone, eigenvalues that decide, clipping, a full LSTM step, recorded runs to diagnose, a full GRU step, notes read both ways, greedy against beam, and BLEU. Every number was checked by machine.

Three habits do most of the work. Write the notes down: keep a table of every \(h_t\) (and \(c_t\)) on the way forward, because the way back needs them. Count steps, not words: from \(h_k\) to \(h_T\) there are \(T-k\) factors. And look for the power: whenever something is multiplied by the same number again and again, it is \(w^T\), and \(\ln\) turns "how many steps?" into a division.

Problem 1easyforward pass

A one-number recurrent cell has \(w=0.8\), \(u=1\), \(b=0\) and \(h_0=0\). It reads \(x=(1,\,-1,\,0.5)\). Find \(h_1\), \(h_2\) and \(h_3\) (four decimals).

What this tests. \(h_t=\tanh(wh_{t-1}+ux_t+b)\), one step at a time. Plan. Keep old, add new, squeeze — three times.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — word 1. \(h_1=\tanh(0.8\cdot0+1)=\tanh(1)\approx0.7616\).
Step 2 — word 2. \(0.8\cdot0.7616=0.6093\); add \(-1\): \(-0.3907\). \(h_2=\tanh(-0.3907)\approx-0.3720\).
Step 3 — word 3. \(0.8\cdot(-0.3720)=-0.2976\); add \(0.5\): \(0.2024\). \(h_3=\tanh(0.2024)\approx0.1997\).

answers at a glance: \(h_1\approx0.7616\), \(h_2\approx-0.3720\), \(h_3\approx0.1997\).

Remember

The note is a running mix: the old note, scaled by \(w\), plus the new word, then squeezed. A negative word can flip the sign of the whole note.

Problem 2easya step with matrices

A cell has a note of 2 numbers and words of 1 number: \[\begin{aligned}W&=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&-0.5\\ 0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad U=\begin{pmatrix}1\\ 2\end{pmatrix},\\ \mathbf b&=\begin{pmatrix}0\\ -1\end{pmatrix},\quad \mathbf h_0=\begin{pmatrix}1\\ 0\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}\] (a) Find \(\mathbf h_1\) for the word \(x_1=0.5\). (b) Find \(\mathbf h_2\) for the word \(x_2=0\).

What this tests. The vector cell \(\mathbf h_t=\tanh(W\mathbf h_{t-1}+Ux_t+\mathbf b)\), with tanh applied entry by entry. Plan. Three pieces, add, squeeze each entry.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a), the three pieces. \(W\mathbf h_0=(0.5,0)\), \(Ux_1=(0.5,1)\), \(\mathbf b=(0,-1)\). Sum: \((1,0)\).
Step 2 — (a), squeeze. \(\mathbf h_1=(\tanh1,\tanh0)\approx(0.7616,0)\).
Step 3 — (b). \(W\mathbf h_1=(0.5\cdot0.7616-0,\ 0)=(0.3808,0)\); \(Ux_2=(0,0)\); add \(\mathbf b\): \((0.3808,-1)\).
Step 4 — (b), squeeze. \(\mathbf h_2\approx(\tanh0.3808,\ \tanh(-1))\approx(0.3634,\ -0.7616)\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(\mathbf h_1\approx(0.7616,\ 0)\). (b) \(\mathbf h_2\approx(0.3634,\ -0.7616)\).

Remember

The shift \(\mathbf b\) acts at every step, even when the word is 0. Here it pushes the second entry to \(\tanh(-1)\) on its own.

Problem 3mediumcounting weights

A word tagger uses notes of \(h=64\) numbers and words of \(d=32\) numbers. (a) How many numbers does one plain cell learn? (b) A two-way plain reader? (c) Two floors of one-way plain cells? (d) A one-way LSTM, and a one-way GRU? (e) A two-way LSTM with a read-out over 12 tags on top of each word's two-way note (read-out \(V\) and \(\mathbf c\) included)?

What this tests. The block count \(h(h+d)+h\) and what changes when you add directions, floors and gates. Plan. Write the general block once; then ask, for each design, how many blocks and what each block reads.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a), one block. \(P=h(h+d)+h=64\cdot96+64=6144+64=6208\).
Step 2 — (b). Two separate readers of the same shape: \(2P=12\,416\).
Step 3 — (c). Floor 2 reads 64-number notes, not 32-number words: \(64\cdot(64+64)+64=8192+64=8256\). Total \(6208+8256=14\,464\).
Step 4 — (d). An LSTM has four blocks: \(4P=24\,832\). A GRU has three: \(3P=18\,624\).
Step 5 — (e). Two LSTM readers: \(2\cdot24\,832=49\,664\). Each word's note has \(2h=128\) numbers, so \(V\) is \(12\times128\) and \(\mathbf c\) has 12: \(1536+12=1548\). Total \(49\,664+1548=51\,212\).

answers at a glance: (a) 6208. (b) 12 416. (c) 14 464. (d) LSTM 24 832, GRU 18 624. (e) 51 212.

Remember

Every design is built from the same block. Count the blocks, and check what each block reads: words (\(d\) numbers), notes (\(h\)) or two-way notes (\(2h\)).

Problem 4easyread-out and loss

After reading "the chai is", a language model's read-out gives the scores hot 3, sweet 1, ready 0, cold −1. (a) Turn them into probabilities (four decimals). (b) The true next word is "sweet". What is this step's loss? What would it have been if the true word were "hot"? (c) What blame does the read-out send back (prediction minus truth)?

What this tests. Softmax, the surprise \(-\ln p\) of the true word, and Unit 15's "prediction − truth". Plan. Exponentiate, add, divide; then read off the true word's share.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — exponentiate. \(e^3\approx20.0855\), \(e^1\approx2.7183\), \(e^0=1\), \(e^{-1}\approx0.3679\); total \(\approx24.1717\).
Step 2 — (a), divide. hot \(\approx0.8310\), sweet \(\approx0.1125\), ready \(\approx0.0414\), cold \(\approx0.0152\).
Step 3 — (b). Loss \(=-\ln0.1125\approx2.185\). Had the true word been "hot": \(-\ln0.8310\approx0.185\).
Step 4 — (c). Subtract the one-hot truth (1 at "sweet"): \((0.8310,\ 0.1125-1,\ 0.0414,\ 0.0152)=(0.8310,\ -0.8875,\ 0.0414,\ 0.0152)\).

answers at a glance: (a) 0.8310, 0.1125, 0.0414, 0.0152. (b) ≈ 2.185 (and ≈ 0.185 for "hot"). (c) (0.8310, −0.8875, 0.0414, 0.0152).

Remember

A confident wrong guess is expensive: "sweet" had 0.11 and costs 2.19. The blame pushes "hot" down by 0.83 and pulls "sweet" up by 0.89.

Problem 5mediumtemperature

A model has two candidate next words with scores 2 and 0. (a) Find the first word's probability at \(T=1\), \(T=0.5\) and \(T=2\). (b) Another model gives two words the probabilities 0.8 and 0.2 at \(T=1\). At what temperature would it give them 0.6 and 0.4? (c) What does it give them as \(T\to0\)?

What this tests. \(p_i\propto e^{s_i/T}\), and the ratio rule \(p_i/p_j=e^{(s_i-s_j)/T}\) used backwards. Plan. For two words, softmax is a sigmoid of the gap; for (b) find the gap first, then the \(T\) that gives the new ratio.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). With two words, \(p_1=\dfrac{e^{2/T}}{e^{2/T}+e^{0}}=\sigma(2/T)\). \(T=1\): \(\sigma(2)\approx0.8808\). \(T=0.5\): \(\sigma(4)\approx0.9820\). \(T=2\): \(\sigma(1)\approx0.7311\).
Step 2 — (b), the gap. At \(T=1\) the ratio is \(0.8/0.2=4=e^{\Delta}\), so the score gap is \(\Delta=\ln4\approx1.3863\).
Step 3 — (b), the new temperature. We need \(e^{\Delta/T}=0.6/0.4=1.5\), so \(\Delta/T=\ln1.5\approx0.4055\) and \(T=1.3863/0.4055\approx3.419\).
Step 4 — (c). As \(T\to0\) the gap \(\Delta/T\) grows without limit: the shares go to \(1\) and \(0\) — greedy.

answers at a glance: (a) 0.8808, 0.9820, 0.7311. (b) \(T=\ln4/\ln1.5\approx3.419\). (c) 1 and 0.

Remember

Temperature only rescales the score gaps. Two shares always stand in the ratio \(e^{\text{gap}/T}\), so you can run the rule forwards or backwards.

Problem 6mediumblame through time

A one-number cell has \(w=0.8\), \(u=1\), \(b=0\), \(h_0=0\) and reads \(x=(1,0,0)\). (a) Find \(h_1,h_2,h_3\). (b) Find \(\partial h_3/\partial h_2\), \(\partial h_2/\partial h_1\) and \(\partial h_3/\partial h_1\). (c) Find \(\partial h_3/\partial x_1\).

What this tests. The BPTT product \(\prod w(1-h_t^2)\). Plan. Forward first and store every note; then one factor per step.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(h_1=\tanh1\approx0.7616\). \(h_2=\tanh(0.6093)\approx0.5436\). \(h_3=\tanh(0.4349)\approx0.4094\).
Step 2 — (b), one factor per step. \[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial h_3}{\partial h_2}&=0.8\,(1-0.4094^2)\\ &\approx0.6659\\ \frac{\partial h_2}{\partial h_1}&=0.8\,(1-0.5436^2)\\ &\approx0.5636\end{aligned}\]
Step 3 — (b), multiply. \(\partial h_3/\partial h_1\approx0.6659\times0.5636\approx0.3753\).
Step 4 — (c). \(h_1=\tanh(ux_1)\), so \(\partial h_1/\partial x_1=u\,(1-h_1^2)\approx0.4200\). Then \(\partial h_3/\partial x_1\approx0.3753\times0.4200\approx0.1576\).

answers at a glance: (a) 0.7616, 0.5436, 0.4094. (b) 0.6659, 0.5636, 0.3753. (c) \(\approx0.1576\).

Remember

With \(w=0.8\) instead of 0.5, each factor is bigger, and the first word still reaches \(h_3\) at more than a third of its size. The factors decide everything.

Problem 7mediumtruncated BPTT

A 2 500-character text is trained with truncated BPTT in chunks of \(k=50\) characters (1–50, 51–100, …). (a) Give the general number of chunks for a text of \(N\) characters, then apply it. (b) The blame from character 130 walks back to which character? (c) Can training learn a link between characters 30 and 70? Between 60 and 90? (d) Full BPTT would walk the blame from the last character back to the first. How many steps is that, against the longest walk in the truncated run?

What this tests. What truncation cuts (the blame) and what it keeps (the forward note). Plan. Find each character's chunk with \(\lceil t/k\rceil\); blame never leaves its chunk.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(\lceil N/k\rceil\) chunks: \(\lceil2500/50\rceil=50\).
Step 2 — (b). Character 130 is in chunk \(\lceil130/50\rceil=3\), which holds 101–150. Its blame walks back to character 101 (29 steps).
Step 3 — (c). 30 is in chunk 1 and 70 in chunk 2: no blame crosses the cut after 50, so that link cannot be learned. 60 and 90 are both in chunk 2 (51–100): yes.
Step 4 — (d). Full BPTT: from character 2 500 back to character 1 is 2 499 steps. Truncated: at most \(k-1=49\) steps, inside one chunk.

answers at a glance: (a) \(\lceil N/k\rceil\), here 50. (b) character 101. (c) 30 and 70: no; 60 and 90: yes. (d) 2 499 steps against at most 49.

Remember

Truncation makes training cheap and limits what can be learned to links shorter than \(k\) — but the note still carries every earlier character forward.

Problem 8mediumfade or explode

In the loop \(h_t=w\,h_{t-1}\), the blame after \(T\) steps is \(w^T\). (a) Write a formula for the first \(T\) at which \(w^T\) drops below \(\varepsilon\) (for \(0<w<1\)). (b) Apply it for \(w=0.8\), \(\varepsilon=10^{-3}\). (c) For \(w=1.2\), find the first \(T\) at which \(w^T\) exceeds 1000.

What this tests. Turning "how many steps?" into a division with logarithms. Plan. Take \(\ln\) of both sides; mind that \(\ln w<0\) flips the inequality.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(w^T<\varepsilon\iff T\ln w<\ln\varepsilon\iff T>\dfrac{\ln\varepsilon}{\ln w}\) (dividing by the negative \(\ln w\) flips the sign). So \(T=\big\lfloor\ln\varepsilon/\ln w\big\rfloor+1\).
Step 2 — (b). \(\ln10^{-3}/\ln0.8\approx-6.9078/-0.2231\approx30.96\), so \(T=31\). Check: \(0.8^{30}\approx0.00124\), \(0.8^{31}\approx0.00099\).
Step 3 — (c). \(1.2^T>1000\iff T>\ln1000/\ln1.2\approx6.9078/0.1823\approx37.89\), so \(T=38\). Check: \(1.2^{37}\approx850.6\), \(1.2^{38}\approx1020.7\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(T=\lfloor\ln\varepsilon/\ln w\rfloor+1\). (b) 31. (c) 38.

Remember

Even gentle numbers are ruthless over a few dozen steps: 0.8 loses a factor of 1000 in 31 steps, and 1.2 gains one in 38.

Problem 9mediumeigenvalues decide

Two recurrent matrices: \(A=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&0.2\\ 0.2&0.6\end{pmatrix}\) and \(B=\begin{pmatrix}1.1&0.3\\ 0.3&1.1\end{pmatrix}\). (a) Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of each and say whether memory fades or explodes. (b) Find \(A^{10}\mathbf v\) and \(B^{10}\mathbf v\) for \(\mathbf v=(1,0)\).

What this tests. Splitting \(\mathbf v\) along eigen-directions (Unit 4) and powering each part. Plan. Both matrices have the form \(\begin{pmatrix}p&q\\ q&p\end{pmatrix}\): eigenvectors \((1,1)\), \((1,-1)\), eigenvalues \(p\pm q\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(A(1,1)=(0.8,0.8)\), \(A(1,-1)=(0.4,-0.4)\): eigenvalues 0.8 and 0.4, both below 1 — fades. \(B(1,1)=(1.4,1.4)\), \(B(1,-1)=(0.8,-0.8)\): eigenvalues 1.4 and 0.8; \(|\lambda|_{\max}=1.4>1\) — explodes.
Step 2 — split v. \((1,0)=\tfrac12(1,1)+\tfrac12(1,-1)\).
Step 3 — (b), A. \(A^{10}\mathbf v=\tfrac12\,0.8^{10}(1,1)+\tfrac12\,0.4^{10}(1,-1)\). With \(\tfrac12\,0.8^{10}\approx0.053687\) and \(\tfrac12\,0.4^{10}\approx0.0000524\): \(\approx(0.05374,\ 0.05363)\).
Step 4 — (b), B. \(B^{10}\mathbf v=\tfrac12\,1.4^{10}(1,1)+\tfrac12\,0.8^{10}(1,-1)\approx14.4627(1,1)+0.0537(1,-1)\approx(14.516,\ 14.409)\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(A\): 0.8 on \((1,1)\), 0.4 on \((1,-1)\) — fades; \(B\): 1.4 on \((1,1)\), 0.8 on \((1,-1)\) — explodes. (b) \(A^{10}\mathbf v\approx(0.0537,\ 0.0536)\), \(B^{10}\mathbf v\approx(14.52,\ 14.41)\).

Remember

After many steps the note points along the eigenvector with the biggest \(|\lambda|\), and its size goes like \(|\lambda|_{\max}^t\).

Problem 10easyclipping

The limit is \(c=4\) and the step size \(\eta=0.1\). (a) Clip \(\mathbf g=(6,-8,0)\) and give the weight change \(-\eta\mathbf g'\). (b) Clip \(\mathbf g=(1,2,2)\).

What this tests. \(\mathbf g'=\min(1,c/\|\mathbf g\|)\,\mathbf g\). Plan. Length first; scale only if it is too long.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a), length. \(\|\mathbf g\|=\sqrt{36+64+0}=10>4\).
Step 2 — (a), scale. Multiply by \(4/10=0.4\): \(\mathbf g'=(2.4,-3.2,0)\), of length 4.
Step 3 — (a), step. \(-0.1\,\mathbf g'=(-0.24,\ 0.32,\ 0)\).
Step 4 — (b). \(\|\mathbf g\|=\sqrt{1+4+4}=3\le4\), so nothing changes: \(\mathbf g'=(1,2,2)\).

answers at a glance: (a) \(\mathbf g'=(2.4,-3.2,0)\), change \((-0.24,0.32,0)\). (b) \((1,2,2)\), unchanged.

Remember

Clipping scales the whole vector by one number. The direction — the ratio 6 : −8 — is kept.

Problem 11mediumone LSTM step

A one-number LSTM has \(c_{t-1}=2\). Before their squeezes, the gates and the candidate come out as: forget 2, input 0, output 1, candidate 1. Find \(f,i,o,g\), then \(c_t\) and \(h_t\).

What this tests. Gates are sigmoids, the candidate is a tanh; \(c_t=fc_{t-1}+ig\), \(h_t=o\tanh c_t\). Plan. Four squeezes, then the two formulas.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the gates. \(f=\sigma(2)\approx0.8808\), \(i=\sigma(0)=0.5\), \(o=\sigma(1)\approx0.7311\).
Step 2 — the candidate. \(g=\tanh(1)\approx0.7616\).
Step 3 — the cell state. \(c_t=0.8808\cdot2+0.5\cdot0.7616\approx1.7616+0.3808=2.1424\).
Step 4 — the note. \(\tanh(2.1424)\approx0.9728\), so \(h_t\approx0.7311\cdot0.9728\approx0.7112\).

answers at a glance: \(f\approx0.8808\), \(i=0.5\), \(o\approx0.7311\), \(g\approx0.7616\); \(c_t\approx2.1424\), \(h_t\approx0.7112\).

Remember

The cell state may grow past 1 — it is never squeezed on the lane. Only the note \(h_t\) is squeezed, when it is read out through the window.

Problem 12harddiagnose the runs

Three recurrent models were trained on the same task. For each, the size of the blame reaching the note \(k\) steps back from the end was recorded:
runk = 012345
A10.420.1760.07410.03110.0131
B12.14.419.2619.440.8
C10.970.9410.9130.8850.859
(a) Find each run's factor per step. (b) Predict the blame 20 steps back for each. (c) One run is an LSTM, one a plain cell with small weights, one a plain cell with big weights. Which is which, and which fix does each plain cell need? (d) If run B's 40.8 were the whole gradient's length, by what factor would clipping at \(c=5\) scale it? (e) Run C's lane factor is \(f\). What is the smallest \(f\) that would still deliver at least half the blame from 100 steps back?

What this tests. Reading a geometric sequence, and matching it to fade, explode or express lane. Plan. Divide neighbours; then raise the factor to the 20th power.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). Neighbour ratios are steady: A ≈ 0.42 (\(0.0131^{1/5}\approx0.42\)), B ≈ 2.1 (\(40.8^{1/5}\approx2.1\)), C ≈ 0.97 (\(0.859^{1/5}\approx0.97\)).
Step 2 — (b). A: \(0.42^{20}\approx2.9\times10^{-8}\). B: \(2.1^{20}\approx2.8\times10^{6}\). C: \(0.97^{20}\approx0.544\).
Step 3 — (c). C holds the blame steady at 0.97 per step: the LSTM's express lane with \(f\approx0.97\). A fades (\(|\lambda|<1\)): the plain cell with small weights — it needs gates (an LSTM or GRU). B explodes (\(|\lambda|>1\)): the plain cell with big weights — it needs clipping first aid (and gates would help it too).
Step 4 — (d). \(40.8>5\), so scale by \(5/40.8\approx0.1225\).
Step 5 — (e). We need \(f^{100}\ge0.5\), so \(f\ge0.5^{1/100}=e^{\ln0.5/100}=e^{-0.006931}\approx0.99309\). Run C's 0.97 is far below that: \(0.97^{100}\approx0.048\).

answers at a glance: (a) 0.42, 2.1, 0.97. (b) \(\approx2.9\times10^{-8}\), \(\approx2.8\times10^{6}\), \(\approx0.544\). (c) A = plain, small weights → gates; B = plain, big weights → clipping; C = LSTM. (d) \(\approx0.1225\). (e) \(f\ge0.99309\).

Remember

Blame through time is a geometric sequence. One ratio tells you everything: below 1 it fades, above 1 it explodes, near 1 you have an express lane.

Problem 13mediumone GRU step

A one-number GRU has old note \(h_{t-1}=-0.5\) and input \(x=2\). Its update score is 1 and its reset score is −1. The draft uses weight 1 on the word and 2 on the consulted old note: \(\tilde h_t=\tanh(1\cdot x+2\cdot r\,h_{t-1})\). (a) Find \(z\), \(r\), \(\tilde h_t\) and \(h_t\) (four decimals). (b) Redo \(\tilde h_t\) and \(h_t\) with the reset gate wide open, \(r=1\). What did the reset gate change?

What this tests. The two GRU gates in the right places: \(r\) inside the draft, \(z\) in the blend. Plan. Squeeze the scores, draft, then blend \((1-z)h_{t-1}+z\tilde h_t\).

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the gates. \(z=\sigma(1)\approx0.7311\), \(r=\sigma(-1)\approx0.2689\).
Step 2 — the draft. \(r\,h_{t-1}\approx0.2689\cdot(-0.5)=-0.1345\); times 2: \(-0.2689\); add the word: \(2-0.2689=1.7311\). \(\tilde h_t=\tanh(1.7311)\approx0.9392\).
Step 3 — the blend. \(h_t=0.2689\cdot(-0.5)+0.7311\cdot0.9392\approx-0.1345+0.6866=0.5521\).
Step 4 — (b). With \(r=1\): \(\tilde h_t=\tanh(2+2\cdot(-0.5))=\tanh(1)\approx0.7616\) and \(h_t\approx-0.1345+0.5568=0.4223\). The mostly shut reset gate hid most of the negative old note from the draft, so the draft came out higher (0.9392 against 0.7616).

answers at a glance: (a) \(z\approx0.7311\), \(r\approx0.2689\), \(\tilde h_t\approx0.9392\), \(h_t\approx0.5521\). (b) \(\tilde h_t\approx0.7616\), \(h_t\approx0.4223\).

Remember

The reset gate decides how much of the old note the draft may consult; the update gate decides how much of the draft enters the note. The old note still passes through \((1-z)\) either way.

Problem 14mediumreading both ways

Two one-number cells, both with \(w=0.5\), \(u=1\), \(b=0\), read \(x=(1,\ 0,\ 0.5)\): the forward cell left to right, the backward cell right to left. (a) Find the three forward notes. (b) Find the three backward notes. (c) Write word 2's two-way note. (d) If \(x_3\) changes, which half of word 1's note changes?

What this tests. Running the same cell in two directions, and which half carries which side of the sentence. Plan. Forward: start at word 1. Backward: start at word 3 and walk left.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). \(\overrightarrow h_1=\tanh1\approx0.7616\); \(\overrightarrow h_2=\tanh(0.3808)\approx0.3634\); \(\overrightarrow h_3=\tanh(0.1817+0.5)=\tanh(0.6817)\approx0.5926\).
Step 2 — (b). \(\overleftarrow h_3=\tanh0.5\approx0.4621\); \(\overleftarrow h_2=\tanh(0.2311)\approx0.2270\); \(\overleftarrow h_1=\tanh(0.1135+1)=\tanh(1.1135)\approx0.8053\).
Step 3 — (c). Word 2's note is \((\overrightarrow h_2,\overleftarrow h_2)\approx(0.3634,\ 0.2270)\).
Step 4 — (d). \(\overrightarrow h_1\) depends on \(x_1\) only, so it cannot change. \(\overleftarrow h_1\) has read \(x_3\), \(x_2\) and \(x_1\): only the backward half changes.

answers at a glance: (a) 0.7616, 0.3634, 0.5926. (b) 0.4621, 0.2270, 0.8053 (words 3, 2, 1: \(\overleftarrow h=(0.8053,\ 0.2270,\ 0.4621)\) in word order). (c) (0.3634, 0.2270). (d) The backward half.

Remember

Same cell, same weights, opposite direction. The forward half of a note knows the past; the backward half knows the future.

Problem 15mediumgreedy vs beam

A decoder's choices. First word: "the" 0.6, "my" 0.4. After "the": "tea" 0.3, "chai" 0.3, "cup" 0.4. After "my": "chai" 0.8, "tea" 0.2. (a) What does greedy decoding output, with what probability? (b) What does beam search with width 2 output? (c) Give both log-scores.

What this tests. Sentence probability as a product; keeping the \(k\) best. Plan. List every two-word candidate the beam sees.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — (a). Greedy takes "the" (0.6), then its best, "cup" (0.4): \(0.6\times0.4=0.24\).
Step 2 — (b), grow both beams. the tea 0.18, the chai 0.18, the cup 0.24, my chai \(0.4\times0.8=0.32\), my tea 0.08.
Step 3 — (b), keep the best 2. "my chai" 0.32 and "the cup" 0.24. The winner is "my chai".
Step 4 — (c). \(\ln0.24\approx-1.427\); \(\ln0.32\approx-1.139\).

answers at a glance: (a) "the cup", 0.24. (b) "my chai", 0.32. (c) −1.427 and −1.139.

Remember

A first word that looks a little worse can lead to a much surer second word. Greedy never finds out; a beam of width 2 does.

Problem 16hardBLEU-2

Reference: "we drink chai every morning". (a) Candidate A: "each morning we drink chai". Find \(p_1\), \(p_2\), the brevity penalty and BLEU-2. (b) Candidate B: "we drink chai every evening". Find the same. (c) Candidate C: "we drink chai". Find the same. (d) Which candidate keeps the meaning, which scores highest, and what does that say about BLEU?

What this tests. Clipped \(n\)-gram precision, the brevity penalty \(\min(1,e^{1-r/c})\) and the geometric mean. Plan. List the candidate's words and pairs, tick those in the reference, count lengths.

Show the full solution
Step 1 — the reference's pieces. Words: we, drink, chai, every, morning (\(r=5\)). Pairs: "we drink", "drink chai", "chai every", "every morning".
Step 2 — (a). Words: each ✗ morning ✓ we ✓ drink ✓ chai ✓: \(p_1=4/5\). Pairs: "each morning" ✗, "morning we" ✗, "we drink" ✓, "drink chai" ✓: \(p_2=2/4\). \(c=r=5\), so BP \(=1\). BLEU-2 \(=\sqrt{0.8\cdot0.5}=\sqrt{0.4}\approx0.6325\).
Step 3 — (b). Words: we ✓ drink ✓ chai ✓ every ✓ evening ✗: \(p_1=4/5\). Pairs: "we drink" ✓, "drink chai" ✓, "chai every" ✓, "every evening" ✗: \(p_2=3/4\). BP \(=1\). BLEU-2 \(=\sqrt{0.8\cdot0.75}=\sqrt{0.6}\approx0.7746\).
Step 4 — (c). Every word and both pairs match: \(p_1=p_2=1\). But \(c=3<r=5\): BP \(=e^{1-5/3}=e^{-2/3}\approx0.5134\). BLEU-2 \(=0.5134\cdot\sqrt{1\cdot1}\approx0.5134\).
Step 5 — (d). Only A keeps the meaning ("each morning" is "every morning"). B gets a fact wrong (evening), and C drops when they drink it. Yet B scores highest, 0.7746, against A's 0.6325 and C's 0.5134: B shares the most pieces, and one wrong word costs it only one word and one pair. BLEU counts shared pieces, not meaning.

answers at a glance: (a) \(p_1=4/5\), \(p_2=1/2\), BP = 1, BLEU-2 ≈ 0.6325. (b) \(p_1=4/5\), \(p_2=3/4\), BP = 1, BLEU-2 ≈ 0.7746. (c) \(p_1=p_2=1\), BP ≈ 0.5134, BLEU-2 ≈ 0.5134. (d) A keeps the meaning, but B — which gets a fact wrong — scores highest: BLEU rewards overlap, so it is trusted only as an average over many sentences.

Remember

BLEU-2 = BP · √(p₁ p₂). Clip the counts, punish short candidates, and never read one sentence's BLEU as a verdict on its meaning.
